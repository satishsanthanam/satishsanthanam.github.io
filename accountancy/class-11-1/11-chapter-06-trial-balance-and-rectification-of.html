Class 11 (Part 1) — Chapter 6: Trial Balance and Rectification of Errors

6.1 Meaning of Trial Balance

A Trial Balance is a statement showing the balances, or total of debits and credits, of all the accounts in the ledger with a view to verify the arithmetical accuracy of posting into the ledger accounts. It is an important statement in the accounting process as it shows the final position of all accounts and helps in preparing the financial statements.

Key features of a Trial Balance:

  • It is an arithmetical check under the Double Entry System.
  • It is usually prepared at the end of an accounting year, but can be prepared monthly, quarterly, or half-yearly depending on requirements.
  • All assets, expenses, and receivables accounts have debit balances, whereas all liabilities, revenues, and payables accounts have credit balances.

6.2 Objectives of Preparing the Trial Balance

The trial balance is prepared to fulfill the following main objectives:

  1. To ascertain the arithmetical accuracy of ledger accounts: Ensures that for every debit, an equal credit has been recorded.
  2. To help in locating errors: If the trial balance does not tally, it indicates the presence of errors in the books.
  3. To help in the preparation of financial statements: Serves as the basis for preparing the Trading and Profit & Loss Account and the Balance Sheet.
6.3 Preparation of Trial Balance

Theoretically, a trial balance can be prepared in three ways:

1. Totals Method

Under the Totals Method, the total of each side of the ledger account (debit and credit) is ascertained separately and shown in the respective columns of the trial balance. The total of the debit column must equal the total of the credit column.

2. Balances Method

Under the Balances Method, only the net balances (debit or credit) of all ledger accounts are shown in the trial balance. This is the most widely used method in practice because it directly facilitates the preparation of financial statements.

3. Totals-cum-Balances Method

This method is a combination of both the Totals and Balances methods. It contains four columns for amounts: two for debit/credit totals and two for debit/credit balances. It is rarely used in practice due to being time-consuming.

6.4 Significance of Agreement of Trial Balance & Classification of Errors

While the agreement of a trial balance indicates arithmetical accuracy, it is not a conclusive proof of absolute correctness. Certain errors do not affect the agreement of the trial balance.

Classification of Errors

Errors can be classified into four main categories:

  • Errors of Commission: Errors committed due to wrong posting, wrong totalling, wrong balancing, or wrong casting of subsidiary books.
  • Errors of Omission: Occur when a transaction is completely or partially omitted from being recorded in the books.
    • Complete Omission: Transaction is not recorded at all.
    • Partial Omission: Transaction is recorded in subsidiary books but not posted to the ledger.
  • Errors of Principle: Occur when accounting principles are violated, such as treating a capital expenditure as a revenue expense (or vice versa). These do not affect the agreement of the trial balance.
  • Compensating Errors: Occur when the effect of one error is neutralized or compensated by another error. These do not affect the agreement of the trial balance.
6.5 Searching of Errors

When a trial balance does not tally, the accountant should take the following steps to locate errors:

  1. Recast the totals of debit and credit columns of the trial balance.
  2. Compare the account titles and amounts in the trial balance with those in the ledger.
  3. Compare the current year's trial balance with the previous year's to check for omissions or unusual variances.
  4. Re-check the balancing of individual ledger accounts.
  5. Re-check the postings from subsidiary books to the ledger.
  6. If the difference is divisible by 2, check if an item has been posted to the wrong side of an account.
  7. If the difference is divisible by 9, check for transposition of figures (e.g., writing ₹459 as ₹954).
6.6 Rectification of Errors

From the rectification perspective, errors are classified into two categories:

  1. Errors which do not affect the trial balance (Two-sided errors): Rectified by passing a journal entry.
  2. Errors which affect the trial balance (One-sided errors): Rectified by giving an explanatory note in the affected account or by using a Suspense Account.

Rectification of Two-Sided Errors

These errors affect two or more accounts and are rectified by passing a rectifying journal entry that cancels the wrong effect and restores the correct effect.

Suspense Account

A Suspense Account is a temporary account opened to tally the trial balance when one-sided errors exist. The difference in the trial balance is placed in this account until the errors are located and rectified.

  • If the debit side of the trial balance is short, the Suspense Account is debited.
  • If the credit side of the trial balance is short, the Suspense Account is credited.
  • Once all errors are located and rectified, the Suspense Account balances to nil and is closed.
