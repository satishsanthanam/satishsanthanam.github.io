Class 111 — Chapter 3: Recording of Transactions-I

Business Transactions and Source Documents

Accounting involves identifying, recording, classifying, and summarising business transactions to communicate financial information to interested users. The first step in this process is identifying transactions and preparing source documents.

Source Documents

A Source Document is a written document that provides evidence of a business transaction. It contains essential details such as the date, amount, parties involved, and nature of the transaction. Common examples include:

  • Cash Memo: Prepared by the seller for cash sales of goods.
  • Invoice / Bill: Prepared by the seller when goods are sold on credit.
  • Pay-in-slip: Used for depositing cash or cheques into a bank account.
  • Cheque: A written order to a bank to pay a specific sum to the bearer or named person.
  • Salary Slip: Evidence of salary payment to employees.

Accounting Vouchers

An Accounting Voucher is a document prepared by an accountant to analyze and record transactions in the books of accounts. Vouchers are arranged chronologically, serially numbered, and kept as audit evidence.

  • Transaction Voucher: Prepared for simple transactions involving one debit and one credit.
  • Compound Voucher: Prepared for transactions involving multiple debits and one credit, or multiple credits and one debit. These include Debit Vouchers and Credit Vouchers.
  • Complex Voucher / Journal Voucher: Prepared for complex transactions involving multiple debits and multiple credits.
The Accounting Equation

The Accounting Equation signifies that the assets of a business are always equal to the total of its liabilities and capital (owner's equity). It is also known as the Balance Sheet Equation.

$$\text{Assets (A)} = \text{Liabilities (L)} + \text{Capital (C)}$$

Derivatives of the accounting equation include:

  1. $$\text{Capital (C)} = \text{Assets (A)} - \text{Liabilities (L)}$$
  2. $$\text{Liabilities (L)} = \text{Assets (A)} - \text{Capital (C)}$$

Every business transaction has a two-fold effect (dual aspect) that keeps the accounting equation balanced at all times.

Rules of Debit and Credit

In double-entry bookkeeping, every transaction affects at least two accounts. The terms Debit (Dr.) and Credit (Cr.) indicate whether transactions are recorded on the left or right side of an account, which is structured like a T-Account.

Books of Original Entry: The Journal

The Journal is the basic book of original entry where transactions are recorded chronologically as they occur. The process of recording transactions in a journal is called Journalising.

Journal Format

A standard journal contains five columns: Date, Particulars, Ledger Folio (L.F.), Debit Amount, and Credit Amount.

Simple and Compound Journal Entries

  • Simple Journal Entry: Involves only two accounts—one debited and one credited.
  • Compound Journal Entry: Involves more than two accounts, where multiple accounts are debited and/or credited.

Accounting Entries under Goods and Services Tax (GST)

GST is an indirect tax levied on the supply of goods and services. It comprises:

  • CGST (Central GST) and SGST (State GST): Charged on intra-state sales (within the same state).
  • IGST (Integrated GST): Charged on inter-state sales (between different states).

GST paid on purchases is recorded as Input GST (an asset), while GST collected on sales is recorded as Output GST (a liability).

The Ledger and Posting

The Ledger is the principal book of accounting where transactions of a similar nature are classified and recorded in individual accounts.

Ledger Format

A ledger account is divided into two symmetric sides: Debit (left) and Credit (right).

Posting from Journal

The process of transferring journal entries to the ledger accounts is called Posting. It involves locating the account in the ledger, entering the date, writing the corresponding account name in the particulars column, entering the journal folio page, and recording the amount.
