Accountancy: Class 12 (Part 2) — Chapter 3: Financial Statements of a Company

3.1 Meaning of Financial Statements

Financial Statements are the basic and formal annual reports through which corporate management communicates financial information to its owners (shareholders) and various external parties (investors, tax authorities, government, employees, etc.).

These statements are the end products of the accounting process and typically refer to:

  • The Balance Sheet (Position Statement) as at the end of the accounting period.
  • The Statement of Profit and Loss of a company.
  • The Cash Flow Statement.
3.2 Nature of Financial Statements

Financial statements reflect a combination of recorded facts, accounting principles, and personal judgements. The key points explaining their nature are:

1. Recorded Facts

Prepared on the basis of facts in the form of cost data recorded in accounting books. Original or historical cost is the basis of recording transactions. Because they are not based on current market prices, they do not show the current financial condition of the concern.

2. Accounting Conventions

Certain accounting conventions are followed, such as valuing inventory at cost or market price (whichever is lower), and valuing assets at cost less depreciation. The convention of materiality is followed for small items (e.g., stationery is treated as expenditure in the year of purchase).

3. Postulates

Prepared on basic assumptions (pre-requisites) known as postulates, including:

  • Going Concern Postulate: Assumes the enterprise will exist for a long period, so assets are shown on a historical cost basis.
  • Money Measurement Postulate: Assumes the value of money remains constant across periods.
  • Realisation Postulate: Revenue is included in the sales of the year in which the sale was undertaken, regardless of when payment is received.

4. Personal Judgements

Many figures are based on personal opinions, estimates, and judgements, such as estimating the useful life of fixed assets for depreciation, making provisions for doubtful debts, and choosing inventory valuation methods under the convention of conservatism.

3.3 Objectives of Financial Statements

The primary objective of financial statements is to assist users in decision-making. Specific objectives include:

  1. To provide information about economic resources and obligations of a business.
  2. To provide information about the earning capacity of the business.
  3. To provide information about cash flows.
  4. To judge the effectiveness of management in utilising resources.
  5. To report on activities affecting society.
  6. To disclose significant accounting policies and concepts followed.

Important Features of Presentation
  • Applies to all Indian companies preparing financial statements as per Schedule III (except Insurance, Banking, or other specified companies).
  • Accounting Standards prevail over Schedule III.
  • Current and non-current bifurcation of assets and liabilities is mandatory.
  • Rounding-off Rules:
    - Turnover < Rs. 100 crore: Nearest hundreds, thousands, lakhs, or millions.
    - Turnover ≥ Rs. 100 crore: Nearest lakhs, millions, or decimals thereof.

Current vs. Non-Current Distinction

An item is classified as current if it meets any of the following criteria:

  • It is expected to be realised or settled within the entity's normal operating cycle.
  • It is held primarily for trading.
  • It is expected to be realised/settled within twelve months after the reporting period.
  • It is cash or cash equivalent.

All other assets and liabilities are classified as non-current.

3.5 Uses and Importance of Financial Statements

Financial statements provide critical information to various stakeholders for economic decision-making:

  • Report on stewardship function: Reports management's performance to shareholders.
  • Basis for fiscal policies: Provides inputs for taxation and industrial policies of the government.
  • Basis for granting credit: Used by banks and financial institutions to assess creditworthiness.
  • Basis for prospective investors: Helps assess security, liquidity, and profitability of investments.
  • Guide to the value of investment: Helps shareholders decide whether to continue or discontinue investments.
  • Helps stock exchanges: Enhances transparency and protects investor interests.
3.6 Limitations of Financial Statements

Despite their utility, financial statements suffer from several limitations:

  1. Do not reflect current situation: Prepared on historical cost; values do not reflect inflation or current market conditions.
  2. Assets may not realise: Stated values of assets may not be realised if forced liquidation occurs.
  3. Bias: Influenced by personal judgements and choice of accounting conventions.
  4. Aggregate information: Shows consolidated figures rather than detailed transaction-level data.
  5. Vital information missing: Non-monetary factors (e.g., loss of market share, quality of management) are omitted.
  6. No qualitative information: Excludes qualitative aspects like industrial relations and employee satisfaction.
  7. Interim reports: They represent performance over a specific period and position at a single point in time, failing to depict future changes.
