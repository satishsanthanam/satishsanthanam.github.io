Accountancy: Class 12 (Part 2) — Chapter 6: Cash Flow Statement

Introduction & Conceptual Framework

A Cash Flow Statement is a critical financial statement that tracks the inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents of an enterprise over a specific accounting period. Prepared in accordance with Accounting Standard-3 (AS-3), it serves as an essential tool for stakeholders to evaluate an entity's liquidity, solvency, and financial adaptability.

Cash and Cash Equivalents

Under AS-3, the components are defined as follows:

  • Cash: Comprises cash in hand and demand deposits with banks.
  • Cash Equivalents: Short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and carry an insignificant risk of changes in value. These typically have a maturity period of three months or less from the date of acquisition (e.g., short-term marketable securities, treasury bills).

Objectives and Benefits

The primary objective of a cash flow statement is to provide useful historical information about cash movements classified under distinct heads.

  • Liquidity & Solvency Analysis: Helps users assess the enterprise's ability to generate cash and meet its immediate obligations.
  • Comparability: Eliminates the effects of different accounting treatments for similar transactions, enhancing comparability across enterprises.
  • Valuation Models: Enables financial analysts to develop models to estimate and compare the present value of future cash flows.
Classification of Cash Flow Activities

To provide structured insights, AS-3 mandates the classification of cash flows into three distinct categories:

1. Operating Activities

Operating Activities are the principal revenue-producing activities of the enterprise that are not investing or financing activities. They reflect the cash impact of transactions that determine net profit or loss.

  • Cash Inflows: Receipts from the sale of goods, rendering of services, royalties, fees, and commissions.
  • Cash Outflows: Payments to suppliers for raw materials, payments to employees, and payments of income taxes (unless specifically linked to financing/investing activities).

2. Investing Activities

Investing Activities involve the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents. They indicate the extent to which expenditures have been made for resources intended to generate future income.

  • Cash Inflows: Sale of fixed assets (tangible and intangible), repayment of loans/advances made to third parties, interest and dividends received on investments.
  • Cash Outflows: Purchase of fixed assets (including capitalized R&D), purchase of shares or debt instruments of other enterprises, loans and advances made to third parties.

3. Financing Activities

Financing Activities are activities that result in changes in the size and composition of the owner's capital and borrowings of the enterprise.

  • Cash Inflows: Proceeds from issuing shares (equity or preference), debentures, bonds, and raising long-term or short-term loans.
  • Cash Outflows: Repayment of borrowed amounts, redemption of preference shares, payment of dividends, and interest paid on loans/debentures.

Taxes, Extraordinary Items & Non-Cash Transactions

  • Taxes on Income: Generally classified as operating cash flows unless they can be specifically identified with financing or investing activities (e.g., capital gains tax on sale of land is classified under investing).
  • Extraordinary Items: Cash flows associated with non-recurring events (e.g., earthquake disaster settlements, insurance claims, theft losses) must be disclosed separately under the appropriate activity head to help users understand their impact.
  • Non-Cash Transactions: Investing and financing transactions that do not require the use of cash (e.g., acquisition of machinery by issuing shares, conversion of debentures into shares) must be excluded from the cash flow statement and disclosed elsewhere in the financial statements.
Methods of Preparing Cash Flow Statements

While operating cash flows can be calculated using either the Direct or Indirect method, the Indirect Method is most commonly used in corporate practice.

The Indirect Method

The indirect method adjusts the net profit or loss for the effects of non-cash items, deferrals/accruals of past or future operating cash receipts/payments, and items of income or expense associated with investing or financing cash flows.

Adjustment Formula for Working Capital Changes:

$$ ext{Operating Profit before Working Capital Changes} + ext{Decrease in Current Assets} + ext{Increase in Current Liabilities} - ext{Increase in Current Assets} - ext{Decrease in Current Liabilities} = ext{Cash Generated from Operations}$$
