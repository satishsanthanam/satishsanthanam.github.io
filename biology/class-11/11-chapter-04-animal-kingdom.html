Class 11 — Chapter 4: Animal Kingdom

Basis of Classification

Inspite of differences in structure and form of different animals, there are fundamental features common to various individuals in relation to the arrangement of cells, body symmetry, nature of coelom, and patterns of organ systems.

Levels of Organisation

  • Cellular Level: Cells are arranged as loose cell aggregates. Division of labour occurs among cells. Example: Sponges (Porifera).
  • Tissue Level: Cells performing the same function are arranged into tissues. Example: Coelenterates.
  • Organ Level: Tissues are grouped together to form organs, each specialised for a particular function. Example: Platyhelminthes.
  • Organ System Level: Organs associate to form functional systems, each concerned with a specific physiological function. Examples: Annelids, Arthropods, Molluscs, Echinoderms, and Chordates.
  • Digestive System Patterns:
    • Incomplete: Has only a single opening to the outside of the body that serves as both mouth and anus (e.g., Platyhelminthes).
    • Complete: Has two distinct openings, a mouth and an anus.
  • Circulatory System Patterns:
    • Open type: Blood is pumped out of the heart and cells/tissues are directly bathed in it.
    • Closed type: Blood is circulated through a series of vessels of varying diameters (arteries, veins, and capillaries).

Symmetry

  • Asymmetrical: Any plane that passes through the centre does not divide the body into equal halves. Example: Most sponges.
  • Radial Symmetry: Any plane passing through the central axis of the body divides the organism into two identical halves. Examples: Coelenterates, Ctenophores, and Echinoderms.
  • Bilateral Symmetry: The body can be divided into identical left and right halves in only one plane. Examples: Annelids, Arthropods, etc.

Diploblastic and Triploblastic Organisation

  • Diploblastic: Cells are arranged in two embryonic layers: an external ectoderm and an internal endoderm, with an undifferentiated layer called mesoglea in between. Example: Coelenterates.
  • Triploblastic: Developing embryo has a third germinal layer, the mesoderm, in between the ectoderm and endoderm. Examples: Platyhelminthes to Chordates.

Coelom

The presence or absence of a cavity between the body wall and the gut wall is key to classification.

  • Coelomates: Possess a true body cavity (coelom) lined by mesoderm. Examples: Annelids, Molluscs, Arthropods, Echinoderms, Hemichordates, and Chordates.
  • Pseudocoelomates: The body cavity is not lined by mesoderm; instead, mesoderm is present as scattered pouches. Example: Aschelminthes.
  • Acoelomates: The body cavity is completely absent. Example: Platyhelminthes.

Segmentation & Notochord

  • Metamerism: External and internal division of the body into segments with serial repetition of at least some organs (e.g., Earthworm).
  • Notochord: A mesodermally derived rod-like structure formed on the dorsal side during embryonic development in some animals. Animals with a notochord are chordates; those without are non-chordates (Porifera to Echinoderms).
Classification of Animals (Non-Chordates)

Phylum - Porifera

  • Commonly known as sponges; mostly marine and asymmetrical.
  • Exhibit cellular level of organisation.
  • Water Transport/Canal System: Water enters through minute pores (ostia) into a central cavity (spongocoel) and exits through the osculum. Helps in food gathering, respiratory exchange, and waste removal.
  • Lined by collar cells (choanocytes).
  • Skeleton made of spicules or spongin fibres.
  • Hermaphrodite; reproduce asexually by fragmentation and sexually by gametes. Fertilisation is internal with indirect development.
  • Examples: Sycon (Scypha), Spongilla (Fresh water sponge), and Euspongia (Bath sponge).

Phylum - Coelenterata (Cnidaria)

  • Aquatic, mostly marine, sessile or free-swimming, radially symmetrical.
  • Possess stinging capsules or cnidoblasts (cnidocytes) on tentacles for anchorage, defense, and prey capture.
  • Tissue level of organisation; diploblastic.
  • Central gastro-vascular cavity with a single opening (mouth on hypostome).
  • Two basic body forms:
    • Polyp: Sessile and cylindrical (e.g., Hydra, Adamsia).
    • Medusa: Umbrella-shaped and free-swimming (e.g., Aurelia / jelly fish).
  • Metagenesis (Alternation of generation): Polyps produce medusae asexually, and medusae form polyps sexually (e.g., Obelia).
  • Examples: Physalia (Portuguese man-of-war), Adamsia (Sea anemone), Pennatula (Sea-pen), Gorgonia (Sea-fan), and Meandrina (Brain coral).

Phylum - Ctenophora

  • Commonly known as sea walnuts or comb jellies; exclusively marine, radially symmetrical, diploblastic.
  • Body bears eight external rows of ciliated comb plates for locomotion.
  • Exhibit prominent bioluminescence (emission of light).
  • Sexes are not separate; reproduction is exclusively sexual. Fertilisation is external with indirect development.
  • Examples: Pleurobrachia and Ctenoplana.

Phylum - Platyhelminthes

  • Dorso-ventrally flattened body, commonly called flatworms.
  • Mostly endoparasites; bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, acoelomate with organ level of organisation.
  • Possess hooks and suckers in parasitic forms.
  • Specialised flame cells help in osmoregulation and excretion.
  • Sexes are not separate; fertilisation is internal with many larval stages. High regeneration capacity in Planaria.
  • Examples: Taenia (Tapeworm), Fasciola (Liver fluke).

Phylum - Aschelminthes

  • Circular in cross-section, commonly called roundworms.
  • Organ-system level, bilaterally symmetrical, triploblastic, and pseudocoelomate.
  • Alimentary canal is complete with a well-developed muscular pharynx.
  • Sexes are separate (dioecious); females are often longer than males.
  • Examples: Ascaris (Roundworm), Wuchereria (Filaria worm), Ancylostoma (Hookworm).

Phylum - Annelida

  • Aquatic or terrestrial; free-living or parasitic.
  • Organ-system level, bilateral symmetry, triploblastic, metamerically segmented, and coelomate.
  • Body surface marked into segments or metameres.
  • Possess longitudinal and circular muscles for locomotion. Aquatic forms like Nereis have lateral appendages called parapodia for swimming.
  • Excretory organs are nephridia.
  • Examples: Nereis (dioecious), Pheretima (Earthworm - monoecious), and Hirudinaria (Blood sucking leech - monoecious).

Phylum - Arthropoda

  • The largest phylum of Animalia, including insects (over two-thirds of all named species).
  • Organ-system level, bilateral symmetry, triploblastic, segmented, and coelomate.
  • Body covered by a chitinous exoskeleton; divided into head, thorax, and abdomen.
  • Possess jointed appendages.
  • Respiratory organs: gills, book gills, book lungs, or tracheal system.
  • Circulatory system is of open type. Excretion via malpighian tubules.
  • Mostly dioecious, internal fertilisation, mostly oviparous.
  • Examples:
    • Economically important: Apis (Honey bee), Bombyx (Silkworm), Laccifer (Lac insect).
    • Vectors: Anopheles, Culex, and Aedes mosquitoes.
    • Living fossil: Limulus (King crab).

Phylum - Mollusca

  • The second largest animal phylum; terrestrial or aquatic.
  • Organ-system level, bilateral symmetry, triploblastic, and coelomate.
  • Body covered by a calcareous shell; unsegmented with a distinct head, muscular foot, and visceral hump.
  • A soft, spongy layer of skin forms a mantle over the visceral hump. The space between them is the mantle cavity, containing feather-like gills.
  • Mouth contains a file-like rasping organ for feeding called radula.
  • Examples: Pila (Apple snail), Pinctada (Pearl oyster), Sepia (Cuttlefish), Octopus (Devil fish).

Phylum - Echinodermata

  • Spiny-bodied animals with an endoskeleton of calcareous ossicles; exclusively marine.
  • Adults are radially symmetrical, but larvae are bilaterally symmetrical. Triploblastic and coelomate.
  • Most distinctive feature: water vascular system, which helps in locomotion, capture/transport of food, and respiration.
  • Excretory system is absent. Sexes are separate; fertilisation is external.
  • Examples: Asterias (Star fish), Echinus (Sea urchin), Antedon (Sea lily), Ophiura (Brittle star).

Phylum - Hemichordata

  • Worm-like marine animals; earlier treated as a sub-phylum of Chordata.
  • Have a rudimentary structure in the collar region called stomochord.
  • Cylindrical body composed of an anterior proboscis, a collar, and a long trunk.
  • Open circulatory system; respiration through gills; excretion via proboscis gland.
  • Examples: Balanoglossus and Saccoglossus.
Phylum Chordata & Vertebrata

Characterised fundamentally by the presence of a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, and paired pharyngeal gill slits.

Chordata Subphyla

  • Urochordata (Tunicata): Notochord present only in larval tail. Examples: Ascidia, Salpa, Doliolum.
  • Cephalochordata: Notochord extends from head to tail and is persistent throughout life. Example: Branchiostoma (Amphioxus or Lancelet).
  • Vertebrata: Possess notochord during embryonic period; replaced by a cartilaginous or bony vertebral column in adults. "All vertebrates are chordates but all chordates are not vertebrates."

Class - Cyclostomata

  • Ectoparasites on some fishes; jawless (Agnatha).
  • Elongated body with 6-15 pairs of gill slits. Sucking and circular mouth without jaws.
  • Devoid of scales and paired fins. Cranium and vertebral column are cartilaginous.
  • Marine but migrate to fresh water for spawning, after which they die. Larvae return to the ocean after metamorphosis.
  • Examples: Petromyzon (Lamprey) and Myxine (Hagfish).

Class - Chondrichthyes

  • Marine animals with streamlined bodies and a cartilaginous endoskeleton.
  • Notochord is persistent throughout life. Gill slits are separate and lack an operculum (gill cover).
  • Skin contains tough, minute placoid scales. Teeth are modified placoid scales.
  • Air bladder is absent; must swim constantly to avoid sinking.
  • Cold-blooded (poikilothermous). Heart is two-chambered.
  • Some possess electric organs (e.g., Torpedo) or poison stings (e.g., Trygon).
  • Examples: Scoliodon (Dog fish), Pristis (Saw fish), Carcharodon (Great white shark).

Class - Osteichthyes

  • Marine and fresh water fishes with a bony endoskeleton.
  • Four pairs of gills covered by an operculum on each side.
  • Skin covered with cycloid/ctenoid scales.
  • Air bladder is present, which regulates buoyancy.
  • Heart is two-chambered; cold-blooded. Mostly oviparous with direct development.
  • Examples: Exocoetus (Flying fish), Hippocampus (Sea horse), Labeo (Rohu), Catla (Katla).

Class - Amphibia

  • Can live in both aquatic and terrestrial habitats. Most have two pairs of limbs.
  • Body divisible into head and trunk; skin is moist and lacks scales.
  • A tympanum represents the ear.
  • Alimentary, urinary, and reproductive tracts open into a common chamber called cloaca.
  • Respiration by gills, lungs, and skin. Heart is three-chambered (two auricles, one ventricle).
  • Examples: Bufo (Toad), Rana (Frog), Hyla (Tree frog), Salamandra (Salamander).

Class - Reptilia

  • Creeping or crawling mode of locomotion. Mostly terrestrial.
  • Body covered by dry and cornified skin, epidermal scales, or scutes.
  • Tympanum represents ear. Limbs, when present, are two pairs.
  • Heart is usually three-chambered, but four-chambered in crocodiles.
  • Poikilotherms. Snakes and lizards shed scales as skin cast.
  • Examples: Chelone (Turtle), Testudo (Tortoise), Chameleon (Tree lizard), Crocodilus (Crocodile), Naja (Cobra).

Class - Aves

  • Presence of feathers; most can fly (except flightless birds like Ostrich).
  • Possess a beak. Forelimbs are modified into wings. Hind limbs adapted for walking, swimming, or perching.
  • Long bones are hollow with air cavities (pneumatic bones).
  • Digestive tract has additional chambers: the crop and gizzard.
  • Heart is completely four-chambered. Warm-blooded (homoiothermous) animals.
  • Respiration by lungs; air sacs supplement respiration.
  • Examples: Corvus (Crow), Columba (Pigeon), Psittacula (Parrot), Struthio (Ostrich).

Class - Mammalia

  • Found in diverse habitats. Unique feature: presence of milk-producing mammary glands.
  • Skin possesses hair. External ears or pinnae are present.
  • Different types of teeth are present in the jaw (thecodont, heterodont).
  • Heart is four-chambered; homoiothermous. Respiration by lungs.
  • Mostly viviparous with direct development.
  • Examples:
    • Oviparous: Ornithorhynchus (Platypus).
    • Viviparous: Macropus (Kangaroo), Pteropus (Flying fox), Camelus (Camel), Panthera tigris (Tiger).
