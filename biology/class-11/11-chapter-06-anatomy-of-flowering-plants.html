Biology: Class 11 — Chapter 6: Anatomy of Flowering Plants

6.1 The Tissue System

Tissues vary depending on their location in the plant body. Based on structure and location, there are three types of tissue systems:

  • Epidermal tissue system
  • Ground or fundamental tissue system
  • Vascular or conducting tissue system

6.1.1 Epidermal Tissue System

Forms the outer-most covering of the whole plant body. It comprises:

  • Epidermis: Outermost layer of the primary plant body, made of elongated, compactly arranged parenchymatous cells. Usually single-layered and covered with a waxy thick layer called cuticle (absent in roots) to prevent water loss.
  • Stomata: Structures present in the epidermis of leaves that regulate transpiration and gaseous exchange. Each stoma is composed of two bean-shaped guard cells (dumb-bell shaped in grasses) enclosing a stomatal pore. Guard cells contain chloroplasts and regulate opening/closing. Specialized neighboring cells are called subsidiary cells. The stomatal aperture, guard cells, and subsidiary cells together form the stomatal apparatus.
  • Epidermal appendages: Includes unicellular root hairs (absorb water/minerals) and multicellular trichomes on stems (prevent water loss, can be secretory).

6.1.2 The Ground Tissue System

All tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles form the ground tissue. It consists of simple tissues:

  • Parenchyma, Collenchyma, and Sclerenchyma: Present in cortex, pericycle, pith, and medullary rays of primary stems and roots.
  • Mesophyll: Ground tissue in leaves, consisting of thin-walled, chloroplast-containing cells.

6.1.3 The Vascular Tissue System

Consists of complex tissues, xylem and phloem, which together form vascular bundles.

  • Open vascular bundles: Present in dicotyledonous stems; possess cambium between xylem and phloem, enabling secondary growth.
  • Closed vascular bundles: Present in monocotyledons; lack cambium and do not form secondary tissues.
  • Radial arrangement: Xylem and phloem arranged alternately along different radii (e.g., in roots).
  • Conjoint arrangement: Xylem and phloem jointly situated along the same radius (common in stems and leaves). Phloem is usually located on the outer side of xylem.
6.2 Anatomy of Dicotyledonous and Monocotyledonous Plants

6.2.1 Dicotyledonous Root

Internal tissue organization (e.g., sunflower root):

  • Epiblema: Outermost layer, many cells protrude as unicellular root hairs.
  • Cortex: Several layers of thin-walled parenchyma cells with intercellular spaces.
  • Endodermis: Innermost layer of cortex, single layer of barrel-shaped cells. Tangential and radial walls have water-impermeable waxy suberin deposition called casparian strips.
  • Pericycle: Thick-walled parenchymatous cells next to endodermis; initiates lateral roots and vascular cambium.
  • Pith: Small or inconspicuous.
  • Conjunctive tissue: Parenchymatous cells between xylem and phloem.
  • Stele: All tissues on the inner side of endodermis (pericycle, vascular bundles, pith).

6.2.2 Monocotyledonous Root

Similar to dicot root anatomy but with key differences:

  • Has more than six (polyarch) xylem bundles (dicot roots have 2 to 4).
  • Pith is large and well-developed.
  • Do not undergo any secondary growth.

6.2.3 Dicotyledonous Stem

Transverse section of a young dicot stem shows:

  • Epidermis: Outermost protective layer, covered with cuticle, may bear trichomes and stomata.
  • Cortex: Divided into three sub-zones: hypodermis (collenchymatous, provides mechanical strength), cortical layers (parenchymatous), and endodermis (rich in starch grains, called starch sheath).
  • Pericycle: Semi-lunar patches of sclerenchyma on the inner side of endodermis.
  • Medullary rays: Radially placed parenchymatous cells between vascular bundles.
  • Vascular Bundles: Arranged in a ring (characteristic of dicot stems). Each bundle is conjoint, open, and has endarch protoxylem.
  • Pith: Central portion of stem, made of rounded parenchymatous cells with large intercellular spaces.

6.2.4 Monocotyledonous Stem

Characterized by:

  • Sclerenchymatous hypodermis.
  • Numerous scattered vascular bundles, each surrounded by a sclerenchymatous bundle sheath.
  • Conjoint and closed vascular bundles; peripheral bundles are smaller than central ones.
  • Phloem parenchyma is absent; water-containing cavities are present within vascular bundles.

6.2.5 Dorsiventral (Dicotyledonous) Leaf

Vertical section shows three main parts:

  • Epidermis: Covers both upper (adaxial epidermis) and lower (abaxial epidermis) surfaces. Abaxial epidermis generally bears more stomata.
  • Mesophyll: Photosynthetic tissue between epidermises, containing chloroplasts. Differentiated into:
    • Palisade parenchyma: Adaxially placed, elongated cells arranged vertically.
    • Spongy parenchyma: Loosely arranged, oval/round cells with large air cavities below palisade cells.
  • Vascular system: Vascular bundles seen in veins and midrib, surrounded by thick-walled bundle sheath cells.

6.2.6 Isobilateral (Monocotyledonous) Leaf

Anatomy is similar to dorsiventral leaf but with distinct differences:

  • Stomata are present on both surfaces of the epidermis.
  • Mesophyll is not differentiated into palisade and spongy parenchyma.
  • In grasses, certain adaxial epidermal cells modify into large, empty, colourless bulliform cells. When turgid, they expose the leaf surface; when flaccid due to water stress, they make leaves curl inwards to minimize water loss.
  • Parallel venation is reflected in near-similar sizes of vascular bundles.
