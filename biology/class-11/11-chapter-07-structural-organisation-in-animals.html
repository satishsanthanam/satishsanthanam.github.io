Biology: Class 11 — Chapter 7: Structural Organisation in Animals

Introduction to Structural Organisation

In multicellular organisms, cells organize into groups to perform specific functions efficiently, exhibiting a division of labour.

  • Tissue: A group of similar cells along with intercellular substances performing a specific function.
  • Organ: Formed by different tissues organized in a specific proportion and pattern (e.g., stomach, heart, lung, kidney).
  • Organ System: Formed when two or more organs coordinate to perform a common physical or chemical function (e.g., digestive system).
  • Morphology: The study of externally visible features or form of organs.
  • Anatomy: The study of the internal structure and morphology of internal organs.
Frog: Classification and Morphology

Taxonomy and General Characteristics

  • Scientific Name: Rana tigrina (Common Indian bullfrog).
  • Classification: Class Amphibia, Phylum Chordata. Can live on both land and freshwater.
  • Thermoregulation: Poikilothermic (cold-blooded); body temperature fluctuates with environmental temperature.
  • Adaptations:
    • Camouflage (mimicry): Ability to change skin colour to blend with surroundings and hide from enemies.
    • Aestivation: Summer sleep to survive extreme heat.
    • Hibernation: Winter sleep to survive extreme cold.

External Morphology

  • Skin: Smooth, slippery (due to mucus secretion), and kept moist. Dorsal side is olive green with dark irregular spots; ventral side is pale yellow. Water is absorbed through the skin (frogs never drink water).
  • Body Divisions: Divided into head and trunk. Neck and tail are completely absent.
  • Sensory Structures:
    • A pair of nostrils above the mouth.
    • Bulged eyes covered by a transparent nictitating membrane for protection underwater.
    • A membranous tympanum (ear) on either side of the eyes to receive sound signals.
  • Limbs:
    • Forelimbs: End in 4 digits; help in swimming, walking, leaping, and burrowing.
    • Hind limbs: Larger, muscular, and end in 5 webbed digits that facilitate swimming.
  • Sexual Dimorphism: Male frogs are distinguished by the presence of sound-producing vocal sacs and a copulatory pad on the first digit of the forelimbs (both absent in females).
Frog: Anatomy and Physiological Systems

Digestive System

Consists of a short alimentary canal (due to carnivorous diet) and digestive glands.

  1. Ingestion: Food is captured using a bilobed tongue.
  2. Pathway: Mouth → Buccal cavity → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Intestine → Rectum → Cloaca.
  3. Digestion in Stomach: Gastric juices and hydrochloric acid ($HCl$) break down food into semi-digested chyme.
  4. Duodenum & Glands: Receives bile (secreted by liver, stored in gall bladder) to emulsify fats, and pancreatic juice (from pancreas) to digest carbohydrates and proteins.
  5. Absorption: Occurs in the intestine via finger-like folds called villi and microvilli.
  6. Egestion: Undigested solid waste passes through the rectum and out via the cloaca.

Respiratory System

  • Cutaneous Respiration (In Water): Skin acts as the respiratory organ. Dissolved oxygen is exchanged via simple diffusion.
  • Pulmonary Respiration (On Land): Performed by a pair of elongated, pink, sac-like lungs in the thorax. Air enters through nostrils into the buccal cavity and then to the lungs.
  • Aestivation & Hibernation: Gaseous exchange occurs exclusively through the skin.

Circulatory and Lymphatic Systems

Well-developed closed circulatory system and a lymphatic system.

  • Heart Structure: Three-chambered (two atria and one ventricle), covered by the pericardium.
    • Sinus venosus: A triangular structure joining the right atrium; receives deoxygenated blood via the vena cava.
    • Conus arteriosus: A sac-like structure on the ventral side of the heart where the ventricle opens.
  • Portal Systems:
    • Hepatic portal system: Venous connection between liver and intestine.
    • Renal portal system: Venous connection between kidneys and lower parts of the body.
  • Blood Composition: Plasma and blood cells.
    • RBCs (Erythrocytes): Nucleated and contain red-coloured haemoglobin.
    • WBCs (Leucocytes) and Platelets.
  • Lymph: Differs from blood; lacks RBCs and certain proteins.

Excretory System

  • Components: A pair of kidneys, ureters, cloaca, and urinary bladder.
  • Kidneys: Compact, dark red, bean-like structures containing structural units called uriniferous tubules or nephrons.
  • Ureters:
    • In males: Act as a urinogenital duct opening directly into the cloaca.
    • In females: Open separately into the cloaca alongside the oviducts.
  • Excretion Type: Ureotelic (excretes urea, which is filtered from the blood by the kidneys).

Control and Coordination

  • Endocrine System: Chemical coordination via hormones from glands: pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, pineal body, pancreatic islets, adrenals, and gonads.
  • Nervous System:
    • Central Nervous System (CNS): Brain (enclosed in a bony cranium) and spinal cord.
    • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS): 10 pairs of cranial nerves and spinal nerves.
    • Autonomic Nervous System (ANS): Sympathetic and parasympathetic systems.
  • Brain Divisions:
    • Fore-brain: Olfactory lobes, paired cerebral hemispheres, and unpaired diencephalon.
    • Mid-brain: Characterised by a pair of optic lobes.
    • Hind-brain: Cerebellum and medulla oblongata (passes through the foramen magnum to continue as the spinal cord).
  • Sense Organs: Sensory papillae (touch), taste buds (taste), nasal epithelium (smell), simple eyes (vision), and tympanum with internal ears (hearing and balancing/equilibrium).

Reproductive System

  • Male Reproductive System:
    • A pair of yellowish ovoid testes adhered to the upper kidneys by a double fold of peritoneum called mesorchium.
    • 10-12 vasa efferentia arise from testes, enter kidneys, and open into Bidder's canal.
    • Communicates with the urinogenital duct to open into the cloaca.
  • Female Reproductive System:
    • A pair of ovaries situated near the kidneys (no functional connection with kidneys).
    • A pair of oviducts opening separately into the cloaca.
    • A mature female lays 2500 to 3000 ova at a time.
  • Fertilisation & Development:
    • Fertilisation is external and occurs in water.
    • Development is indirect, involving a larval stage called a tadpole, which undergoes metamorphosis to become an adult.
Economic Importance of Frogs
  • Pest Control: Beneficial to agriculture as they eat insects and protect crops.
  • Ecological Balance: Serve as an important link in food chains and food webs within ecosystems.
  • Food Source: In some countries, the muscular legs of frogs are used as food by humans.
