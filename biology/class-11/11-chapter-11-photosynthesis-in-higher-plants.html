Biology: Class 11 — Chapter 11: Photosynthesis in Higher Plants

Introduction & Historical Milestones

Overview of Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is a vital physico-chemical process by which green plants use light energy to drive the synthesis of organic compounds. It is the primary source of all food on Earth and is responsible for releasing oxygen into the atmosphere.

Key Historical Experiments
  • Joseph Priestley (1770): Revealed the essential role of air in plant growth. Using a bell jar, candle, and mouse, he discovered that plants restore oxygen to the air. He discovered oxygen in 1774.
  • Jan Ingenhousz (1730-1799): Showed that sunlight is essential for this purification process. He observed oxygen bubbles forming around the green parts of aquatic plants only in the presence of light.
  • Julius von Sachs (1854): Provided evidence that growing plants produce glucose, which is stored as starch, and that chlorophyll is located in special cellular bodies (chloroplasts).
  • T.W. Engelmann (1843-1909): Used a prism to split light and illuminated green algae (Cladophora) in an aerobic bacterial suspension. He plotted the first action spectrum of photosynthesis, showing maximum activity in blue and red light.
  • Cornelius van Niel (1897-1985): Demonstrated that photosynthesis is a light-dependent reaction where hydrogen from an oxidisable compound reduces carbon dioxide. He proved that oxygen evolved by green plants comes from water ($H_2O$), not carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), using purple and green sulphur bacteria ($H_2S$ as donor).

The overall balanced chemical equation is:

$$6CO_2 + 12H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{Light}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6H_2O + 6O_2$$

Site of Photosynthesis & Pigments

Chloroplast Structure & Division of Labor

Photosynthesis occurs in the green leaves and other green parts of plants, specifically within the mesophyll cells which contain a large number of chloroplasts. Within the chloroplast, there is a clear division of labor:

  • Membranous System: Consists of grana, stroma lamellae, and fluid stroma. It is responsible for trapping light energy and synthesizing ATP and NADPH (light reactions).
  • Stroma Matrix: Site of enzymatic reactions that synthesize sugar, which in turn forms starch (dark reactions / biosynthetic phase).

Photosynthetic Pigments

Chromatographic separation reveals that leaf color is due to four distinct pigments:

  • Chlorophyll a (bright or blue-green): The chief pigment associated with photosynthesis.
  • Chlorophyll b (yellow-green).
  • Xanthophylls (yellow).
  • Carotenoids (yellow to yellow-orange).

Accessory pigments (Chl b, xanthophylls, carotenoids) absorb light at different wavelengths, transfer energy to Chl a, and protect it from photo-oxidation.

The Light Reaction (Photochemical Phase)

Light Harvesting Complexes (LHC)

The light reactions include light absorption, water splitting, oxygen release, and the formation of high-energy chemical intermediates (ATP and NADPH). Pigments are organized into two discrete photochemical light harvesting complexes (LHC) within Photosystem I (PS I) (reaction center $P_{700}$) and Photosystem II (PS II) (reaction center $P_{680}$).

Electron Transport & The Z-Scheme

When PS II absorbs red light at 680 nm, electrons become excited and jump into an orbit farther from the atomic nucleus. These electrons are picked up by an electron acceptor and passed through an electron transport system consisting of cytochromes to PS I.

Simultaneously, electrons in the reaction center of PS I are excited by red light of 700 nm and transferred to another acceptor with greater redox potential. These electrons are then moved downhill to reduce $NADP^+$ to $NADPH + H^+$. This continuous pathway is called the Z-scheme due to its characteristic shape on a redox potential scale.

Splitting of Water

To supply electrons continuously to PS II, water is split on the inner side of the thylakoid membrane:

$$2H_2O \rightarrow 4H^+ + O_2 + 4e^-$$

This process releases oxygen as a byproduct and accumulates protons ($H^+$) inside the thylakoid lumen.

Cyclic and Non-cyclic Photophosphorylation
  • Non-cyclic Photophosphorylation: Occurs when PS II and PS I work in series. It results in the synthesis of both ATP and NADPH.
  • Cyclic Photophosphorylation: Occurs when only PS I is functional (typically in stroma lamellae membranes, which lack PS II and NADP reductase). The excited electron is cycled back to the PS I complex through the electron transport chain, resulting only in the synthesis of ATP (no NADPH is formed). This occurs when only light of wavelengths beyond 680 nm is available.

Chemiosmotic Hypothesis

ATP synthesis is linked to the development of a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane. Protons accumulate in the lumen due to water splitting, proton translocation via plastoquinone, and the consumption of stroma protons during $NADP^+$ reduction.

This gradient is broken down as protons diffuse back to the stroma through the transmembrane channel of ATP synthase ($CF_0$ and $CF_1$). This movement releases enough energy to catalyze the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate.

The Biosynthetic Phase (Dark Reaction)

The Calvin Cycle ($C_3$ Pathway)

The products of the light reaction (ATP and NADPH) are used to drive the synthesis of sugars from $CO_2$ and $H_2O$. The primary $CO_2$ acceptor is a 5-carbon ketose sugar, ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), and the first stable product is a 3-carbon acid, 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA). The cycle proceeds in three stages:

  1. Carboxylation: Fixation of $CO_2$ into RuBP, catalyzed by the enzyme RuBisCO to form two molecules of 3-PGA.
  2. Reduction: Utilization of 2 ATP and 2 NADPH per $CO_2$ fixed to form triose phosphates, which eventually yield glucose.
  3. Regeneration: Regeneration of the $CO_2$ acceptor RuBP, requiring 1 ATP.

To synthesize one molecule of glucose, 6 turns of the cycle are required:

  • In: $6 CO_2 + 18 ATP + 12 NADPH$
  • Out: $1 \text{ Glucose} + 18 ADP + 12 NADP^+$

The $C_4$ Pathway (Hatch and Slack Pathway)

Plants adapted to dry tropical regions use the $C_4$ pathway. They exhibit Kranz anatomy, characterized by large bundle sheath cells around vascular bundles that contain many chloroplasts and lack intercellular spaces.

  • The primary $CO_2$ acceptor is phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) (3-carbon) in mesophyll cells, catalyzed by PEP carboxylase (PEPcase).
  • The first stable product is oxaloacetic acid (OAA) (4-carbon).
  • OAA is converted to malic or aspartic acid and transported to bundle sheath cells, where it is decarboxylated to release $CO_2$ (which enters the Calvin cycle) and a 3-carbon molecule that returns to the mesophyll to regenerate PEP.

Photorespiration

A wasteful process in $C_3$ plants where RuBisCO binds with $O_2$ instead of $CO_2$ under high oxygen concentrations. RuBP combines with $O_2$ to form one molecule of PGA and one molecule of phosphoglycolate ($C_2$ compound). This pathway does not synthesize sugar, ATP, or NADPH, but instead consumes ATP and releases $CO_2$.

$C_4$ plants lack photorespiration because they have a mechanism that increases the concentration of $CO_2$ at the RuBisCO enzyme site, ensuring high productivity and tolerance to high temperatures.

Factors Affecting Photosynthesis

Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors

Formulated in 1905, this law states: "If a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, then its rate will be determined by the factor which is nearest to its minimal value."

Key Limiting Factors
  • Light: There is a linear relationship between incident light and $CO_2$ fixation at low intensities. Light saturation occurs at 10% of full sunlight. Very high light intensities can cause chlorophyll breakdown.
  • Carbon Dioxide Concentration: The major limiting factor. Atmospheric levels are 0.03-0.04%. Increasing concentration up to 0.05% increases fixation rates. $C_4$ plants show saturation at 360 $\mu L L^{-1}$, while $C_3$ plants respond up to 450 $\mu L L^{-1}$.
  • Temperature: Dark reactions are enzymatic and highly temperature-controlled. $C_4$ plants have a higher temperature optimum than $C_3$ plants.
  • Water: Indirectly affects photosynthesis. Water stress causes stomatal closure (reducing $CO_2$ availability) and leaf wilting (reducing surface area).
