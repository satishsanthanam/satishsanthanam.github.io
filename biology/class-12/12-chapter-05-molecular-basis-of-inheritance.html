Biology: Class 12 — Chapter 5: Molecular Basis of Inheritance

Structure and Packaging of DNA

Structure of a Polynucleotide Chain

A nucleotide is the basic monomeric unit of nucleic acids like DNA and RNA. It consists of three main components:

  • Nitrogenous Base: Divided into Purines (Adenine and Guanine) and Pyrimidines (Cytosine, Thymine, and Uracil). Thymine is unique to DNA, while Uracil is unique to RNA.
  • Pentose Sugar: Ribose in RNA and Deoxyribose in DNA.
  • Phosphate Group: Linked to the 5'-hydroxyl group of a nucleoside via a phosphoester bond.

Nucleotides are linked together by 3'-5' phosphodiester linkages to form a polynucleotide chain, creating a sugar-phosphate backbone with projecting nitrogenous bases.

The Double Helix Model

Proposed by Watson and Crick in 1953 based on X-ray diffraction data from Franklin and Wilkins, and Chargaff's rules. Key features include:

  • Two polynucleotide chains with antiparallel polarity (one 5' to 3', the other 3' to 5').
  • Hydrogen bonding between complementary bases: Adenine pairs with Thymine (2 H-bonds), and Guanine pairs with Cytosine (3 H-bonds).
  • Right-handed coiling with a pitch of 3.4 nm and approximately 10 base pairs per turn.

Packaging of DNA Helix

The length of DNA in a mammalian cell is approximately 2.2 meters, requiring sophisticated packaging:

  • Prokaryotes: DNA is held by positively charged proteins in a region called the nucleoid.
  • Eukaryotes: DNA wraps around a positively charged octamer of histone proteins (rich in lysine and arginine) to form a nucleosome.
  • Nucleosomes form a "beads-on-a-string" structure called chromatin.
  • Chromatin is classified into transcriptionally active, loosely packed euchromatin and inactive, densely packed heterochromatin.
The Search for Genetic Material

Transforming Principle

Aim: To understand the pattern of inheritance and identify the genetic material.

Apparatus/Setup: Streptococcus pneumoniae bacteria (virulent S-strain with a polysaccharide coat, and non-virulent R-strain) and mice.

Observation: Mice injected with live S-strain die; those with live R-strain or heat-killed S-strain live. However, mice injected with a mixture of heat-killed S-strain and live R-strain die, and live S-strain bacteria are recovered from their bodies.

Inference: The R-strain bacteria were transformed by a "transforming principle" from the heat-killed S-strain, enabling them to synthesize a smooth coat and become virulent.

Biochemical Characterization

Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty purified biochemicals from heat-killed S-cells to determine the transforming substance:

  • Proteases and RNases did not affect transformation.
  • Digestion with DNase inhibited transformation, proving that DNA is the transforming genetic material.

The Hershey-Chase Experiment

Unequivocal proof that DNA is the genetic material came from experiments by Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase (1952) using bacteriophages:

  • Phages grown on radioactive phosphorus ($^{32}\text{P}$) labeled the DNA.
  • Phages grown on radioactive sulfur ($^{35}\text{S}$) labeled the protein coat.
  • Only radioactive $^{32}\text{P}$ was detected inside infected Escherichia coli cells, proving that DNA enters the bacteria and acts as the genetic material.

Properties of Genetic Material

To act as genetic material, a molecule must fulfill these criteria:

  1. Ability to replicate (generate its replica).
  2. Chemical and structural stability.
  3. Scope for slow changes (mutation) required for evolution.
  4. Ability to express itself in the form of Mendelian characters.

DNA is chemically less reactive and structurally more stable than RNA due to the absence of the reactive 2'-OH group and the presence of Thymine instead of Uracil.

RNA World & DNA Replication

The RNA World

RNA was the first genetic material. Essential life processes like translation, splicing, and metabolism evolved around RNA. RNA can act as both genetic material and a catalyst (ribozyme), but its reactivity led to the evolution of the more stable double-stranded DNA.

Semiconservative Replication

Watson and Crick proposed that the two strands of DNA separate and act as templates for synthesizing new complementary strands. This was experimentally proven by Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl (1958) using heavy nitrogen ($^{15}\text{N}$) and normal nitrogen ($^{14}\text{N}$) isotopes in Escherichia coli, demonstrating hybrid density DNA after one generation.

Replication Machinery and Enzymes

DNA replication is highly accurate and energetically expensive, occurring at a localized replication fork:

  • DNA-dependent DNA Polymerase: Catalyzes polymerization only in the 5' to 3' direction.
  • Leading Strand: Continuous synthesis on the template with 3' to 5' polarity.
  • Lagging Strand: Discontinuous synthesis on the template with 5' to 3' polarity, producing fragments joined by DNA ligase.
  • Replication begins at a specific sequence called the origin of replication.
Transcription

The Transcription Unit

Transcription is the process of copying genetic information from one strand of DNA into RNA. A transcription unit consists of:

  • Promoter: Binding site for RNA polymerase, located upstream (5'-end of coding strand).
  • Structural Gene: The template area for transcription.
  • Terminator: Defines the end of transcription, located downstream (3'-end of coding strand).
  • Template Strand: Has 3' to 5' polarity.
  • Coding Strand: Has 5' to 3' polarity and shares the sequence of the transcribed RNA (with Uracil instead of Thymine).

Transcription in Prokaryotes vs. Eukaryotes

In prokaryotes, a single RNA polymerase transcribes all RNA types, and translation can begin before transcription is complete (coupled transcription-translation).

In eukaryotes, there are three distinct RNA polymerases and complex post-transcriptional processing:

  • Splicing: Removal of non-coding introns and joining of coding exons.
  • Capping: Addition of methyl guanosine triphosphate to the 5'-end of hnRNA.
  • Tailing: Addition of adenylate residues (poly-A tail) to the 3'-end.
Genetic Code & Translation

Salient Features of the Genetic Code

Deciphered by scientists including George Gamow, Har Gobind Khorana, and Marshall Nirenberg. Key features include:

  • The codon is a triplet. There are $4^3 = 64$ codons; 61 code for amino acids, and 3 act as stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA).
  • The code is degenerate (some amino acids are coded by multiple codons) and universal.
  • AUG: Dual function as the initiator codon and coding for Methionine.

tRNA - The Adapter Molecule

The tRNA molecule acts as an adapter. It features an anticodon loop complementary to the mRNA codon and an amino acid acceptor end that binds to specific amino acids.

The Translation Process

Translation is the synthesis of a polypeptide chain from mRNA, occurring in the ribosome:

  1. Charging of tRNA: Amino acids are activated by ATP and linked to their cognate tRNA.
  2. Initiation: Ribosome binds to mRNA at the start codon (AUG), recognized by initiator tRNA.
  3. Elongation: Aminoacyl-tRNAs sequentially bind to codons, and peptide bonds are formed, catalyzed by the ribozyme.
  4. Termination: A release factor binds to the stop codon, releasing the completed polypeptide.
Regulation of Gene Expression & Genomics

The Lac Operon

Elucidated by Francois Jacob and Jacques Monod, the lac operon is a transcriptionally regulated system in bacteria:

  • Regulatory Gene (i gene): Codes for the repressor protein.
  • Structural Genes: z (codes for $eta$-galactosidase), y (codes for permease), and a (codes for transacetylase).
  • In the Absence of Inducer: Repressor binds to the operator, blocking RNA polymerase.
  • In the Presence of Inducer (Lactose): Lactose binds and inactivates the repressor, allowing transcription of structural genes.

Human Genome Project (HGP)

A 13-year mega project (1990-2003) aimed at sequencing the entire human genome ($3 \times 10^9$ base pairs). It utilized methodologies like Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs) and Sequence Annotation, giving rise to the field of Bioinformatics.

DNA Fingerprinting

Developed by Alec Jeffreys, this technique identifies genetic variations based on DNA polymorphism in repetitive DNA sequences known as Variable Number of Tandem Repeats (VNTRs). Steps include DNA isolation, restriction digestion, gel electrophoresis, Southern blotting, hybridization with a VNTR probe, and autoradiography.
