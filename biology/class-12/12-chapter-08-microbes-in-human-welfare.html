Class 12 — Chapter 8: Microbes in Human Welfare

Introduction to Microbes

Besides macroscopic plants and animals, microbes are the major components of biological systems on Earth. They are present everywhere – in soil, water, air, inside our bodies, and in extreme environments like thermal vents (geysers) where temperatures exceed 100°C, deep in soil, under thick snow layers, and in highly acidic environments.

  • Diversity of Microbes: Includes protozoa, bacteria, fungi, microscopic animal and plant viruses, viroids, and proteinaceous infectious agents called prions.
  • Culturability: Bacteria and many fungi can be grown on nutritive media to form visible colonies, which are highly useful in scientific studies.
8.1 Microbes in Household Products

Microbes and their products are used daily in various household activities:

  • Curd Production: Micro-organisms like Lactobacillus and others, collectively called Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB), grow in milk and convert it to curd. During growth, LAB produce acids that coagulate and partially digest milk proteins. A small amount of curd added as an inoculum (starter) contains millions of LAB, which multiply at suitable temperatures. LAB also improves nutritional quality by increasing Vitamin $B_{12}$ and plays a beneficial role in checking disease-causing microbes in the stomach.
  • Dough Fermentation: Dough used for making foods like dosa and idli is fermented by bacteria. The puffed-up appearance of the dough is due to the production of $CO_2$ gas. Dough for making bread is fermented using baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae).
  • Traditional Drinks and Foods: Toddy, a traditional drink of southern India, is made by fermenting sap from palms. Microbes are also used to ferment fish, soyabean, and bamboo shoots.
  • Cheese: One of the oldest food items utilizing microbes. Different varieties are known by their characteristic texture, flavor, and taste:
    • Swiss Cheese: Characterized by large holes due to the production of a large amount of $CO_2$ by the bacterium Propionibacterium sharmanii.
    • Roquefort Cheese: Ripened by growing a specific fungus on them, which imparts a particular flavor.
8.2 Microbes in Industrial Products

Industrial production requires growing microbes in very large vessels called fermentors to synthesize valuable products like beverages and antibiotics.

8.2.1 Fermented Beverages

Yeasts have been used since ancient times for producing beverages like wine, beer, whisky, brandy, or rum.

  • Brewer's Yeast: Saccharomyces cerevisiae is used for fermenting malted cereals and fruit juices to produce ethanol.
  • Distillation vs. Non-distillation: Wine and beer are produced without distillation, whereas whisky, brandy, and rum are produced by distillation of the fermented broth.

8.2.2 Antibiotics

Antibiotics are chemical substances produced by some microbes that can kill or retard the growth of other disease-causing microbes.

  • Discovery of Penicillin: The first antibiotic discovered was Penicillin, which was a chance discovery by Alexander Fleming. While working on Staphylococci bacteria, he observed a mould (Penicillium notatum) growing on an unwashed culture plate around which the bacteria could not grow.
  • Development: Its full potential as an effective antibiotic was established later by Ernest Chain and Howard Florey. Fleming, Chain, and Florey were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1945.
  • Diseases Treated: Antibiotics have greatly improved our capacity to treat deadly diseases such as plague, whooping cough (kali khansi), diphtheria (gal ghotu), and leprosy (kusht rog).

8.2.3 Chemicals, Enzymes, and other Bioactive Molecules

Microbes are commercially used for producing organic acids, alcohols, enzymes, and bioactive molecules:

  • Organic Acid Producers:
    • Citric acid: Aspergillus niger (a fungus)
    • Acetic acid: Acetobacter aceti (a bacterium)
    • Butyric acid: Clostridium butylicum (a bacterium)
    • Lactic acid: Lactobacillus (a bacterium)
  • Enzymes:
    • Lipases: Used in detergent formulations to help remove oily stains from laundry.
    • Pectinases and Proteases: Used to clarify bottled fruit juices.
    • Streptokinase: Produced by the bacterium Streptococcus and modified by genetic engineering; used as a 'clot buster' to remove clots from blood vessels of patients who have undergone myocardial infarction (heart attack).
  • Bioactive Molecules:
    • Cyclosporin A: Used as an immunosuppressive agent in organ-transplant patients; produced by the fungus Trichoderma polysporum.
    • Statins: Used as blood-cholesterol lowering agents; produced by the yeast Monascus purpureus. It acts by competitively inhibiting the enzyme responsible for cholesterol synthesis.
8.3 Microbes in Sewage Treatment

Municipal waste-water (sewage) contains large amounts of organic matter and pathogenic microbes. It must be treated in Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) before disposal to make it less polluting. Treatment is carried out in two distinct stages:

Primary Treatment

This stage involves the physical removal of large and small particles through filtration and sedimentation:

  1. Floating debris is removed by sequential filtration.
  2. Grit (soil and small pebbles) is removed by sedimentation.
  3. All solids that settle form the primary sludge, and the supernatant forms the effluent, which is taken for secondary treatment.

Secondary Treatment (Biological Treatment)

This stage relies on microbial activity to digest organic matter:

  1. The primary effluent is passed into large aeration tanks where it is constantly agitated mechanically and air is pumped into it. This allows vigorous growth of useful aerobic microbes into flocs (masses of bacteria associated with fungal filaments to form mesh-like structures).
  2. These microbes consume the major part of the organic matter in the effluent, significantly reducing the BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand).
  3. BOD Definition: The amount of oxygen that would be consumed if all the organic matter in one liter of water were oxidized by bacteria. It is a direct measure of the organic matter present in water; greater BOD indicates higher polluting potential.
  4. Once BOD is significantly reduced, the effluent is passed into a settling tank where bacterial flocs are allowed to sediment. This sediment is called activated sludge.
  5. A small part of the activated sludge is pumped back into the aeration tank to serve as inoculum. The remaining major part is pumped into large tanks called anaerobic sludge digesters.
  6. In these digesters, anaerobic bacteria digest the bacteria and fungi in the sludge. During digestion, a mixture of gases is produced (methane, hydrogen sulphide, and carbon dioxide), which forms biogas.
  7. The treated effluent is released into natural water bodies like rivers and streams.

Conservation Plans: The Ministry of Environment and Forests has initiated the Ganga Action Plan and Yamuna Action Plan to build sewage treatment plants and prevent untreated sewage discharge into these major rivers.

8.4 Microbes in Production of Biogas

Biogas is a mixture of gases (predominantly methane) produced by microbial activity, used as fuel.

  • Methanogens: Anaerobic bacteria that grow on cellulosic material and produce large amounts of methane along with $CO_2$ and $H_2$. A common example is Methanobacterium.
  • Occurrence: These bacteria are found in anaerobic sludge during sewage treatment and in the rumen (a part of the stomach) of cattle, where they help break down cellulose.
  • Gobar Gas: The excreta (dung) of cattle, commonly called gobar, is rich in these bacteria and is used to generate biogas (gobar gas).
  • Biogas Plant Structure: Consists of a concrete tank (10-15 feet deep) where bio-wastes and a slurry of dung are fed. A floating cover is placed over the slurry, which rises as gas is produced. The plant has an outlet pipe to supply biogas to nearby houses, and another outlet to remove spent slurry, which is used as fertilizer.
  • Development in India: The technology was developed mainly due to the efforts of the Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI) and the Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC).
8.5 Microbes as Biocontrol Agents

Biocontrol refers to the use of biological methods for controlling plant diseases and pests, reducing reliance on toxic chemical pesticides and insecticides.

  • Natural Predation: Organic farming relies on natural predation and maintaining biodiversity rather than eradication. Examples:
    • Ladybird (beetle with red and black markings) is used to control aphids.
    • Dragonflies are used to get rid of mosquitoes.
  • Microbial Biocontrol Agents:
    • Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt): Used to control butterfly caterpillars. Dried spores are mixed with water and sprayed onto vulnerable plants. In the gut of the insect larvae, the toxin is released, killing them while leaving other insects unharmed. Genetic engineering has introduced Bt-toxin genes directly into plants, creating pest-resistant varieties like Bt-cotton.
    • Trichoderma: Free-living fungi common in root ecosystems that act as effective biocontrol agents against several plant pathogens.
    • Baculoviruses (Genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus): Pathogens that attack insects and other arthropods. They are excellent candidates for species-specific, narrow-spectrum insecticidal applications, having no negative impacts on plants, mammals, birds, fish, or non-target insects. They are highly useful in Integrated Pest Management (IPM) programs.
8.6 Microbes as Biofertilisers

Biofertilisers are organisms that enrich the nutrient quality of the soil. The main sources are bacteria, fungi, and cyanobacteria:

  • Bacteria:
    • Rhizobium: Forms symbiotic associations in the root nodules of leguminous plants, fixing atmospheric nitrogen into organic forms.
    • Azospirillum and Azotobacter: Free-living bacteria in the soil that fix atmospheric nitrogen, enriching soil nitrogen content.
  • Fungi (Mycorrhiza): Fungi form symbiotic associations with plants called mycorrhiza. Many members of the genus Glomus form mycorrhiza. The fungal symbiont absorbs phosphorus from the soil and passes it to the plant. These plants also show resistance to root-borne pathogens, tolerance to salinity and drought, and overall increased growth.
  • Cyanobacteria: Autotrophic microbes widely distributed in aquatic and terrestrial environments that can fix atmospheric nitrogen. Examples include Anabaena, Nostoc, and Oscillatoria. In paddy fields, cyanobacteria serve as an important biofertiliser. Blue-green algae also add organic matter to the soil, increasing its fertility.
