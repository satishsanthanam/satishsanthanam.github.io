Biotechnology: Class 11 — Chapter 3: Biomolecules

3.1 Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are one of the most abundant classes of biomolecules in nature. Chemically, they are aldehyde or ketone derivatives of polyhydric alcohols. Their primary biological role is to function as a primary source of energy, energy stores, metabolic intermediates, and structural components of bacterial and plant cell walls.

(A) Classification of Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are classified into three major categories based on the number of monomeric units:

  1. Monosaccharides: Simple sugars that cannot be further hydrolysed. They contain free aldehyde ($-CHO$) or ketone ($>C=O$) groups and two or more hydroxyl ($-OH$) groups, with the general formula $C_n(H_2O)_n$.
  2. Oligosaccharides: Carbohydrates containing 2 to 10 monosaccharide units joined by glycosidic bonds (e.g., maltose, lactose, sucrose).
  3. Polysaccharides: High-molecular-weight polymers consisting of more than 10 monosaccharide units linked by glycosidic bonds (e.g., starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin).

(B) Structure and Properties of Carbohydrates

  • Asymmetric (Chiral) Carbon: All monosaccharides except dihydroxyacetone contain one or more chiral carbons, making them optically active isomers (enantiomers). A molecule with $n$ chiral centres has $2^n$ stereoisomers (e.g., glyceraldehyde has $2^1 = 2$; glucose has $2^4 = 16$).
  • D and L Isomers: Determined by the orientation of the $-OH$ group on the chiral carbon most distant from the carbonyl carbon. If $-OH$ is on the right, it is a D-sugar; if on the left, it is an L-sugar. Most biological sugars are D-sugars.
  • Anomers: Isomeric cyclic forms of monosaccharides that differ only in configuration about the hemiacetal or hemiketal carbon (anomeric carbon). In the $\alpha$-anomer, the $-OH$ group is opposite the $CH_2OH$ group; in the $\beta$-anomer, it is on the same side.
  • Mutarotation: The spontaneous interconversion of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ anomers in aqueous solution via a brief opening into the linear form.
  • Epimers: Isomers differing in configuration at only one specific carbon atom (e.g., mannose is a C-2 epimer of glucose; galactose is a C-4 epimer of glucose).
  • Glycoconjugates: Carbohydrates covalently linked to proteins or lipids:
    • Glycoproteins: Predominantly protein component.
    • Proteoglycans: Predominantly carbohydrate component.
    • Glycolipids: Carbohydrate conjugated with lipids.

3.2 Fatty Acids and Lipids

Lipids are hydrophobic, non-polar organic compounds soluble in organic solvents. They are primarily composed of hydrocarbon chains connected to glycerol via ester linkages.

(A) Fatty Acids

Fatty acids are long-chain hydrocarbons containing a terminal carboxylic acid group. They are synthesized from two-carbon units, resulting in an even number of carbon atoms.

  • Saturated Fatty Acids: Contain no double bonds (e.g., Palmitic acid, Stearic acid).
  • Unsaturated Fatty Acids: Contain one or more double bonds. Classified into:
    • Monounsaturated (MUFAs): One double bond (e.g., Oleic acid).
    • Polyunsaturated (PUFAs): More than one double bond (e.g., Linoleic acid, Linolenic acid, Arachidonic acid).
  • Nomenclature: Denoted as [Total Carbons]:[Number of Double Bonds] (Δ^positions). For example, linoleic acid is $18:2 (\Delta^{9,12})$.

(B) Classification of Lipids

  1. Simple Lipids: Esters of fatty acids with various alcohols.
    • Triacylglycerols (Triglycerides): Esters of glycerol and three fatty acids. Simple triglycerides contain identical fatty acids; mixed triglycerides contain different fatty acids. Primary energy storage in adipose tissue.
    • Waxes: Esters of long-chain fatty acids with high-molecular-weight monohydric alcohols. Serve as protective coatings.
  2. Compound Lipids: Esters of fatty acids containing additional hydrophilic groups, making them amphipathic.
    • Phospholipids: Major membrane lipids. Composed of two hydrophobic fatty acid tails and a hydrophilic phosphate head. Divided into glycerophospholipids (glycerol backbone, e.g., phosphatidylcholine) and sphingophospholipids (sphingoid base backbone, e.g., sphingomyelin). Form micelles in water.
    • Steroids: Characterized by a four-fused ring structure. Cholesterol is the key precursor for steroid hormones (testosterone, estradiol), vitamin D, and bile acids. Plant steroids include phytosterols and stigmasterols; fungal steroids include ergosterol.
3.3 Amino Acids

Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins. They consist of a central $\alpha$-carbon linked to an acidic carboxyl group ($-COOH$), a basic amino group ($-NH_2$), a hydrogen atom, and a variable side chain ($-R$ group).

(A) Stereochemistry and Classification

  • Chirality: The $\alpha$-carbon is asymmetric in 19 of the 20 standard amino acids (except glycine), allowing for optically active L and D isomers. Only L-amino acids are found in ribosomal proteins.
  • Zwitterion State: At physiological pH (~7.0), both the carboxyl group (deprotonated to $-COO^-$) and the amino group (protonated to $-NH_3^+$) are ionized, forming a dipolar ion with a net charge of zero.

(B) Non-Standard and Non-Protein Amino Acids

  • Non-Standard Amino Acids: Modified after translation in proteins (e.g., 4-hydroxyproline, 5-hydroxylysine, selenocysteine, $\gamma$-carboxyglutamic acid).
  • Non-Protein Amino Acids: Occur naturally in cells but are not incorporated into proteins (e.g., L-ornithine, L-citrulline, $\beta$-alanine, creatine, $\gamma$-aminobutyrate).
3.4 Protein Structure

Proteins are linear polymers of amino acids linked covalently by peptide bonds. They exhibit four hierarchical levels of structural organization.

3.4.1 Primary Structure

The linear sequence of amino acids linked by covalent peptide bonds (amide bonds). Peptide bond formation occurs via a condensation reaction between the $\alpha$-carboxyl group of one amino acid and the $\alpha$-amino group of another, releasing $H_2O$.

  • Polarity: Polypeptides have an amino-terminal (N-terminal) end on the left and a carboxy-terminal (C-terminal) end on the right.
  • Naming Convention: Amino acid residues are named by adding the suffix -yl sequentially from the N-terminus, leaving the C-terminal residue unchanged.
  • The average molecular weight of an amino acid residue is approximately 110 Da.

Peptide Bond Conformation & Ramachandran Plot

Peptide Bond Planarity: The six atoms of the peptide group lie in a single plane. Resonance interactions give the peptide bond ~40% double-bond character, preventing free rotation around the $C-N$ bond.

Backbone Flexibility: Rotation is permitted only around the pure single bonds adjacent to the peptide group: the $C_\alpha-N$ bond (dihedral angle phi, $\phi$) and the $C_\alpha-C$ bond (dihedral angle psi, $\psi$).

Ramachandran Plot: A 2D plot mapping the sterically allowed conformations of $\phi$ and $\psi$ angles. It defines distinct regions corresponding to common secondary structures: $\beta$-sheets (parallel/antiparallel), right-handed $\alpha$-helices, and collagen helices.

3.4.2 Secondary Structure

Local spatial folding of the polypeptide backbone, stabilized by hydrogen bonds between main-chain peptide groups:

  • \alpha-Helix: A rod-like helical structure stabilized by intrachain hydrogen bonds between the carbonyl oxygen ($C=O$) of residue $i$ and the amide hydrogen ($N-H$) of residue $i+4$. One turn of the helix spans 5.4 Å. Right-handed helices are sterically more stable. Found in keratin, myosin, and ferritin.
  • \beta-Pleated Sheet: Formed by hydrogen bonding between peptide groups of adjacent polypeptide strands. Strands can run in the same direction (parallel) or opposite directions (antiparallel).

3.4.3 Tertiary and Quaternary Structures

  • Tertiary Structure: The overall three-dimensional folding of a single polypeptide chain. Stabilized by weak interactions (hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic/electrostatic bonds) and covalent disulfide bonds (formed by the oxidation of cysteine residues). Hydrophobic residues are packed in the interior, while polar residues remain on the exterior.
  • Quaternary Structure: The spatial arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits (identical or non-identical) in a multi-subunit protein complex. Subunits are held together by non-covalent interactions and disulfide bridges (e.g., homodimers, heterotetramers like hemoglobin).
3.5 Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polymers of nucleotides linked by phosphodiester bonds, serving as the primary genetic material of cells and viruses.

(A) Chemical Components

  1. Pentose Sugar: Closed five-membered rings. D-ribose in RNA and 2'-deoxy-D-ribose in DNA (lacking an $-OH$ group at the C-2' position).
  2. Nitrogenous Bases: Planar, aromatic ring structures that absorb UV light at 260 nm. Divided into:
    • Purines: Double-ring structures; Adenine (A) and Guanine (G).
    • Pyrimidines: Single-ring structures; Cytosine (C), Thymine (T, unique to DNA), and Uracil (U, unique to RNA).
  3. Phosphate Group: Esterified to the pentose sugar.

Nucleoside vs. Nucleotide:
$$\text{Nucleoside} = \text{Base} + \text{Pentose Sugar}$$
$$\text{Nucleotide} = \text{Base} + \text{Pentose Sugar} + \text{Phosphate Group}$$

(B) Polynucleotide Chain & Watson-Crick DNA Model

  • Phosphodiester Linkage: Nucleotides are covalently linked by a $3',5'$-phosphodiester bond, where the 3'-hydroxyl group of one sugar is esterified to the 5'-phosphate group of the adjacent sugar. This establishes a distinct $5' \rightarrow 3'$ polarity.
  • Watson-Crick Double Helix: DNA consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide chains wound around a central axis in a right-handed double helix. The sugar-phosphate backbones form the outer rails, while the bases stack inside.
  • Complementary Base Pairing: Adenine pairs with Thymine via 2 hydrogen bonds ($A = T$); Guanine pairs with Cytosine via 3 hydrogen bonds ($G \equiv C$).
  • Structural Variants: B-DNA (most stable, standard physiological form), A-DNA (right-handed, wider, 11 bp/turn), and Z-DNA (left-handed, zigzag backbone, 12 bp/turn).
  • Denaturation (Melting): Separation of double-stranded DNA into single strands by heating or chemical treatment, which disrupts hydrogen bonds. The temperature at which 50% of DNA is denatured is the melting temperature ($T_m$). Spontaneous renaturation upon cooling is called annealing.

(C) Types of RNA

  • Messenger RNA (mRNA): Single-stranded linear molecule that carries genetic information from DNA to ribosomes. Features untranslated regions (UTRs), an initiation codon, a coding region, and a stop codon. Eukaryotic mRNA undergoes 5' capping (7-methylguanylate) and 3' polyadenylation.
  • Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): Structural and catalytic component of ribosomes.
    • Prokaryotic (70S) Ribosome: Contains 16S rRNA (30S subunit), 23S and 5S rRNAs (50S subunit).
    • Eukaryotic (80S) Ribosome: Contains 18S rRNA (40S subunit), 28S, 5.8S, and 5S rRNAs (60S subunit).
  • Transfer RNA (tRNA): Small adaptor molecules that transfer amino acids to ribosomes during translation. Folds into a clover-leaf secondary structure with an acceptor arm (terminating in a $3'\text{-CCA}$ sequence for amino acid attachment) and an anticodon arm.
