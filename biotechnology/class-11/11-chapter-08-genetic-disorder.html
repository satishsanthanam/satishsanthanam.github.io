8.1 Chromosomal Abnormalities and Syndromes

Introduction to Chromosomal Aberrations

Chromosomes can suffer structural damage or numerical changes due to environmental radiation, food intake, or internal genetic conditions. These changes often lead to significant phenotypic alterations, diseases, or syndromes.

  • Structural Chromosomal Abnormality: A change in the physical structure of the chromosome (aberration).
  • Numerical Chromosomal Abnormality: A change in the total number of chromosomes.
  • Aneuploidy: A condition where one or a few chromosomes are absent or present in extra copies. This includes Monosomy ($2n-1$, e.g., monosomy of chromosome 1) and Trisomy ($2n+1$, e.g., trisomy of chromosome X).
  • Polyploidy: A condition where the entire set of chromosomes is multiplied (e.g., triploid $3n$, tetraploid $4n$, hexaploid $6n$, octoploid $8n$). Highly common in plants and artificial breeding (e.g., hexaploid bread wheat, triploid banana/apple, octoploid strawberry/sugar cane).

Structural Chromosomal Abnormalities

  1. Deletion: A segment of a chromosome breaks away, leading to shortening. Example: Retinoblastoma (deletion of a portion of chromosome 13). Deletion of both ends can lead to the formation of a ring chromosome.
  2. Duplication: A segment of the chromosome is repeated, resulting in a longer chromosome. Example: Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (duplication of genes on chromosome 17).
  3. Inversion: A segment breaks away, completely reverses by 180 degrees, and reattaches. The overall length remains the same, but the gene orientation is reversed. Example: RCAD syndrome (inversion of a segment of chromosome 17).
  4. Translocation: A segment breaks away and reattaches to another chromosome. Includes reciprocal translocation (mutual exchange of segments, e.g., Burkitt's lymphoma between chromosomes 8 and 14) and Robertsonian translocation (one-way attachment without mutual exchange, decreasing the cell's chromosome count).

8.2 Monogenic Disorders and Pedigree Mapping

Introduction to Monogenic Diseases

Monogenic diseases are caused by an error or mutation in a single gene, inherited according to Mendel's Laws. Over 10,000 human diseases are estimated to be monogenic.

Pedigree Analysis Symbols

Pedigree analysis is the interpretation of family tree information to diagnose inherited genetic diseases.

  • Square: Male
  • Circle: Female
  • Filled Square/Circle: Affected individual
  • Horizontal line connecting male and female: Mating
  • Suspended branch: Offspring in birth order (generations labeled I, II; offspring numbered II-1, II-2)
  • Diverging lines from a single point: Identical twins
  • Diverging lines with a crossbar or separate origins: Non-identical twins

Classification of Monogenic Disorders

  • Sickle Cell Anemia: Mutation in hemoglobin-$eta$ gene on chromosome 11. Defective Hb molecules cluster into rod-like structures under low oxygen, causing red blood cells to assume a rigid, sickle shape. Common in sub-Saharan Africa, India (Deccan plateau, Kerala, Tamil Nadu), etc. Genotype $s/s$ is affected; $S/s$ is carrier; $S/S$ is normal.
  • Cystic Fibrosis: Produces abnormally thick, sticky mucus that damages organs, especially lungs, leading to chronic infections.
  • Tay-Sachs Disease: Absence of enzyme hexosaminidase A, leading to fatty substance accumulation in nerve cells, affecting the brain. Fatal in childhood. High incidence in European Ashkenazi Jewish populations.
  • Phenylketonuria: Mutation in phenylalanine hydroxylase gene, causing toxic accumulation of phenylalanine in the blood.
Autosomal DominantOnly one copy of the abnormal allele is needed to manifest the disease. Normal individuals are homozygous recessive ($d/d$).
  • Achondroplasia: A form of dwarfism. Genotype $D/d$ represents mild disease (surviving heterozygotes); $D/D$ is severely affected and often lethal; $d/d$ is normal.
  • Huntington's Disease: A rare autosomal dominant disorder affecting the nervous system.
X-linked RecessiveMutated gene is on the X chromosome. Females ($XX$) are typically carriers if they have one mutated copy; males ($XY$) are affected because they have only one X chromosome. Affected fathers pass the gene to all daughters (carriers) but no sons.
  • Haemophilia: Bleeding disorder caused by mutation in coagulation factor VIII gene (Type A) or factor IX gene (Type B), preventing effective blood clotting and causing spontaneous bleeding.
  • Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD): Mutation in the dystrophin gene, leading to reduction/absence of dystrophin protein, causing muscle degeneration and fragility.
X-linked DominantAffected males pass the mutated dominant gene to all daughters but no sons. Affected females pass it to half of their sons and daughters.
  • Hypophosphatemia: A type of vitamin D resistant rickets.
  • Alport Syndrome: Associated with progressive hearing loss and kidney disease.
8.3 Polygenic Disorders

Characteristics of Polygenic Disorders

Polygenic disorders are caused by the combined action or defects of multiple genes. They depend on the simultaneous association of several genes and environmental factors, and cannot be explained by simple Mendelian monogenic inheritance.

Major Polygenic Disorders

  • Diabetes Mellitus: A heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by persistent high blood sugar (hyperglycemia).
    • Type 1 Diabetes (T1D): Insulin-dependent diabetes (IDDM). Caused by immunological destruction of pancreatic beta cells. Accounts for ~10% of cases. Requires lifelong insulin injections.
    • Type 2 Diabetes (T2D): Non-insulin-dependent diabetes (NIDDM). Commonest form (~90% of cases). Caused by impaired insulin secretion and peripheral insulin resistance.
  • Hypertension: Persistent increase in blood pressure. Major risk factor for renal failure, heart disease, and stroke.
    • Normal BP: $<120$ (systolic) / $<80$ (diastolic) mm Hg
    • Elevated BP: 120-129 / $<80$ mm Hg
    • Hypertension Stage 1: 130-139 or 80-89 mm Hg
    • Hypertension Stage 2: $\ge 140$ or $\ge 90$ mm Hg
  • Coronary Heart Disease (CHD): Narrowing of the coronary artery due to gradual build-up of fatty material (plaque) on its wall, a condition called atherosclerosis. This reduces oxygen-rich blood supply to heart muscles, causing ischemia (formerly called ischaemic heart disease).
Mitochondrial Inheritance and Diseases

Mitochondrial Genetics

Mitochondria are cytoplasmic organelles responsible for ATP production. They contain circular DNA, making them the only animal organelle besides the nucleus to contain DNA and genes.

  • Maternal Inheritance: Sperm contains very few mitochondria, so offspring inherit mitochondrial genes exclusively from the mother. An affected father cannot transmit mitochondrial diseases to his offspring.
  • Heteroplasmy & Severity: Disease severity and symptoms depend on the ratio of normal to abnormal (mutated) mitochondria within the cells.
  • Affected Organs: High-energy demanding organs like the brain, heart, kidneys, skeletal muscle, and liver are most severely affected by mitochondrial gene defects.
