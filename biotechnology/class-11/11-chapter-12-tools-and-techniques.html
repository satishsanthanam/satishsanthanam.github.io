Biotechnology: Class 11 — Chapter 12: Tools and Techniques

12.1 Microscopy

Microscopy is a fundamental biological technique enabling the visualization of structures beyond the limit of resolution of the human eye, ranging from cellular organelles to macromolecular complexes like DNA.

12.1.1 Magnification and Resolution

  • Magnification: The ratio of the size of the retinal image with the microscope to that without the microscope. For a single lens, magnification ($M$) is calculated as:
    $$M = \frac{f}{f-d}$$
    where $f$ is the focal length of the lens and $d$ is the distance of the object from the lens.
  • Compound Microscope Magnification: The product of the magnifying power of the objective lens ($M_o$) and the eyepiece ($M_e$):
    $$M = M_o \times M_e$$
  • Resolving Power: The ability of an optical instrument to form separate images of two objects situated very close to each other, measured by the minimum distance between the two points.

12.1.2 Functioning of a Light Microscope

A compound light microscope utilizes a dual-lens system to illuminate and magnify specimens. Light from a source (mirror or bulb) is focused by a condenser onto the specimen. The objective lens (typically 4x, 10x, 40x, or 100x) forms a real, magnified image, which is further magnified by the eyepiece (10x or 15x) to form a virtual image for the observer.

12.1.3 Different Forms of Microscopy

Microscopy TypePhysical PrincipleKey Applications & Stains
Bright Field MicroscopyLight passes directly through the specimen; contrast relies on light absorption by stained regions.General tissue and cell morphology. Stains: carmine, eosin, safranin, methylene blue, giemsa.
Dark Field MicroscopyCentral light beam is blocked; specimen is illuminated by oblique light, appearing bright against a dark background.Detection of highly minute structures, living bacteria, mitochondria, nuclei, and vacuoles.
Phase Contrast MicroscopyTranslates minute phase shifts in light passing through cellular structures of varying densities into amplitude (contrast) changes.Study of living, unstained cells, cell organelles, and chromosomes.
Fluorescence MicroscopySpecimens are stained with fluorophores that absorb short-wavelength light and emit longer-wavelength light.Immunodiagnosis, pathogen identification. Fluorophores: acridine orange, bisbenzimide, merocyanine.
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)An electron beam passes through an ultra-thin specimen coated with heavy metal salts (lead, tungsten) in a vacuum.High-resolution internal ultrastructure of cells and macromolecular complexes.
Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)An electron beam scans the surface of a specimen coated with gold or platinum; reflected secondary electrons form a 3D image.High-resolution 3D surface topography of cells and micro-organisms.
Confocal MicroscopyUses point illumination via lasers and a spatial pinhole to eliminate out-of-focus light, allowing optical sectioning.High-resolution 3D reconstruction of thick, fluorescently labeled specimens and tissues.
12.2 Centrifugation

Centrifugation is a separation technique that segregates particles, organelles, or macromolecules based on their size, shape, and density under the influence of a centrifugal force.

12.2.1 Types of Centrifugation

  • Differential Centrifugation: Separates particles based on differences in their sedimentation rate. Successive runs at increasing centrifugal forces pellet progressively smaller components (e.g., nuclei, then mitochondria, then microsomes).
  • Density-Gradient Centrifugation: Particles travel through a continuous or discontinuous density gradient (e.g., sucrose or CsCl) until they reach a zone matching their own buoyant density (isopycnic point).
  • Ultracentrifugation: Performed at extremely high speeds (typically $\ge 100,000 \times g$) under vacuum to prevent frictional heating, allowing the separation of macromolecules like proteins and nucleic acids.
12.3 Electrophoresis

Electrophoresis is the migration of charged macromolecules through a gel matrix under the influence of an applied electric field, separating them based on their charge-to-mass ratio and molecular size.

12.3.1 Agarose Gel Electrophoresis

Used primarily for separating nucleic acids (DNA/RNA). Agarose forms a porous polysaccharide matrix held together by hydrogen bonds.

  • Migration Direction: DNA is negatively charged due to its phosphate backbone and migrates toward the positive electrode (anode).
  • Size Separation: Shorter DNA fragments travel faster through the pores than longer fragments.
  • DNA Ladder: A mixture of DNA fragments of known sizes run alongside samples to determine absolute molecular weights.
  • Visualization: Stained with ethidium bromide (EtBr), which intercalates between bases and fluoresces orange-red under a UV trans-illuminator.

12.3.2 Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE)

Used for high-resolution separation of proteins and small nucleic acids.

  • SDS-PAGE: Proteins are denatured and coated with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), an anionic detergent that imparts a uniform negative charge proportional to mass, allowing separation strictly by molecular weight.
  • Gel Matrix: Formed by polymerization of acrylamide monomer and N,N'-methylene-bis-acrylamide (BIS) cross-linker. Pore size is controlled by adjusting total monomer concentration.
  • Protein Visualization: Stained using Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250, which binds non-specifically to proteins, appearing as blue bands.

12.3.3 Tracking Dyes

Small, highly mobile charged molecules added to samples to monitor the progress of electrophoresis. Common dyes include bromophenol blue, xylene cyanol, and Orange G.

12.4 Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

ELISA is a highly sensitive, quantitative immunological assay used to detect and measure specific antigens or antibodies in a sample using enzyme-labeled reagents.

12.4.1 ELISA Methodologies

ELISA TypeAssay Configuration & MechanismKey Advantages & Disadvantages
Direct ELISAAntigen is immobilized on the plate; an enzyme-conjugated primary antibody binds directly to the target antigen.Fast, simple workflow; high risk of non-specific binding due to direct sample coating.
Indirect ELISAAntigen is immobilized; primary antibody binds to antigen, followed by an enzyme-conjugated secondary antibody targeting the primary antibody.High sensitivity (multiple secondary antibodies bind per primary); cost-effective.
Sandwich ELISACapture antibody is coated on the plate; antigen binds to it, and a second enzyme-linked detection antibody binds to a different epitope on the antigen.Extremely high specificity; does not require antigen purification.
Competitive ELISASample antigen competes with labeled antigen for a limited number of antibody binding sites. Signal intensity is inversely proportional to sample antigen concentration.Useful for detecting small antigens with few epitopes.

12.4.2 Applications of ELISA

  • Quantification of serum antibodies in viral diagnostics (e.g., HIV, hepatitis).
  • Detection of potential food allergens in the food processing industry.
  • Epidemiological tracking of disease outbreaks (e.g., bird flu, cholera).
12.5 Chromatography

Chromatography is a physical method of separation in which components are distributed between two phases: a stationary phase and a mobile phase.

12.5.1 Chromatographic Modes

Chromatography ModeStationary PhaseSeparation Mechanism
Adsorption Chromatography (ADC)Polar solid adsorbent (e.g., silica gel, alumina)Based on competitive adsorption of solute molecules onto polar active sites.
Liquid-Liquid Partition (LLC)Liquid film immobilized on a solid supportBased on differences in partition coefficients (solubility) between two immiscible solvents.
Ion-Exchange Chromatography (IEC)Charged resin (cationic or anionic exchange)Based on electrostatic attraction between solute ions and charged functional groups on the resin.
Gel Filtration ChromatographyPorous gel beads (e.g., dextran, agarose, polyacrylamide)Based on molecular size; larger molecules bypass pores and elute first, while smaller molecules enter pores and elute later.
Affinity Chromatography (AFC)Immobilized ligand specific to target biomoleculeBased on highly specific, reversible biological interactions (e.g., antigen-antibody, enzyme-substrate).
High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)Micro-particulate stationary phase packed in columnsUtilizes high pressure to pump mobile phase through fine-particle columns, yielding high resolution and speed.
Gas Chromatography (GC)Liquid or solid phase inside a capillary columnSeparates volatile organic compounds using an inert gas (e.g., helium, nitrogen) as the mobile phase.
12.6 Spectroscopy and Colorimetry

Spectroscopy studies the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter to identify, characterize, and quantify chemical substances.

12.6.1 The Beer-Lambert Law

The fundamental law governing quantitative spectrophotometric analysis. It states that absorbance ($A$) is directly proportional to the concentration ($c$) of the absorbing solute and the path length ($l$) of the sample:

$$A = \varepsilon l c$$

Where $\varepsilon$ is the molar extinction coefficient. Transmittance ($T$) is the ratio of transmitted light intensity ($I$) to incident light intensity ($I_0$):

$$T = \frac{I}{I_0}$$
$$A = -\log(T) = \log\left(\frac{I_0}{I}\right)$$

12.6.2 Colorimetry vs. UV-Visible Spectrophotometry

  • Colorimetry: Measures light absorption in the visible spectrum using colored filters to select broad bandwidths of light. Simple, portable, and inexpensive.
  • UV-Visible Spectrophotometry: Uses a prism or diffraction grating monochromator to isolate highly specific, narrow wavelengths of light across both ultraviolet (200-400 nm) and visible (400-800 nm) ranges.
12.7 Mass Spectrometry

Mass spectrometry is an analytical technique used to identify unknown compounds, quantify known materials, and elucidate molecular structures by measuring the mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$) of gas-phase ions.

Workflow: Sample Introduction → Ionization (conversion to gaseous ions) → Mass Analyzer Separation (by $m/z$ ratio) → Ion Detection → Data Output (Mass Spectrum).
12.8 Fluorescence In Situ Hybridisation (FISH)

FISH is a cytogenetic technique used to detect and localize the presence or absence of specific DNA sequences on chromosomes using fluorescently labeled complementary probes.

FISH Experimental Workflow:

  1. Design and label a single-stranded DNA probe complementary to the target genomic sequence with a fluorophore.
  2. Prepare metaphase or interphase chromosomes on a microscope slide and denature the chromosomal DNA.
  3. Denature the labeled probe and apply it to the slide to allow hybridization with the complementary target sequence.
  4. Wash away unbound probe and visualize the fluorescent signals using a fluorescence microscope.
12.9 DNA Sequencing

DNA sequencing is the process of determining the precise order of nucleotides (A, T, G, C) within a DNA molecule.

12.9.1 Sanger's Chain Termination Method

The gold-standard enzymatic sequencing method based on the selective incorporation of chain-terminating dideoxynucleotides.

  • Mechanism: Uses dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs), which lack the 3'-OH group required for phosphodiester bond formation. Incorporation of a ddNTP terminates chain elongation.
  • Reaction Setup: Four separate reactions (or one multiplexed reaction) containing template DNA, primer, DNA polymerase, normal dNTPs, and a low concentration of one specific ddNTP (ddATP, ddTTP, ddGTP, or ddCTP).
  • Resolution: The resulting fragments of varying lengths are separated by high-resolution polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) or capillary electrophoresis.

12.9.2 Chemical Degradation (Maxam-Gilbert Method)

Based on base-specific chemical cleavage of end-labeled DNA fragments. Uses hazardous chemicals (e.g., dimethyl sulphate for purines, hydrazine for pyrimidines) followed by piperidine treatment to cleave the backbone.

12.9.3 Automated and Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS)

  • Automated Sequencing: Utilizes four different fluorescently labeled ddNTPs in a single tube reaction. Fragments are separated by capillary electrophoresis and read in real-time by a laser detector.
  • Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS): High-throughput, massively parallel sequencing platforms (e.g., Illumina, Roche/454, SOLiD) capable of sequencing millions of fragments simultaneously, enabling whole-genome sequencing.
12.10 DNA Microarray

DNA microarray is a high-throughput hybridization-based technology used to analyze the expression levels of thousands of genes in parallel.

Microarray Workflow:

  1. Extract mRNA from control and experimental cell populations.
  2. Perform reverse transcription to synthesize stable complementary DNA (cDNA).
  3. Label control cDNA with one fluorophore (e.g., Cy3 - green) and experimental cDNA with another (e.g., Cy5 - red).
  4. Hybridize the mixed labeled cDNAs to a microarray chip containing immobilized single-stranded DNA probes.
  5. Wash away unhybridized cDNA, scan with a laser, and analyze the ratio of red-to-green fluorescence to identify upregulated and downregulated genes.
12.11 Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometry is a laser-based biophysical technology used for cell counting, cell sorting, and biomarker detection by suspending cells in a stream of fluid and passing them through an electronic detection apparatus.

  • Impedance (Coulter Principle): Measures changes in electrical resistance as cells pass through a microchannel, proportional to cell volume.
  • Optical Scattering: Laser light deflection provides physical data: Forward Scatter (FSC) correlates with cell size, while Side Scatter (SSC) correlates with internal complexity/granularity.
  • Fluorescence Detection: Fluorophore-conjugated antibodies targeting specific cell-surface antigens allow immunophenotyping and mechanical sorting of distinct cell populations.
