Biotechnology: Class 12 — Chapter 5: Genome Technology and Engineering

Mapping of Genome: Genetic and Physical

Genome mapping is crucial for identifying the relative positions of genes or loci on a chromosome. Two primary approaches are utilized: genetic mapping and physical mapping.

Genetic Mapping

  • Genetic Mapping: Estimates relative distances between genetic loci based on recombination frequencies during crossing over.
  • Recombination Frequency: Directly proportional to the physical distance between loci; measured in centimorgans (cM), where $1\text{ cM} = 1\text{%}$ recombination frequency.
  • Drawbacks: Sparsely populated and coarsely grained due to the limited number of known phenotypic genetic markers.

Physical Mapping

  • Physical Mapping: Identifies specific physical coordinates on the genome using DNA-based landmarks.
  • Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (RFLP): Uses restriction endonucleases to cut DNA at specific sequences; variations in fragment lengths are visualized via agarose gel electrophoresis.
  • Simple Sequence Length Polymorphism (SSLP): Uses variable repeat sequences (microsatellites/minisatellites) as length-variant physical markers.
  • Sequence Tagged Sites (STS): Unique, short DNA sequences (200–500 bp) of known genomic location, easily amplified and detected via PCR.

High-Throughput DNA Sequencing

DNA sequencing technology has evolved through three distinct generations, dramatically reducing costs and increasing throughput.

First Generation Sequencing

  • Sanger Chain Termination: Uses dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs) to terminate DNA synthesis; fragments are separated by capillary gel electrophoresis and read by a fluorescence detector.
  • Throughput: Sequences 800–1000 bp per run; up to 96 capillaries in parallel yielding 96,000 bases per run. Highly accurate but slow and expensive.

Second Generation (Next-Gen) Sequencing

  • Illumina Technology: Massively parallel sequencing-by-synthesis platform.
  • Bridge PCR Amplification: DNA fragments with ligated adaptors bind to a flow cell and bend over to form bridges, amplifying into dense clonal clusters.
  • Reversible Terminators: Uses fluorescently tagged dNTPs; one base is added at a time, imaged, chemically cleaved, and repeated in parallel across millions of clusters.

Third Generation Sequencing

  • Nanopore Sequencing: Measures real-time disruptions in ionic current as single-stranded DNA passes through a protein pore (porin) embedded in a synthetic membrane.
  • Enzymatic Control: A DNA helicase unwinds double-stranded DNA and feeds a single strand through the nanopore.
  • Advantages: Generates ultra-long reads (up to 1 Mb), portable, rapid sample processing, and low cost.

Other Genome-Related Technologies

Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS)

  • WGS: Comprehensive method to determine the entire DNA sequence of an organism.
  • De Novo Sequencing: Assembling a genome from scratch without a reference sequence.
  • Reference-Based Sequencing: Aligning and assembling sequencing reads against an existing, phylogenetically related reference genome.

Targeted and Functional Sequencing

  • Targeted Sequencing: Focuses on specific genes or genomic regions; highly cost-effective.
  • Clinical Exome Sequencing: Sequences only disease-associated exons (e.g., curated in OMIM, HGMD databases).
  • ChipSeq: Maps genome-wide DNA binding sites of transcription regulators.
  • RNASeq: Profiles the global transcriptome by converting total RNA to cDNA and sequencing it to quantify gene expression.

Metagenomics

  • Metagenomics: Direct sequencing of total genetic material (DNA/cDNA) from environmental or clinical microbial communities without culturing individual species.
  • Applications: Studying gut microbiomes, extreme environments (hot springs), viral epidemiology, and identifying novel industrial enzymes.
Genome Engineering

Genome engineering involves targeted modifications to introduce, remove, or edit specific genetic sequences within an organism.

Transposon-Mediated Engineering

  • Transposons: Mobile DNA elements ('jumping genes') that move via a "cut and paste" mechanism.
  • Knock-out: Engineered transposons insert into a target gene, disrupting its coding frame and preventing functional transcription.
  • Knock-in: Transposons are used to insert a functional foreign gene into a specific genomic locus.

CRISPR-Cas9 Genome Editing

  • CRISPR-Cas9: Repurposed bacterial immune system used for precise double-strand DNA cleavage.
  • Guide RNA (gRNA): Engineered RNA containing a sequence complementary to the target genomic DNA site; directs the Cas9 endonuclease.
  • Cas9 Endonuclease: Introduces a double-strand break (DSB) at the targeted locus.
  • Repair Pathways:
    • Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ): Error-prone repair leading to insertions/deletions (indels) that knock out genes.
    • Homologous Recombination (HDR): Uses a donor template to precisely knock in or edit a sequence.
  • Nobel Prize 2020: Awarded to Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer A. Doudna for discovering CRISPR-Cas9 genetic scissors.
Structural, Functional, and Comparative Genomics

Structural Genomics

Deals with the physical nature of the whole genome, including chromosome organization, nucleosome status, and the 3D structures of all proteins encoded by the genome.

Functional Genomics

Aims to understand how the physiological and pathological information encoded in the genome is executed, utilizing tools like RNASeq, ChipSeq, and Metagenomics.

Comparative Genomics

Compares genes and genomes across different species or individuals to identify evolutionary relationships, core conserved genes, and species-specific unique traits.

Protein Engineering

Protein engineering uses recombinant DNA technology to modify codon sequences, producing proteins with enhanced stability, activity, or novel therapeutic properties.

Rational Protein Engineering Blueprints

Wild-type subtilisin contains Met 222 in its active site which is easily oxidised in the presence of bleach, leading to a drastic reduction in its proteolytic cleansing action in detergents.

Engineering Methodology & Mechanism: Use site-directed mutagenesis to replace the codon for Met 222 with a codon for Ala. This point mutation preserves the catalytic triad (Ser 221, His 64, and Asp 32) while rendering the engineered enzyme highly stable and active even in bleach-containing detergents.

Wild-type recombinant proteins lack specific high-affinity handles, making their purification from complex cellular lysates highly tedious and low-yield.

Engineering Methodology & Mechanism: Fuse DNA encoding a 6-His-Tag (six consecutive histidine residues) to the gene of interest. The resulting chimeric protein binds with high affinity to divalent metal ions (such as Nickel, Zinc, or Cobalt) immobilized on agarose columns at mildly alkaline pH, allowing easy elution with imidazole or elution buffer.

In vivo tracking of protein localization and dynamics within living cells is impossible without destructive chemical staining or cell lysis.

Engineering Methodology & Mechanism: Fuse the gene encoding Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP) from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria to the target gene. The resulting chimeric fusion protein retains its native localization and emits green fluorescence under UV light, enabling real-time tracking via confocal microscopy.

Systemic administration of wild-type cellular toxins (e.g., Pseudomonas exotoxin) causes lethal non-specific toxicity, while wild-type antibodies lack sufficient cell-killing potency.

Engineering Methodology & Mechanism: Engineer a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) containing the variable heavy ($V_H$) and light ($V_L$) domains of a tumor-specific antibody linked by a flexible peptide. Fuse this scFv DNA sequence to a truncated toxin gene (e.g., Pseudomonas exotoxin A or Diphtheria toxin). The chimeric protein selectively binds cancer cell surface antigens, undergoes endocytosis, and releases the toxin to kill the target cell.

Mouse monoclonal antibodies produced via hybridoma technology trigger a severe anti-mouse immune response (HAMA) when administered therapeutically to humans, neutralizing their efficacy.

Engineering Methodology & Mechanism: Identify the antigen-binding complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the mouse antibody. Transfer these specific DNA sequences to the equivalent positions in a human antibody framework gene. The resulting humanised monoclonal antibody retains high antigen affinity while being recognized as "self" by the human immune system.
