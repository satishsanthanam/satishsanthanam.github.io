Biotechnology: Class 12 — Chapter 8: Animal Cell Culture

Introduction to Animal Cell Culture

Animal cell culture is the in vitro maintenance and proliferation of animal cells outside the living organism under controlled laboratory conditions, provided with appropriate nutrients, temperature, pH, and osmolality. It involves the isolation of cells from animal tissues, surgical removal of organs, and their placement into an artificial environment to enhance survival and growth.

  • Clone: A homogenous population of cells derived from a single parental cell, making all cells within the clonal population genetically identical.
  • Growth Rate: Animal cells divide relatively slowly, requiring $18$ to $24$ hours to complete a single division cycle.
  • Contamination Vulnerability: Due to slow growth rates, animal cell cultures are highly vulnerable to bacterial or fungal contamination, as rapidly dividing microbes can easily outgrow the host animal cells.
8.1 Historical Perspective

Milestones and Landmark Discoveries

The establishment of animal cell culture as a routine laboratory technique accelerated in the 1950s with the creation of the first human cell line.

Historical Limitation: Early biological research was constrained by the finite lifespan of primary cells isolated from tissues, which underwent senescence and died after a limited number of divisions.

Discovery of Immortal Cells (HeLa): In 1951, George Gey established the first continuous human cell line, HeLa, derived from a cervical cancer biopsy of an African-American patient named Henrietta Lacks. These cells possessed the unique ability to divide indefinitely, providing a standardized, immortal model system that revolutionized polio vaccine testing, radiation research, and molecular genetics.

8.2 Culture Media

Media Composition and Classification

The selection of an appropriate growth medium is the most critical stage in cell culture, determined by the specific cell type and experimental objectives (e.g., growth, differentiation, or recombinant protein production).

  • Basal Components: A typical medium contains amino acids, vitamins, glucose (carbon source), inorganic salts, and hormones.
  • Natural Media: Composed of naturally occurring biological fluids (e.g., coagulants/clots, tissue extracts, plasma, serum, amniotic fluid). Disadvantaged by poor reproducibility and undefined composition.
  • Synthetic/Artificial Media: Prepared by adding specific organic and inorganic nutrients to a basal medium, categorized into serum-containing, serum-free, xeno-free, protein-free, and chemically defined media.

Media Supplements and the Role of Serum

Serum is a complex mixture of proteins, growth factors, hormones, and lipids that serves as a universal supplement for animal cell growth.

  • Functions of Serum: Provides essential amino acids, vitamins, lipids, and hormones; supplies transport proteins like albumin and transferrin; provides cell adhesion/attachment factors; acts as a pH buffer. Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) is the most widely used.
  • Disadvantages of Serum: High cost; batch-to-batch variability; risk of contamination with viruses, mycoplasma, or prions; contains growth-inhibiting factors; complicates downstream purification of biopharmaceuticals.
  • Antibiotics: Non-essential for growth but commonly added (e.g., penicillin and streptomycin) to control low-level bacterial and fungal contamination.
8.3 Physical Environment for Culturing Animal Cells

Physiological Parameters

To support optimal cell survival and proliferation, the physical environment must mimic the homeostatic conditions of the host organism.

  1. Temperature: Mammalian cells are cultured at $37^\circ\text{C}$ to simulate the core body temperature of $Homo\ sapiens$ and other warm-blooded animals.
  2. Osmolality: Maintained at approximately $300\text{ mOsmol}$ to preserve cell membrane integrity. Glucose and inorganic salts (primarily $NaCl$) are the major osmotic regulators.
  3. Buffering Systems: Crucial for maintaining physiological pH ($7.2\text{--}7.4$). Achieved via two primary systems:
    • Natural Buffering System: Relies on the equilibrium between gaseous carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) and bicarbonate ($HCO_3^-$) ions in the medium:$$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$$Requires a controlled incubator atmosphere containing $5\text{--}10\%\ \text{CO}_2$. Low-cost and non-toxic.
    • Chemical Buffering System: Utilizes zwitterionic buffers, primarily HEPES ($4\text{-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid}$), which has superior buffering capacity in the pH range $7.2\text{--}7.4$ without requiring a controlled $CO_2$ atmosphere. It is more expensive and can be toxic at high concentrations.
  4. Phenol Red: A pH indicator dye added to commercial media to visually monitor metabolic activity. At optimal pH ($7.4$), the medium is bright red; it turns yellow at acidic pH ($< 6.8$) due to lactic acid accumulation, and pink at alkaline pH ($> 8.2$) due to waste depletion or $CO_2$ loss.
8.4 Equipment Used for Cell Culture

Essential Laboratory Instrumentation

  • Laminar Flow Hood / Biosafety Cabinet: Provides an aseptic work area by passing air through a pre-filter and a HEPA (High Efficiency Particulate Air) filter.
    • Horizontal Flow Hood: Directs sterile air from the back of the cabinet forward toward the operator. Offers sample protection but no operator protection; unsuitable for handling hazardous or infectious agents.
    • Vertical Flow Hood: Directs sterile air downward from the top of the cabinet. Safer for the operator as it prevents air from blowing directly toward them, making it standard for biosafety cabinets.
  • $CO_2$ Incubator: Maintains a sterile environment with constant temperature ($37^\circ\text{C}$), controlled $CO_2$ levels ($5\text{--}10\%$), and high relative humidity ($\approx 95\%$) to prevent media evaporation.
  • Inverted Microscope: Features a light source and condenser located above the stage, with objectives positioned below. This configuration allows observation of cells growing at the bottom of thick culture flasks and roller bottles. Used to monitor cell morphology, confluence, and contamination.
8.5 Types of Animal Cell Cultures and Cell Lines

Primary vs. Secondary Cultures

  • Primary Cell Culture: Obtained directly by isolating cells from host tissue via mechanical or enzymatic disaggregation (using enzymes like trypsin or collagenase). These cells are heterogeneous, genetically representative of the parent tissue, but have a finite lifespan.
  • Secondary Cell Culture: Formed when a primary culture is sub-cultured (passaged) by transferring a portion of cells to a new vessel with fresh medium. This process selects for rapidly proliferating cells, leading to genotypic and phenotypic uniformity.

Adherent vs. Suspension Cells

  • Adherent Cells: Anchorage-dependent cells that require a solid or semi-solid substrate to attach and proliferate. They form a single-cell-thick layer known as a monolayer. Examples include fibroblasts and epithelial cells.
  • Suspension Cells: Anchorage-independent cells that do not require attachment and float freely in the culture medium. They grow faster, do not require enzymatic detachment during passaging, and reach higher densities. Examples include hematopoietic stem cells and leukemia cell lines.

Subculturing and Cryopreservation

When adherent cells reach confluence (covering the entire available substrate surface), they must be sub-cultured to prevent overcrowding and cell death.

  1. Trypsinization: The old medium is removed, cells are washed, and treated with trypsin + EDTA to cleave cell-adhesion molecules, causing cells to detach and round up.
  2. Neutralization and Centrifugation: Trypsin is neutralized with serum-containing medium, and the cell suspension is centrifuged to pellet the cells.
  3. Resuspension and Splitting: The cell pellet is resuspended in fresh medium and distributed into new culture vessels.
  4. Cryopreservation: Long-term storage of cells at ultra-low temperatures ($-180^\circ\text{C}$ to $-196^\circ\text{C}$) in liquid nitrogen. To prevent lethal intracellular ice crystal formation, a cryoprotective agent such as DMSO (Dimethyl sulfoxide) or glycerol is added to a freezing medium containing $90\%$ serum and $10\%$ DMSO. Cells are cooled slowly to $-80^\circ\text{C}$ before liquid nitrogen storage, and thawed rapidly at $37^\circ\text{C}$ when revived.
8.6 Cell Viability Determination

Assay Methodologies

Cell viability assays are performed to determine the proportion of living cells in a culture, evaluate drug/toxin efficacy, or monitor culture health.

  • Dye Exclusion Assays: Based on cell membrane integrity. Live cells with intact membranes exclude certain dyes, while dead cells with damaged membranes allow the dye to enter and stain intracellular structures.
    • Trypan Blue: Stains dead cells blue, leaving live cells clear and refractile under a microscope.
    • Other dyes: propidium iodide, 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD), and acridine orange.
  • Metabolic Viability Assays (MTT Assay): Based on the metabolic activity of viable cells. Viable cells contain active mitochondrial dehydrogenase enzymes that reduce the yellow, water-soluble tetrazolium salt MTT into purple, water-insoluble formazan crystals. These crystals are dissolved in DMSO, and the resulting color intensity (proportional to the number of living cells) is measured spectrophotometrically.
8.7 Advantages and Limitations of Animal Cell Culture

Comparative Analysis

  • Advantages:
    • Enables precise control of the physico-chemical microenvironment (pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen, osmotic pressure).
    • Provides a homogenous genetic population (clones) for highly reproducible experiments.
    • Allows direct testing of chemical compounds, drugs, and pathogens without systemic physiological interference.
    • Facilitates the cost-effective, large-scale production of complex biopharmaceuticals.
    • Bypasses the ethical clearances required for in vivo animal testing.
  • Limitations:
    • Highly sensitive technique requiring strict aseptic conditions; minor deviations can lead to complete culture loss.
    • High operational costs associated with specialized media, reagents, and sterile instrumentation.
    • Isolated cells may undergo phenotypic drift and lose tissue-specific characteristics, failing to fully represent in vivo physiology.
8.8 Applications of Animal Cell Culture

Biomedical and Industrial Applications

Animal cell culture serves as a foundational platform for modern biotechnology, virology, and pharmaceutical manufacturing.

  • Model Systems: Used to study intracellular interactions, cellular signaling, drug toxicity, and the mechanisms of disease progression.
  • Virology and Vaccine Production: Serves as host systems for propagating viruses to manufacture viral vaccines (e.g., polio, rabies, measles, and influenza vaccines).
  • Recombinant Protein Pharmaceuticals: Mammalian cells (especially CHO (Chinese Hamster Ovary) cells) are preferred for producing complex therapeutic proteins because they perform correct post-translational modifications (such as glycosylation) essential for protein activity and stability.

Wild-Type Molecular Limitation: Traditional thrombolytic treatments relied on systemic anticoagulants or bacterial enzymes (like streptokinase) that carried high risks of systemic bleeding and immunogenic reactions.

Engineering Methodology & Recombinant Production of tPA: Tissue Plasminogen Activator (tPA) was the first therapeutic drug produced via mammalian cell culture. The human tPA gene is inserted into a plasmid vector and transfected into CHO cells. The recombinant cells are scaled up in large-scale bioreactors/fermenters. The secreted tPA—a serine protease—is harvested and purified. It selectively binds to fibrin in blood clots and catalyzes the conversion of inactive plasminogen to active plasmin, dissolving the clot locally and minimizing systemic side effects.

  • Therapeutic MAB - OKT3: Muromonab-CD3 (OKT-3) is a murine monoclonal antibody directed against the CD3 receptor on T-cells. It acts as an immunosuppressant by inducing TCR endocytosis, rendering T-cells inert and preventing acute graft rejection in kidney, heart, and liver transplants.
  • Trastuzumab (Herceptin): A monoclonal antibody designed to target the HER2 (Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2) receptor, which is overexpressed in aggressive forms of breast cancer. Trastuzumab binds to HER2, blocking growth signals and inhibiting tumor proliferation.
