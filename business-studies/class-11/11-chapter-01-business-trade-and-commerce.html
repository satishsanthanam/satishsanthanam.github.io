Business Studies: Class 11 — Chapter 1: Business, Trade and Commerce

1.1 Introduction & Historical Background of Commerce

Business is a major economic activity concerned with the production and sale of goods and services required by people. The roots of business activities in India trace back to ancient times, where maritime routes and the Silk Route established the subcontinent as a global commercial hub, often referred to as 'Swaran Bharat' or 'Swaran Dweep'.

Indigenous Banking System & Hundi

As economic life progressed, metals began to be used as money, leading to the development of metallic currency and documents like Hundi and Chitties to facilitate credit transactions and safe transfer of money across long distances.

Major Trade Centres in Ancient India

  • Pataliputra: Major centre for export of stones and commercial transactions.
  • Peshawar: Important exporting centre for wool and importing horses.
  • Taxila: Financial and commercial banking hub on the land route to Central Asia.
  • Indraprastha: Commercial junction where major royal highways converged.
  • Varanasi: Major centre of the textile industry, famous for gold silk cloth and sandalwood.
  • Surat: Emporium of western trade; Surat hundi was honoured in far-off markets like Egypt.
  • Kaveripatta: Scientific port city convenient for trade with Malaysia, Indonesia, and the Far East.
1.2 Concept and Characteristics of Business

Human activities are broadly classified into Economic Activities (undertaken to earn a livelihood) and Non-Economic Activities (performed out of love, sympathy, or sentiment).

Characteristics of Business Activities

  • An economic activity: Undertaken with the objective of earning money or livelihood.
  • Production or procurement of goods and services: Goods must be produced or acquired before consumption.
  • Sale or exchange of goods and services: Involves transfer of ownership for value (personal consumption is not business).
  • Dealings on a regular basis: One single transaction of sale or purchase does not constitute business.
  • Profit earning: The primary purpose of business is to earn income by way of profit.
  • Uncertainty of return: Lack of knowledge relating to the amount of money the business will earn.
  • Element of risk: Exposure to loss caused by unfavorable or undesirable events.

1.3 Classification of Business Activities

Business activities are broadly classified into Industry and Commerce.

1.3.1 Industry

Industry refers to economic activities connected with the conversion of resources into useful goods.

  • Primary Industries: Extraction and production of natural resources and reproduction of living organisms.
    • Extractive Industries: Farming, mining, lumbering, fishing.
    • Genetic Industries: Breeding plants and animals, poultry farms, nurseries.
  • Secondary Industries: Processing materials extracted at the primary stage to produce goods for final consumption.
    • Manufacturing Industries: Analytical (oil refinery), Synthetical (cement), Processing (sugar/paper), Assembling (television/cars).
    • Construction Industries: Buildings, dams, bridges, roads.
  • Tertiary Industries: Support services to primary and secondary industries (transport, banking, insurance, warehousing, communication).

1.3.2 Commerce & Auxiliaries to Trade

Commerce includes Trade (buying and selling of goods) and Auxiliaries to Trade (activities that facilitate trade by removing hindrances of person, place, time, risk, finance, and information).

  • Transport and Communication: Removes the hindrance of place by moving goods from production sites to markets.
  • Banking and Finance: Removes the hindrance of finance by providing necessary capital and credit facilities.
  • Insurance: Removes the hindrance of risk by providing protection against fire, theft, and damage.
  • Warehousing: Removes the hindrance of time by storing goods until they are demanded.
  • Advertising: Removes the hindrance of information by educating consumers about available products.
1.4 Objectives of Business

While earning profit is a leading objective, a business must pursue multiple objectives to survive and prosper in the long run.

  • Market standing: Securing a strong position in relation to competitors by offering quality products.
  • Innovation: Developing new products, services, or operational skills to maintain a competitive edge.
  • Productivity: Ensuring efficiency by comparing the value of output with the value of inputs.
  • Physical and financial resources: Acquiring and efficiently utilizing plants, machines, and funds.
  • Earning profits: Generating a reasonable return on capital employed to ensure survival and growth.
  • Social responsibility: Contributing resources to solve social problems and operating in a socially desirable manner.
1.5 Business Risk

Business Risk refers to the possibility of inadequate profits or even losses due to uncertainties or unexpected events.

Nature of Business Risks

  • Risk is an essential part of every business and cannot be completely eliminated.
  • Business risks arise due to uncertainties (e.g., natural calamities, policy changes).
  • The degree of risk depends mainly upon the nature and size of the business.
  • Profit is the reward for risk-taking ("No risk, no gain").

Causes of Business Risks

  • Natural causes: Floods, earthquakes, lightning, heavy rains, famine.
  • Human causes: Dishonesty, carelessness, strikes, power failures, management inefficiency.
  • Economic causes: Fluctuations in demand and supply, competition, price changes, technological shifts.
  • Other causes: Political disturbances, mechanical failures (e.g., boiler bursting), exchange rate fluctuations.
1.6 Starting a Business — Basic Factors

Setting up a business enterprise requires careful consideration of several fundamental factors:

  1. Selection of line of business: Choosing the nature and type of business based on market demand and technical knowledge.
  2. Size of firm: Deciding whether to operate on a large scale or start as an MSME based on capital and demand.
  3. Choice of form of ownership: Selecting sole proprietorship, partnership, or a joint-stock company.
  4. Location of business enterprise: Choosing a site based on availability of raw materials, labor, power, and transport.
  5. Financing the proposition: Planning capital requirements for fixed assets (land, machinery) and working capital.
  6. Physical facilities: Arranging machines, equipment, and building infrastructure.
  7. Competent and committed workforce: Identifying and hiring skilled and unskilled workers and managerial staff.
  8. Tax planning: Analyzing tax liabilities under various laws and their impact on business decisions.
  9. Launching the enterprise: Mobilizing resources, fulfilling legal formalities, and starting the production process.
1.7 Business Case Study

Business Case Title: Dipak Bharali and the 'Chaneki' Innovation

Corporate Context/Problem: In Sualkuchi, Assam, the slow and mentally/physically exhausting process of manual weft insertion (buta weaving) limited the productivity of silk weavers, preventing them from taking on extra work or expanding their business without buying expensive additional looms.

Strategic Action Taken: Dipak Bharali, a local weaver, designed and developed an eco-friendly, automated device called Chaneki made of recyclable materials after 80 failed attempts, which automated the weft insertion process.

Managerial Inference: Innovation and risk-taking are central to business growth. By introducing new methods, a business can dramatically increase productivity (Chaneki runs 40 times faster than manual weaving) and achieve sustainable development while satisfying multiple objectives.
