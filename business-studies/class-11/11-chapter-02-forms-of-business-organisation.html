Business Studies: Class 11 — Chapter 2: Forms of Business Organisation

2.1 Introduction

When starting or expanding a business, choosing the right form of organisation is a critical decision. This choice requires weighing the advantages and disadvantages of each form against individual requirements. The primary forms of business organisation include:

  • Sole proprietorship
  • Joint Hindu family business
  • Partnership
  • Cooperative societies
  • Joint stock company
2.2 Sole Proprietorship

Sole proprietorship refers to a form of business organisation which is owned, managed, and controlled by a single individual who receives all profits and bears all risks. It is the simplest and most common form for small-scale businesses.

Features

  • Formation and closure: Easy to start and close as there is no separate governing law; minimal legal formalities are required.
  • Liability: The sole proprietor has unlimited liability, meaning personal assets can be used to repay business debts if business assets are insufficient.
  • Sole risk bearer and profit recipient: The owner bears the entire risk of failure and enjoys all the profits of success.
  • Control: Absolute decision-making power lies with the sole proprietor.
  • No separate entity: In the eyes of the law, no distinction is made between the sole trader and their business.
  • Lack of business continuity: Death, insanity, imprisonment, physical ailment, or bankruptcy of the owner can directly lead to closure.

Merits

  • Quick decision making: Freedom from consulting others allows prompt actions.
  • Confidentiality of information: Sole decision-making authority helps maintain complete business secrecy.
  • Direct incentive: The owner directly reaps the benefits of their efforts without sharing profits.
  • Sense of accomplishment: Working for oneself instils personal satisfaction and confidence.
  • Ease of formation and closure: Minimal legal regulations make it easy to enter and exit.

Limitations

  • Limited resources: Capital is restricted to personal savings and limited borrowing capacity.
  • Limited life of a business concern: The business is highly vulnerable to the personal circumstances of the owner.
  • Unlimited liability: High personal financial risk due to the lack of legal separation between owner and business.
  • Limited managerial ability: A single individual rarely excels in all functional areas of management (purchasing, sales, finance, etc.).
Business Case Title: Coca-Cola's Sole Proprietor Origins
Corporate Context/Problem: Dr. John Stith Pemberton, a local pharmacist in Atlanta, created the syrup for Coca-Cola in 1886 but did not realise its massive commercial potential, gradually selling off parts of his business.
Strategic Action Taken: Asa G. Candler, an entrepreneur with great business acumen, bought additional rights to acquire complete control, proclaiming his drug business as the "sole proprietors of Coca-Cola" in 1889 before eventually forming a corporation in 1892.
Managerial Inference: While sole proprietorship is excellent for initial creation and local testing, scaling a massive global brand eventually requires transitioning to a corporate structure to pool capital and resources.
2.3 Joint Hindu Family Business

Joint Hindu family business is a specific form of business organisation found only in India, wherein the business is owned and carried on by the members of a Hindu Undivided Family (HUF). It is governed by Hindu Law.

Features

  • Formation: Requires at least two family members and ancestral property to be inherited by them. Membership is by birth.
  • Liability: The liability of all members (co-parceners) is limited to their share in the family property, except for the Karta (the head of the family), who has unlimited liability.
  • Control: The Karta exercises absolute control and decision-making power over the business.
  • Continuity: The business continues even after the death of the Karta, as the next eldest member automatically takes over.
  • Minor Members: Minors can be members of the business by virtue of their birth in the family.

Merits & Limitations

Merits:

  • Effective control by the Karta avoids internal conflicts.
  • Continued business existence ensures stability.
  • Limited liability for co-parceners reduces individual risk.
  • Increased loyalty and cooperation among family members.

Limitations:

  • Limited resources as capital depends mainly on ancestral property.
  • Unlimited liability of the Karta puts their personal assets at risk.
  • Dominance of the Karta can cause family conflicts.
  • Limited managerial skills of a single Karta.
2.4 Partnership

A partnership is defined by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, as "the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profit of the business carried on by all or any of them acting for all."

Features

  • Formation: Governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932; arises from a legal agreement (oral or written) to carry on a lawful business for profit.
  • Liability: Partners have joint and several unlimited liability.
  • Risk bearing: Partners share both the risks and the profits in an agreed ratio.
  • Decision making and control: Shared responsibility; decisions are generally taken by mutual consent.
  • Continuity: Lack of continuity; death, retirement, insolvency, or insanity of any partner can dissolve the firm.
  • Number of Partners: Minimum of 2; maximum is currently restricted to 50 (as per Companies Rules, 2014).
  • Mutual agency: Every partner is both an agent (binding others by their acts) and a principal (bound by the acts of other partners).

Types of Partnerships

  • Classification on the basis of duration:
    • Partnership at will: Continues as long as the partners want; terminated by notice.
    • Particular partnership: Formed for a specific project or time period; dissolves automatically upon completion.
  • Classification on the basis of liability:
    • General Partnership: Unlimited and joint liability of all partners.
    • Limited Partnership: Liability of at least one partner is unlimited, while others have limited liability.

Partnership Deed & Registration

The written agreement which specifies the terms and conditions governing the partnership is called the Partnership Deed. Registration of a partnership firm is optional but highly recommended due to the consequences of non-registration (e.g., inability to file suits against third parties or other partners).

Registration Procedure:

  1. Submission of application in the prescribed form to the Registrar of Firms.
  2. Deposit of the required registration fees.
  3. Entry by the Registrar in the register of firms and issuance of a Certificate of Registration.
2.5 Cooperative Society

A cooperative society is a voluntary association of persons who join together with the motive of welfare of the members and to protect their economic interests. Registration is compulsory under the Cooperative Societies Act.

Features

  • Voluntary membership: Open to all irrespective of religion, caste, or gender; members can join or leave at will.
  • Legal status: Compulsory registration grants it a separate legal identity distinct from its members.
  • Limited liability: Limited to the extent of the capital contributed by each member.
  • Control: Managed democratically by an elected managing committee on the principle of "one man, one vote".
  • Service motive: Primary focus is mutual help and service, rather than profit maximisation.

Types of Cooperative Societies

  • Consumer's cooperative societies: Formed to provide consumer goods at reasonable prices by eliminating middlemen.
  • Producer's cooperative societies: Protects small producers by providing raw materials and inputs.
  • Marketing cooperative societies: Helps small producers sell their outputs at favourable prices.
  • Farmer's cooperative societies: Provides farmers with better inputs (seeds, fertilisers, machinery) to improve productivity.
  • Credit cooperative societies: Provides easy credit and loans at reasonable interest rates to members.
  • Cooperative housing societies: Helps members construct or procure residential accommodation at lower costs.
Business Case Title: Amul's Cooperative Success
Corporate Context/Problem: Small dairy farmers in Anand, Gujarat, were exploited by monopolistic middlemen and distributors, receiving poor returns for their milk.
Strategic Action Taken: In 1946, farmers formed the Khera District Milk Cooperative Union (Amul), pooling milk directly, processing it, and marketing it collectively under a unified brand.
Managerial Inference: Cooperative structures can successfully scale to compete with large corporations while ensuring equitable distribution of profits back to primary producers.
2.6 Joint Stock Company

A joint stock company is an association of persons formed for carrying out business activities, having a separate legal identity, perpetual succession, and a common seal. It is governed by the Companies Act, 2013.

Features

  • Artificial person: Created by law; can own property, incur debts, and enter contracts, but has no physical existence.
  • Separate legal entity: Distinct from its members; assets and liabilities belong to the company, not the owners.
  • Formation: A highly complex, expensive, and time-consuming legal process.
  • Perpetual succession: Continuous existence unaffected by the death, retirement, or insolvency of its members.
  • Control: Managed by an elected Board of Directors, separating ownership (shareholders) from management.
  • Liability: Limited to the unpaid amount of shares held by each member.

2.7 Choice of Form of Business Organisation

The choice of an appropriate business form depends on several interrelated factors:

  1. Cost and ease of setting up: Sole proprietorship is the cheapest and easiest; companies are the most complex and expensive.
  2. Liability: Companies and cooperatives offer limited liability, whereas sole proprietorships and partnerships involve unlimited liability.
  3. Continuity: Companies and cooperatives offer stable, perpetual existence.
  4. Management ability: Companies can afford professional, specialised management, unlike sole proprietorships.
  5. Capital considerations: Companies are best suited for raising large amounts of capital.
  6. Degree of control: Sole proprietorship offers direct, absolute control; companies involve shared, indirect control.
  7. Nature of business: Direct customer contact businesses suit sole proprietorships; large-scale manufacturing suits companies.
