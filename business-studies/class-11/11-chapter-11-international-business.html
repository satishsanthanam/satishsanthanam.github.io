Business Studies: Class 11 — Chapter 11: International Business

Introduction & Meaning of International Business

In the modern era, national economies are shifting away from complete self-reliance toward mutual interdependence. Driven by rapid advancements in communication, technology, and transportation infrastructure, the world has transformed into a "global village" where cross-border trade and investment are standard business practices.

Business Case Title: Sudhir Manchanda's Global Expansion Dilemma

Corporate Context/Problem: Mr. Sudhir Manchanda, a small-scale manufacturer of automobile components in Gurgaon, faces a domestic market recession. While some competitors are already exporting, he is uncertain about the complexities of international trade and how to enter overseas markets in South-East Asia and the Middle East.

Strategic Action Taken: He evaluates multiple entry strategies, including direct exporting, routing products through specialized export houses, or establishing a fully owned manufacturing plant in Bangkok as suggested by his MBA-graduate son to save transportation costs.

Managerial Inference: Transitioning from domestic to international business introduces environmental complexities (political, socio-cultural, and economic) that require managers to carefully analyze risks, control requirements, and resource commitments before selecting an entry mode.

Defining International Business

International Business refers to business activities that take place across national frontiers. It is a much broader term than international trade, as it encompasses not only the movement of goods and services, but also the cross-border flow of capital, personnel, technology, and intellectual property (such as patents, trademarks, and copyrights).

International Business vs. Domestic Business

Conducting business operations across national borders is significantly more complex than operating within a single country due to environmental variations. The key differences are summarized below:

Basis of Comparison Domestic Business International Business
Nationality of buyers and sellers Participants are from the same country, making mutual understanding easier. Participants are from different countries, introducing language and cultural barriers.
Nationality of other stakeholders Stakeholders (employees, suppliers, shareholders) share a relatively consistent value system. Stakeholders belong to different nations, requiring a wider set of values to be managed.
Mobility of factors of production Factors like labor and capital move relatively freely within the country. Mobility is restricted by legal, socio-cultural, and geographic barriers across nations.
Customer heterogeneity Markets are relatively homogeneous in tastes, preferences, and behaviors. Markets lack homogeneity due to diverse socio-cultural backgrounds and customs.
Business systems and practices Systems and practices are uniform and consistent within the nation. Systems, infrastructure, and customs vary considerably from country to country.
Political system and risks Subject to the political system and risks of a single nation. Subject to diverse political ideologies, systems, and cross-border political risks.
Business regulations and policies Governed by a single, uniform set of domestic laws and taxation policies. Governed by multiple sets of laws, tariffs, quotas, and regulations of different nations.
Currency used Involves the use of a single domestic currency. Involves multiple currencies, exposing firms to fluctuating exchange rates.
Reasons for and Scope of International Business

Fundamental Reasons for International Trade

The primary driver of international business is that no single country can produce all its required goods and services equally well or cheaply. This is due to:

  • Unequal distribution of natural resources among nations.
  • Differences in productivity levels and labor costs.
  • Varying availability of production factors like capital, raw materials, and technology.

Consequently, countries specialize in producing goods where they hold a comparative advantage and trade their surpluses to procure other necessities.

Scope of International Business

The scope of international operations extends across four major areas:

  • Merchandise Exports and Imports: Trade in tangible, physical goods (also known as trade in goods).
  • Service Exports and Imports: Trade in intangibles (also known as invisible trade), which includes tourism, transportation, banking, insurance, and professional consulting.
  • Licensing and Franchising: Contractual permissions granted to foreign entities to use trademarks, patents, or technology in exchange for a fee (Royalty).
  • Foreign Investments: Financial investments made abroad, divided into:
    • Foreign Direct Investment (FDI): Direct investment in physical assets like plants and machinery, providing controlling interest.
    • Portfolio Investment: Acquisition of shares or provision of loans to foreign companies without direct operational control.
Benefits of International Business

Benefits to Countries
  • Earning of Foreign Exchange: Enables countries to import critical capital goods, technology, petroleum, and pharmaceuticals.
  • Efficient Resource Utilization: Promotes specialization, allowing nations to produce what they make most efficiently, maximizing global output.
  • Growth and Employment: Export-led growth strategies (e.g., Singapore, South Korea) help scale up production and generate employment.
  • Higher Standard of Living: Allows citizens to consume diverse goods and services produced globally, improving quality of life.

Benefits to Firms
  • Prospects for Higher Profits: International markets often offer higher prices and profit margins than saturated domestic markets.
  • Increased Capacity Utilization: Firms can utilize surplus production capacity by catering to overseas demand, achieving economies of scale.
  • Prospects for Growth: Offers a path to expansion when domestic demand becomes saturated.
  • Mitigating Domestic Competition: Serves as a strategic buffer when domestic competition becomes highly intense.
  • Improved Business Vision: Enhances competitiveness, diversification, and strategic positioning on a global scale.
Modes of Entry into International Business

1. Exporting and Importing

The easiest and most common entry mode, involving the physical movement of goods across borders. Can be direct (handling all formalities internally) or indirect (using middlemen like export houses).

  • Advantages: Low complexity, minimal financial investment, and zero or low foreign investment risk.
  • Limitations: High transportation and packaging costs, vulnerability to import tariffs/quotas, and lack of direct contact with foreign customers.

2. Contract Manufacturing

Also known as Outsourcing, this involves contracting with local manufacturers in foreign countries to produce components or complete products according to specifications.

  • Advantages: Large-scale production without capital investment in facilities, minimal investment risk, and access to lower labor/material costs.
  • Limitations: Risk of quality deviations by local firms, loss of operational control by the local manufacturer, and restrictions on selling the output independently.

3. Licensing and Franchising

Licensing is a contractual agreement where a licensor grants a foreign licensee access to patents, trade secrets, or technology for a royalty fee. Franchising is a similar but more stringent arrangement used in service industries (e.g., fast food chains) where the franchiser sets strict operational rules.

  • Advantages: Extremely low cost of entry, zero exposure to foreign business losses, and utilization of local market expertise.
  • Limitations: Risk of the licensee becoming a competitor, potential leakage of trade secrets, and frequent conflicts over royalties or quality standards.

4. Joint Ventures

Establishing a business enterprise that is jointly owned by two or more independent firms (e.g., a foreign investor partnering with a local entrepreneur).

  • Advantages: Shared financial burden, capability to execute massive projects, and access to the local partner's cultural, legal, and market knowledge.
  • Limitations: Risk of disclosing proprietary technology/secrets, and potential conflicts over management control and strategic direction.

5. Wholly Owned Subsidiaries

The parent company exercises 100% control over foreign operations by investing entirely in its equity. This can be done via a greenfield venture (setting up a new plant) or acquisition of an existing foreign firm.

  • Advantages: Complete operational control and absolute protection of technology and trade secrets.
  • Limitations: Requires massive capital investment (unsuitable for small/medium firms), high risk of bearing all losses, and exposure to political risks.
Export-Import Procedures and Documentation

The Export Procedure

Exporting involves a structured sequence of regulatory and logistical steps:

  1. Receipt of Enquiry and Sending Quotations: Exporter responds to an importer's inquiry with a Proforma Invoice detailing price, quality, and terms.
  2. Receipt of Order or Indent: Importer places an order (Indent) containing product descriptions, shipping instructions, and price.
  3. Assessing Importer's Creditworthiness: Exporter verifies the importer's financial standing and secures a Letter of Credit from the importer's bank.
  4. Obtaining Export Licence: Exporter registers with the RBI, obtains an Import Export Code (IEC) from the DGFT, and registers with Export Promotion Councils.
  5. Obtaining Pre-shipment Finance: Securing working capital from banks to procure raw materials and process goods.
  6. Production or Procurement of Goods: Manufacturing or purchasing goods as per the importer's specifications.
  7. Pre-shipment Inspection: Ensuring quality compliance through designated government inspection agencies.
  8. Excise Clearance: Obtaining clearance under excise laws; exporters may receive a refund known as Duty Drawback.
  9. Obtaining Certificate of Origin: Securing proof of manufacture from trade consulates to help the importer claim tariff concessions.
  10. Reservation of Shipping Space: Applying to a shipping company to secure cargo space, resulting in a Shipping Order.
  11. Packing and Forwarding: Packing, marking, and transporting goods to the port, obtaining a railway receipt if applicable.
  12. Insurance of Goods: Securing marine insurance to protect against transit risks.
  13. Customs Clearance: Submitting a Shipping Bill and other documents to customs authorities for clearance.
  14. Obtaining Mate's Receipt: The ship's commanding officer issues a Mate's Receipt once cargo is loaded.
  15. Payment of Freight and Issuance of Bill of Lading: Exchanging the Mate's Receipt for a Bill of Lading (or Airway Bill) from the shipping company.
  16. Preparation of Invoice: Generating the final commercial invoice attested by customs.
  17. Securing Payment: Sending documents (including a Bill of Exchange) through banks to secure payment via sight draft or usance draft.

The Import Procedure

Importing requires systematic coordination with foreign suppliers and domestic customs:

  1. Trade Enquiry: Gathering information on foreign suppliers and requesting quotations.
  2. Procurement of Import Licence: Checking EXIM policy and obtaining an IEC number.
  3. Obtaining Foreign Exchange: Applying to an authorized bank to secure foreign exchange sanctioned by the RBI.
  4. Placing Order or Indent: Sending a formal purchase order with detailed specifications.
  5. Obtaining Letter of Credit: Getting a bank guarantee to secure payment for the exporter.
  6. Arranging for Finance: Planning funds to pay for the goods upon arrival to avoid demurrage penalties.
  7. Receipt of Shipment Advice: Receiving details of the vessel, invoice number, and dispatch date from the exporter.
  8. Retirement of Import Documents: Accepting the Bill of Exchange to receive the title documents from the bank.
  9. Arrival of Goods: The carrier submits an Import General Manifest to unload the cargo.
  10. Customs Clearance and Release of Goods: Paying dock dues, submitting a Bill of Entry, paying import duties, and obtaining a release order.
