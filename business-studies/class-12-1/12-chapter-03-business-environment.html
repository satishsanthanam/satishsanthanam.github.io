Business Studies: Class 12 (Part 1) — Chapter 3: Business Environment

Introduction & Case Study

Business Case Title: Dharamveer Kamboj's Multipurpose Processing Machine

Corporate Context/Problem: Manual grating of gooseberries by women self-help groups in Rajasthan was painful and inefficient. Existing processing machines were not cost-effective for small-scale, modest-means owners.

Strategic Action Taken: Dharamveer, despite limited formal education, designed and developed a cost-effective, portable, multipurpose processing machine that could cut, grate, juice, and pulverize without damaging seeds.

Managerial Inference: Understanding and responding to the needs of the external environment (social pain points, economic constraints of small industries) can lead to successful technological innovation and entrepreneurship.

Meaning and Features of Business Environment

The Business Environment refers to the sum total of all individuals, institutions, and other forces that are outside the control of a business enterprise but may affect its performance.

Key Features

  • Totality of external forces: It is aggregative in nature as it includes all external factors.
  • Specific and general forces: Specific forces (investors, customers, competitors) affect enterprises directly, while general forces (social, political, legal, technological) affect them indirectly.
  • Inter-relatedness: Different elements are closely co-dependent (e.g., health awareness increasing demand for organic food).
  • Dynamic nature: It keeps changing constantly due to technological shifts or consumer preferences.
  • Uncertainty: Highly unpredictable, especially in fast-moving sectors like IT and fashion.
  • Complexity: Easier to understand in parts but difficult to grasp in its totality.
  • Relativity: Differs from country to country and region to region.

Importance of Business Environment

Understanding the environment enables managers to take proactive measures rather than reactive ones.

  • First-Mover Advantage: Identifying opportunities early to exploit them before competitors (e.g., Maruti Suzuki recognizing the demand for small cars).
  • Early Warning Signals: Identifying threats to prepare defensive strategies (e.g., local firms preparing for multinational entry).
  • Tapping Useful Resources: Assembling inputs (finance, raw materials, labor) that the environment provides and outputting what it desires.
  • Coping with Rapid Changes: Navigating turbulent market conditions, intense global competition, and rapid technological shifts.
  • Planning and Policy Formulation: Serving as a foundation for deciding future courses of action.
  • Improving Performance: Continuous monitoring leads to sustained market success and growth.
Dimensions of Business Environment

The general environment consists of five key dimensions:

1. Economic Environment

Includes factors like interest rates, inflation rates, changes in disposable income, stock market indices, and the value of the rupee.

2. Social Environment

Consists of social forces like customs, traditions, values, social trends, and societal expectations (e.g., health and fitness trends boosting organic food demand).

3. Technological Environment

Includes forces relating to scientific improvements and innovations that provide new ways of producing goods and services (e.g., online railway ticket booking).

4. Political Environment

Includes political conditions such as general stability, peace, and the specific attitudes of the elected government towards business.

5. Legal Environment

Consists of legislations passed by the government, administrative orders, court judgments, and decisions of various commissions at all levels of government.

Economic Reforms in India & 1991 Policy

In July 1991, India launched major economic reforms to address a severe balance of payments crisis, high inflation, and fiscal deficit.

The LPG Framework

  • Liberalisation: Liberating Indian industry from unnecessary controls, licensing requirements, and restrictions (ending the 'Licence Raj').
  • Privatisation: Reducing the role of the public sector and giving a greater role to the private sector through disinvestment and transfer of ownership.
  • Globalisation: Integrating the domestic economy with the global economy, allowing free flow of goods, services, capital, and technology across borders.
Demonetisation

On November 8, 2016, the Government of India declared the two largest denomination notes (Rs. 500 and Rs. 1,000) invalid as legal tender.

Key Features & Impact

  • Tax Administration Measure: Forced declaration of unaccounted cash and payment of taxes/penalties.
  • Channelizing Savings: Shifted cash into the formal financial system, increasing bank deposits.
  • Less-Cash Economy: Promoted digital transactions and financial savings.
