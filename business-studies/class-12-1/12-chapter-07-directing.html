Class 12 (Part 1) — Chapter 7: Directing

Introduction & Meaning of Directing

Business Case Title: Grassroot Leadership - Ford Motor Co.

Corporate Context/Problem: Ford Motor Company needed a cultural overhaul to strengthen its automotive business, improve operational fitness, and accelerate a strategic shift to capitalize on emerging opportunities. While it excelled at nurturing capable managers, it struggled to develop change agents and leaders.

Strategic Action Taken: Ford embarked on a sweeping attempt to mass-manufacture leaders, building an army of "warrior-entrepreneurs" with the courage to challenge old ideas. CEO Jim Hackett restructured the senior management team to lead the business toward becoming a trusted mobility company.

Managerial Inference: Grassroot leadership is the best vehicle for creating a successful, adaptable, and profitable business enterprise.

Meaning of Directing

Directing refers to the process of instructing, guiding, counselling, motivating and leading people in the organisation to achieve its objectives. It is not a mere issue of communication but encompasses key elements like supervision, motivation, and leadership.

Characteristics of Directing

  • Directing initiates action: While other functions prepare a setting for action, directing initiates action in the organisation.
  • Directing takes place at every level of management: Every manager, from top executive to supervisor, performs the function of directing wherever superior-subordinate relations exist.
  • Directing is a continuous process: It takes place throughout the life of the organisation, irrespective of changes in managers.
  • Directing flows from top to bottom: It is initiated at the top level and flows to the bottom through the organisational hierarchy.
Importance & Principles of Directing

Importance of Directing

  • Initiates Action: Helps individuals start working towards desired objectives when guided by superiors.
  • Integrates Employees' Efforts: Ensures that individual efforts contribute to collective organisational performance.
  • Guides Employees: Helps employees fully realise their potential and capabilities through motivation and leadership.
  • Facilitates Introduction of Changes: Reduces resistance to change by convincing employees of the benefits of transitions.
  • Brings Stability and Balance: Fosters cooperation and commitment, helping to balance various groups and departments.

Principles of Directing

  • Maximum individual contribution: Directing techniques should help employees contribute to their maximum potential.
  • Harmony of objectives: Directing must resolve conflicts between individual goals and organisational objectives.
  • Unity of Command: A subordinate should receive instructions from only one superior to avoid confusion and conflict.
  • Appropriateness of direction technique: Appropriate motivational and leadership techniques should be used based on situational variables.
  • Managerial communication: Clear instructions and effective feedback systems ensure total understanding.
  • Use of informal organisation: Managers should spot and utilize informal groups for effective directing.
  • Leadership: Managers must exercise good leadership to influence subordinates positively.
  • Follow through: Managers must continuously review whether orders are being implemented properly.
Elements of Directing & Supervision

The process of directing involves four key elements: Supervision, Motivation, Leadership, and Communication.

Supervision

Supervision can be understood as an element of directing (overseeing subordinates' work) and as a managerial position at the operative level (immediately above the workers).

Importance of Supervision:

  • Maintains day-to-day contact and friendly relations with workers, acting as a guide, friend, and philosopher.
  • Acts as a link between workers and management, conveying ideas upward and decisions downward.
  • Maintains group unity and resolves internal differences among workers.
  • Ensures performance of work according to set targets.
  • Provides on-the-job training to workers.
  • Builds high morale among workers through supervisory leadership.
  • Analyses work performed and provides constructive feedback.
Motivation

Motivation means the process of making subordinates act in a desired manner to achieve certain organisational goals. It relies on three terms: Motive (inner state that energises behaviour), Motivation (the process of stimulating action), and Motivators (techniques used to influence people, like pay or praise).

Business Case Title: Directing a Dissatisfied Manager (Rashmi Joshi)

Corporate Context/Problem: Rashmi Joshi, an ambitious district sales manager at Fine Productions, was bypassed for a promotion to marketing manager due to concerns about her teamwork and tendency to take sole credit. Consequently, she became despondent, her work deteriorated, and she delayed sales reports.

Strategic Action Taken: The newly appointed marketing manager faced the challenge of identifying how to motivate and inspire Rashmi to restore her former high level of performance without relying solely on formal authority.

Managerial Inference: Formal authority alone cannot extract the best work; managers must understand human needs and apply appropriate motivational strategies to handle dissatisfied employees.

Features of Motivation

  • It is an internal feeling arising from individual needs and desires.
  • It produces goal-directed behaviour.
  • It can be either positive (rewards, promotion) or negative (punishment, threats).
  • It is a complex process because individuals are heterogeneous with varied expectations.

The Motivation Process

  1. Unsatisfied Need: An individual experiences a deficiency or desire.
  2. Tension: The unsatisfied need creates restlessness.
  3. Drives: Restlessness stimulates action-oriented urges.
  4. Search Behaviour: The individual looks for ways to satisfy the need.
  5. Satisfied Need: The need is met.
  6. Reduction of Tension: The individual is relieved of restlessness.

Business Case Title: Motivating Employees in Tata Steel

Corporate Context/Problem: Tata Steel needed to maintain high motivation levels, foster innovation, and build a robust leadership pipeline to remain a low-cost, world-class steel producer.

Strategic Action Taken: The company introduced personal development programmes, performance-linked compensation, quality circles, and transparent multi-path communication channels (like 'MD Online'). It actively promoted the freedom to work, innovate, and fail.

Managerial Inference: Comprehensive motivational systems and participatory management build a highly focused team, leading to industry leadership and organizational excellence.

Maslow's Need Hierarchy Theory

Abraham Maslow's theory is based on a hierarchy of five human needs:

  1. Basic Physiological Needs: Primary needs like hunger, thirst, and shelter. In organisations, basic salary satisfies these.
  2. Safety/Security Needs: Protection from physical and emotional harm (e.g., job security, pension plans).
  3. Affiliation/Belonging Needs: Affection, acceptance, and friendship.
  4. Esteem Needs: Self-respect, autonomy, status, and recognition.
  5. Self-Actualisation Needs: The drive to become what one is capable of becoming (growth, self-fulfillment).

Financial and Non-Financial Incentives

Incentives are measures used to motivate employees to improve performance.

Financial Incentives (Direct monetary benefits):

  • Pay and allowances: Basic salary, dearness allowance, and increments.
  • Productivity linked wage incentives: Linking payment of wages to increase in productivity.
  • Bonus: Incentive offered over and above the regular salary.
  • Profit Sharing: Providing a share to employees in the profits of the organisation.
  • Co-partnership/Stock option: Offering company shares at a price lower than the market price.
  • Retirement Benefits: Provident fund, pension, and gratuity.
  • Perquisites: Fringe benefits like car allowance, housing, and medical aid.

Non-Financial Incentives (Psychological and social satisfaction):

  • Status: Ranking of positions, authority, and prestige of the job.
  • Organisational Climate: Characteristics like individual autonomy and reward orientation.
  • Career Advancement Opportunity: Skill development and promotion policies.
  • Job Enrichment: Designing jobs with greater variety, autonomy, and responsibility.
  • Employee Recognition programmes: Congratulating, displaying achievements, or giving mementos.
  • Job security: Stability of future income and employment.
  • Employee participation: Involving employees in decision-making.
  • Employee Empowerment: Giving more autonomy and powers to subordinates.
Leadership

Leadership is the process of influencing the behaviour of people by making them strive voluntarily towards the achievement of organisational goals.

Features & Importance of Leadership

  • Indicates the ability of an individual to influence others.
  • Tries to bring change in the behaviour of others.
  • Reflects interpersonal relations between leaders and followers.
  • Exercised to achieve common organisational goals.
  • It is a continuous process.

Leadership Styles

  • Autocratic or Authoritarian Leader: Gives orders and expects strict obedience. Communication is only one-way. Dogmatic and relies on reward/punishment.
  • Democratic or Participative Leader: Develops action plans and makes decisions in consultation with subordinates, encouraging participation.
  • Laissez-faire or Free-rein Leader: Gives followers a high degree of independence to formulate objectives and resolve issues, acting only as a support system.
Communication

Communication is the process of exchange of ideas, views, facts, and feelings between or among people to create a common understanding.

Elements of the Communication Process

  1. Sender: The source who conveys thoughts or ideas.
  2. Message: The content of ideas, feelings, or suggestions.
  3. Encoding: Converting the message into communication symbols (words, gestures).
  4. Media: The path through which the encoded message is transmitted (written, phone, internet).
  5. Decoding: Converting encoded symbols back into understandable terms.
  6. Receiver: The person who receives the communication.
  7. Feedback: Actions of the receiver indicating understanding of the message.
  8. Noise: Obstructions or hindrances that distort communication (e.g., poor connection, inattentive receiver).

Barriers to Communication

  • Semantic Barriers (Meaning of words): Badly expressed messages, symbols with different meanings, faulty translations, unclarified assumptions, technical jargon, and body language mismatch.
  • Psychological Barriers (Emotional state): Premature evaluation, lack of attention, loss by transmission and poor retention, and distrust.
  • Organisational Barriers (Structure & Policy): Rigid rules, restrictive policies, status distance, complexity in managerial levels, and lack of communication facilities.
  • Personal Barriers (Individual factors): Fear of challenge to authority, lack of confidence in subordinates, unwillingness to communicate, and lack of proper incentives.

Improving Communication Effectiveness

  • Clarify the ideas before communication.
  • Communicate according to the needs of the receiver.
  • Consult others before communicating.
  • Be aware of languages, tone, and content of the message.
  • Convey things of help and value to listeners.
  • Ensure proper feedback.
  • Communicate for the present as well as the future.
  • Follow up communications.
  • Be a good listener.
