Class 11 — Chapter 1: Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

1. Development and Importance of Chemistry

Chemistry is the science of molecules and their transformations. It is the science not so much of the one hundred elements but of the infinite variety of molecules that may be built from them.

Development of Chemistry in Ancient India

  • In ancient India, chemistry was called Rasayan Shastra, Rastantra, Ras Kriya, or Rasvidya. It included metallurgy, medicine, cosmetics, glass, and dyes.
  • Systematic excavations at Mohenjodaro and Harappa show early chemical processes like baked bricks, glazed pottery, gypsum cement (containing lime, sand, and traces of $CaCO_3$), and metal forging (lead, silver, gold, copper, tin, arsenic).
  • Sushruta Samhita explains the importance of alkalies. Charaka Samhita mentions the preparation of sulphuric acid, nitric acid, oxides of copper, tin, zinc, and carbonates of lead and iron.
  • Rasopanishada describes the preparation of gunpowder mixtures.
  • Nagarjuna was a reputed chemist, alchemist, and metallurgist whose work Rasratnakar deals with mercury compounds and extraction of metals.
  • Chakrapani discovered mercury sulphide and is credited with inventing soap using mustard oil and alkalies.
  • Acharya Kanda (born 600 BCE) formulated the atomic theory of matter, naming the indivisible particles Paramānu (comparable to atoms) centuries before John Dalton.

Importance of Chemistry

Chemistry plays a central role in science and is applicable in diverse areas such as weather patterns, brain functioning, computer operations, and chemical industries.

  • Provides life-saving drugs: cisplatin and taxol (effective in cancer therapy), and AZT (Azidothymidine) used for AIDS patients.
  • Assists in environmental management: Synthesizing safer alternatives to environmentally hazardous refrigerants like CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) to protect the ozone layer.
2. Nature and Classification of Matter

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space.

States of Matter

  • Solids: Have definite volume and definite shape. Particles are held very close to each other in an orderly fashion.
  • Liquids: Have definite volume but no definite shape; they take the shape of the container.
  • Gases: Have neither definite volume nor definite shape. They completely occupy the container.
  • These states are interconvertible: $$\text{Solid} \underset{\text{cool}}{\overset{\text{heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Liquid} \underset{\text{cool}}{\overset{\text{heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Gas}$$

Classification of Matter

  • Mixtures: Contain two or more substances in any ratio. Their composition is variable.
    • Homogeneous Mixture: Components completely mix and the composition is uniform throughout (e.g., sugar solution, air).
    • Heterogeneous Mixture: Composition is not uniform and different components are visible (e.g., mixture of salt and sugar, grains and pulses with dirt).
  • Pure Substances: Have a fixed composition.
    • Elements: Consist of only one type of atoms or molecules (e.g., Sodium, Copper, Hydrogen gas $H_2$).
    • Compounds: Formed when two or more atoms of different elements combine in a fixed ratio (e.g., Water $H_2O$, Carbon dioxide $CO_2$). The properties of a compound are different from its constituent elements.
3. Properties of Matter and their Measurement

Physical and Chemical Properties

  • Physical Properties: Can be measured or observed without changing the identity or composition of the substance (e.g., color, odor, melting point, boiling point, density).
  • Chemical Properties: Require a chemical change to occur for measurement (e.g., acidity, basicity, combustibility).

SI Base Units

The International System of Units (SI) defines seven base units:

  • Length: metre ($m$)
  • Mass: kilogram ($kg$)
  • Time: second ($s$)
  • Electric current: ampere ($A$)
  • Thermodynamic temperature: kelvin ($K$)
  • Amount of substance: mole ($mol$)
  • Luminous intensity: candela ($cd$)

Mass, Density, and Temperature

  • Mass vs Weight: Mass is the amount of matter present in a substance (constant). Weight is the force exerted by gravity on an object (variable).
  • Density: Mass per unit volume. $$\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$$ SI unit: $kg\,m^{-3}$ (often expressed as $g\,cm^{-3}$ in laboratories).
  • Temperature Scales: Celsius ($^\circ C$), Fahrenheit ($^\circ F$), and Kelvin ($K$).
    • $$^\circ F = \frac{9}{5}(^\circ C) + 32$$
    • $$K = ^\circ C + 273.15$$
4. Uncertainty in Measurement

Scientific Notation

Exponential notation represented as $N \times 10^n$, where $n$ is an exponent and $N$ is a digit term between $1.000...$ and $9.999...$

  • Example: $232.508$ is written as $2.32508 \times 10^2$.
  • Example: $0.00016$ is written as $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$.

Significant Figures

Significant figures are meaningful digits known with certainty plus one that is estimated or uncertain.

  • Rules:
    1. All non-zero digits are significant.
    2. Zeros preceding the first non-zero digit are not significant (e.g., $0.03$ has one significant figure).
    3. Zeros between two non-zero digits are significant (e.g., $2.005$ has four).
    4. Zeros at the end or right of a number are significant if they are on the right side of the decimal point (e.g., $0.200\,g$ has three). Terminal zeros without a decimal point are not significant (e.g., $100$ has one).
    5. Exact numbers have an infinite number of significant figures (e.g., $2$ balls = $2.000000...$).
  • Rounding Off Rules:
    • If the rightmost digit to be removed is $> 5$, the preceding number is increased by $1$.
    • If it is $< 5$, the preceding number is unchanged.
    • If it is $= 5$, the preceding number is unchanged if even, and increased by $1$ if odd (e.g., $6.35 \rightarrow 6.4$; $6.25 \rightarrow 6.2$).

Precision vs Accuracy

  • Precision refers to the closeness of various measurements for the same quantity.
  • Accuracy is the agreement of a particular value to the true value of the result.
5. Laws of Chemical Combinations

The Five Basic Laws

  1. Law of Conservation of Mass: Formulated by Antoine Lavoisier (1789). Matter can neither be created nor destroyed during physical or chemical changes.
  2. Law of Definite Proportions: Proposed by Joseph Proust. A given compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by weight.
  3. Law of Multiple Proportions: Proposed by John Dalton (1803). If two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in the ratio of small whole numbers.
  4. Gay Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes: Proposed in 1808. When gases combine or are produced in a chemical reaction, they do so in a simple ratio by volume, provided all gases are at the same temperature and pressure.
  5. Avogadro’s Law: Proposed in 1811. Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure should contain an equal number of molecules.

Dalton's Atomic Theory Postulates

According to John Dalton's atomic hypothesis, the nature of matter can be broken down into the following structural rules:

  1. Matter consists of indivisible particles called Atoms.
  2. All atoms of a given element identical properties, including identical mass. Atoms of different elements differ in mass.
  3. Compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine in a fixed, simple numerical ratio.
  4. Chemical reactions involve reorganization of atoms. They are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reactions.
7. Atomic and Molecular Masses

Atomic Mass and Average Atomic Mass

  • One atomic mass unit (amu) (now unified mass, u) is defined as a mass exactly equal to $1/12^{\text{th}}$ of the mass of one carbon-12 atom. $$1\,\text{amu} = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\,g$$
  • Average Atomic Mass is calculated by taking into account the natural abundance of different isotopes of an element. For Carbon: $$(0.98892)(12\,u) + (0.01108)(13.00335\,u) + (2 \times 10^{-12})(14.00317\,u) = 12.011\,u$$

Molecular Mass and Formula Mass

  • Molecular Mass is the sum of atomic masses of the elements present in a molecule. For methane ($CH_4$): $$12.011\,u + 4(1.008\,u) = 16.043\,u$$
  • Formula Mass is used for ionic substances (like $NaCl$) that do not contain discrete molecules. For $NaCl$: $$23.0\,u + 35.5\,u = 58.5\,u$$
8. Mole Concept and Molar Masses

One mole is the amount of substance that contains as many elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, etc.) as there are atoms in exactly $12\,g$ of the $^{12}C$ isotope.

  • This number is the Avogadro constant ($N_A$): $$N_A = 6.02214076 \times 10^{23}\,\text{entities/mol}$$
  • The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is called its molar mass. It is numerically equal to the atomic/molecular/formula mass in $u$.
9. Percentage Composition and Chemical Formulas

Mass Percentage

$$\text{Mass % of an element} = \frac{\text{Mass of that element in the compound} \times 100}{\text{Molar mass of the compound}}$$

Empirical and Molecular Formulas

  • An empirical formula represents the simplest whole-number ratio of various atoms present in a compound.
  • A molecular formula shows the exact number of different types of atoms present in a molecule of a compound.
  • $$\text{Molecular Formula} = n \times \text{Empirical Formula}$$ where $$n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}}$$
10. Stoichiometry and Reactions in Solutions

Stoichiometric Calculations

Stoichiometry deals with the calculation of masses (and volumes) of reactants and products involved in a chemical reaction. Example balanced equation:

CH4 (g) + 2O2 (g) -> CO2 (g) + 2H2O (g)

Limiting Reagent

The reactant that is completely consumed first in a reaction, limiting the amount of product formed, is called the limiting reagent.

Reactions in Solutions

The concentration of a solution can be expressed in several ways:

  1. Mass Percent: $$\text{Mass %} = \frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}} \times 100$$
  2. Mole Fraction ($x$): Ratio of the number of moles of a particular component to the total number of moles of the solution. $$x_A = \frac{n_A}{n_A + n_B}$$
  3. Molarity ($M$): Number of moles of solute in $1$ litre of solution. $$\text{Molarity (M)} = \frac{\text{No. of moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution in litres}}$$
  4. Molality ($m$): Number of moles of solute present in $1\,kg$ of solvent. $$\text{Molality (m)} = \frac{\text{No. of moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}}$$
