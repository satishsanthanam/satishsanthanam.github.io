Class 111 — Chapter 1: Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

1. Introduction and Historical Development of Chemistry

Chemistry is the science of molecules and their transformations. It is the science not so much of the one hundred elements but of the infinite variety of molecules that may be built from them.

Development of Chemistry

In ancient India, chemistry was called Rasayan Shastra, Rastantra, Ras Kriya, or Rasvidya. It encompassed metallurgy, medicine, cosmetics, glass, and dyes.

  • Archaeological Evidence: Excavations at Mohenjodaro and Harappa showed the use of baked bricks, glazed pottery, gypsum cement (containing lime, sand, and traces of $CaCO_3$), and early glass (faience).
  • Metallurgy: Indigenous technologies for copper and iron extraction were developed. Copper metallurgy dates back to the chalcolithic cultures.
  • Ancient Texts:
    • Rigveda mentions tanning of leather and dyeing of cotton.
    • Sushruta Samhita explains the importance of alkalies.
    • Charaka Samhita describes the preparation of mineral acids (sulphuric acid, nitric acid) and the use of bhasmas (nanoparticles of metals) for treating ailments.
    • Rasopanishada describes the preparation of gunpowder mixtures.
    • Nagarjuna's Rasratnakar deals with the formulation of mercury compounds and extraction of metals like gold, silver, tin, and copper.
  • Atomic Theory in Ancient India: Acharya Kanda (born 600 BCE) proposed that matter is made of indivisible particles called Paramānu (comparable to atoms), which are eternal, indestructible, spherical, and in constant motion in their original state.

Importance of Chemistry

Chemistry plays a central role in science and is deeply integrated into daily life and national economy:

  • Agriculture: Large-scale production of fertilizers (urea, ammonium sulphate), pesticides, and insecticides.
  • Medicine: Isolation and synthesis of life-saving drugs such as cisplatin and taxol (effective in cancer therapy) and AZT (Azidothymidine, used for AIDS patients).
  • Environment: Synthesis of safer alternatives to environmentally hazardous refrigerants like CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) to protect the ozone layer.
2. Nature and Classification of Matter

Matter is defined as anything that has mass and occupies space.

States of Matter

Matter exists in three physical states:

  1. Solids: Have definite volume and definite shape. Particles are held very close to each other in an orderly fashion with restricted movement.
  2. Liquids: Have definite volume but no definite shape; they take the shape of the container. Particles are close but can move around.
  3. Gases: Have neither definite volume nor definite shape. They completely occupy the container. Particles are far apart and move rapidly.

These states are interconvertible by changing temperature and pressure:

$$\text{Solid} \underset{\text{cool}}{\overset{\text{heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Liquid} \underset{\text{cool}}{\overset{\text{heat}}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Gas}$$

Classification of Matter
  • Mixtures: Contain two or more substances present in any ratio. Their composition is variable.
    • Homogeneous Mixtures: Components completely mix and have a uniform composition throughout (e.g., sugar solution, air).
    • Heterogeneous Mixtures: Composition is not uniform, and different components are visible (e.g., mixture of salt and sugar, grains and dirt).
  • Pure Substances: Have a fixed composition. Constituents cannot be separated by simple physical methods.
    • Elements: Consist of only one type of particles (atoms or molecules) (e.g., Sodium, Copper, Hydrogen gas $H_2$).
    • Compounds: Formed when two or more atoms of different elements combine in a fixed ratio. Their properties are entirely different from their constituent elements (e.g., Water $H_2O$, Carbon dioxide $CO_2$).
3. Properties of Matter and their Measurement

Physical and Chemical Properties
  • Physical Properties: Can be measured or observed without changing the identity or composition of the substance (e.g., color, odor, melting point, boiling point, density).
  • Chemical Properties: Require a chemical change to occur for measurement (e.g., acidity, basicity, combustibility, reactivity).

The International System of Units (SI)

Established by the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), the SI system consists of seven base units:

  • Length ($l$): metre ($\text{m}$)
  • Mass ($m$): kilogram ($\text{kg}$)
  • Time ($t$): second ($\text{s}$)
  • Electric current ($I$): ampere ($\text{A}$)
  • Thermodynamic temperature ($T$): kelvin ($\text{K}$)
  • Amount of substance ($n$): mole ($\text{mol}$)
  • Luminous intensity ($I_v$): candela ($\text{cd}$)

Mass, Volume, Density, and Temperature
  • Mass vs. Weight: Mass is the amount of matter present in a substance (constant). Weight is the force exerted by gravity on an object (variable).
  • Volume: Amount of space occupied. SI unit is $\text{m}^3$. Commonly measured in litres ($\text{L}$), where $1\text{ L} = 1000\text{ mL} = 1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ dm}^3$.
  • Density: Mass per unit volume.
    $$\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}$$
    SI unit is $\text{kg m}^{-3}$, often expressed as $\text{g cm}^{-3}$ in laboratories.
  • Temperature Scales: Measured in $^\circ\text{C}$ (Celsius), $^\circ\text{F}$ (Fahrenheit), and $\text{K}$ (Kelvin).
    $$^\circ\text{F} = \frac{9}{5}(^\circ\text{C}) + 32$$ $$\text{K} = ^\circ\text{C} + 273.15$$
    Note: Negative temperatures are possible on the Celsius scale but impossible on the Kelvin scale.
4. Uncertainty in Measurement

Scientific Notation

Exponential notation where any number is represented in the form $N \times 10^n$, where $n$ is an exponent (positive or negative) and $N$ is a digit term between $1.000...$ and $9.999...$

  • Example: $232.508 = 2.32508 \times 10^2$
  • Example: $0.00016 = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}$

Significant Figures

Significant figures are meaningful digits known with certainty plus one that is estimated or uncertain.

Rules for Determining Significant Figures:
  1. All non-zero digits are significant (e.g., $285\text{ cm}$ has three).
  2. Zeros preceding the first non-zero digit are not significant (e.g., $0.03$ has one).
  3. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant (e.g., $2.005$ has four).
  4. Zeros at the end or right of a number are significant if they are on the right side of the decimal point (e.g., $0.200\text{ g}$ has three). Terminal zeros without a decimal point are not significant (e.g., $100$ has one, but $100.$ has three).
  5. Exact numbers have an infinite number of significant figures (e.g., $2$ balls $= 2.000000...$).

Precision and Accuracy
  • Precision refers to the closeness of various measurements for the same quantity.
  • Accuracy is the agreement of a particular value to the true value of the result.

Dimensional Analysis

Also called the factor label method or unit factor method, used to convert units from one system to another using unit factors.

$$\text{Since } 1\text{ in} = 2.54\text{ cm} \implies \frac{1\text{ in}}{2.54\text{ cm}} = 1 = \frac{2.54\text{ cm}}{1\text{ in}}$$
5. Laws of Chemical Combinations

Law of Conservation of Mass

Proposed by Antoine Lavoisier (1789). States that matter can neither be created nor destroyed during physical or chemical changes; hence, there is no net change in mass.

Law of Definite Proportions

Proposed by Joseph Proust. States that a given compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by weight, regardless of its source.

Law of Multiple Proportions

Proposed by John Dalton (1803). If two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in the ratio of small whole numbers.

Example: Hydrogen and Oxygen combine to form Water ($2\text{g}$ H + $16\text{g}$ O) and Hydrogen Peroxide ($2\text{g}$ H + $32\text{g}$ O). The ratio of masses of oxygen is $16:32$ or $1:2$.

Gay Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes

Proposed by Gay Lussac (1808). When gases combine or are produced in a chemical reaction, they do so in a simple ratio by volume, provided all gases are at the same temperature and pressure.

Example: $100\text{ mL}$ of hydrogen combines with $50\text{ mL}$ of oxygen to give $100\text{ mL}$ of water vapour (ratio $2:1:2$).

Avogadro’s Law

Proposed by Amedeo Avogadro (1811). Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure should contain an equal number of molecules.

6. Dalton's Atomic Theory

Published in 'A New System of Chemical Philosophy' (1808):

  1. Matter consists of indivisible atoms.
  2. All atoms of a given element have identical properties, including identical mass. Atoms of different elements differ in mass.
  3. Compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine in a fixed ratio.
  4. Chemical reactions involve reorganization of atoms. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
7. Atomic and Molecular Masses

Atomic Mass

Defined relative to Carbon-12 ($^{12}\text{C}$) as the standard, which is assigned a mass of exactly $12$ atomic mass units ($\text{amu}$ or $\text{u}$, unified mass).

$$1\text{ amu} = \frac{1}{12} \times \text{mass of one }^{12}\text{C atom} = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g}$$

Average Atomic Mass

Calculated by taking into account the natural abundance of different isotopes of an element.

$$\text{Average Atomic Mass} = \sum (\text{Isotopic Mass} \times \text{Fractional Abundance})$$

Molecular Mass and Formula Mass
  • Molecular Mass is the sum of atomic masses of the elements present in a molecule (e.g., $CH_4 = 12.011\text{ u} + 4(1.008\text{ u}) = 16.043\text{ u}$).
  • Formula Mass is used for ionic substances (like $NaCl$) which do not contain discrete molecules but exist as three-dimensional crystal lattices (e.g., Formula mass of $NaCl = 23.0\text{ u} + 35.5\text{ u} = 58.5\text{ u}$).
8. Mole Concept and Molar Masses

The mole (symbol: $\text{mol}$) is the SI unit for the amount of substance.

  • One mole contains exactly $6.02214076 \times 10^{23}$ elementary entities. This number is the fixed numerical value of the Avogadro constant ($N_A$).
  • The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is called its molar mass (expressed in $\text{g mol}^{-1}$). It is numerically equal to its atomic/molecular/formula mass in $\text{u}$.
9. Percentage Composition and Chemical Formulas

Mass Percentage
$$\text{Mass \% of an element} = \frac{\text{Mass of that element in the compound} \times 100}{\text{Molar mass of the compound}}$$

Empirical and Molecular Formulas
  • An empirical formula represents the simplest whole-number ratio of various atoms present in a compound.
  • A molecular formula shows the exact number of different types of atoms present in a molecule of a compound.
  • Relationship:
    $$\text{Molecular Formula} = n \times \text{Empirical Formula}$$ $$n = \frac{\text{Molar Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}}$$
10. Stoichiometry and Reactions in Solutions

Stoichiometric Calculations

Deals with the calculation of masses (and volumes) of reactants and products involved in a balanced chemical reaction.

Limiting Reagent

The reactant that is completely consumed first in a chemical reaction, limiting the amount of product formed, is called the limiting reagent.

Reactions in Solutions

The concentration of a solution can be expressed in several ways:

  1. Mass Percent (w/w %):
    $$\text{Mass \%} = \frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}} \times 100$$
  2. Mole Fraction ($x$): Ratio of number of moles of a particular component to the total number of moles of the solution.
    $$x_A = \frac{n_A}{n_A + n_B}$$
  3. Molarity ($M$): Number of moles of solute in $1$ litre of solution.
    $$\text{Molarity (M)} = \frac{\text{No. of moles of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution in litres}}$$
    Note: Molarity is temperature-dependent because volume changes with temperature.
  4. Molality ($m$): Number of moles of solute present in $1\text{ kg}$ of solvent.
    $$\text{Molality (m)} = \frac{\text{No. of moles of solute}}{\text{Mass of solvent in kg}}$$
    Note: Molality is independent of temperature as mass does not change with temperature.
