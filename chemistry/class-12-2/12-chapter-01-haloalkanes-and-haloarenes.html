Chemistry: Class 12 — Chapter 1: Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

Introduction and Classification

The replacement of hydrogen atom(s) in an aliphatic or aromatic hydrocarbon by halogen atom(s) results in the formation of alkyl halide (haloalkane) and aryl halide (haloarene), respectively.

Classification Based on Number of Halogen Atoms

Compounds are classified depending on whether they contain one, two, or more halogen atoms in their structures:

  • Monohaloalkanes / Monohaloarenes: Contain one halogen atom (e.g., $C_2H_5X$).
  • Dihaloalkanes / Dihaloarenes: Contain two halogen atoms (e.g., $CH_2X-CH_2X$).
  • Trihaloalkanes / Trihaloarenes: Contain three halogen atoms.

Compounds Containing $sp^3$ C—X Bond
  • Alkyl halides or haloalkanes (R—X): The halogen atom is bonded to an alkyl group. They form a homologous series represented by $C_nH_{2n+1}X$. They are further classified into:
    • Primary ($1^\circ$): Halogen attached to a primary carbon.
    • Secondary ($2^\circ$): Halogen attached to a secondary carbon.
    • Tertiary ($3^\circ$): Halogen attached to a tertiary carbon.
  • Allylic halides: Halogen atom is bonded to an $sp^3$-hybridised carbon atom adjacent to a carbon-carbon double bond ($C=C$).
  • Benzylic halides: Halogen atom is bonded to an $sp^3$-hybridised carbon atom attached to an aromatic ring.

Compounds Containing $sp^2$ C—X Bond
  • Vinylic halides: Halogen atom is bonded to an $sp^2$-hybridised carbon atom of a carbon-carbon double bond ($C=C$).
  • Aryl halides: Halogen atom is directly bonded to the $sp^2$-hybridised carbon atom of an aromatic ring.
Nomenclature and Nature of C—X Bond

Nomenclature Rules
  • Common Names: Derived by naming the alkyl group followed by the halide name (e.g., n-propyl bromide).
  • IUPAC Names: Named as halosubstituted hydrocarbons (e.g., 1-Bromopropane).
  • Dihaloalkanes: Classified as:
    • Geminal halides (gem-dihalides): Both halogen atoms are on the same carbon atom (alkylidene halides).
    • Vicinal halides (vic-dihalides): Halogen atoms are on adjacent carbon atoms (alkylene dihalides).

Nature of C—X Bond

Halogen atoms are more electronegative than carbon, making the carbon-halogen bond highly polarized. The carbon atom bears a partial positive charge ($\delta+$) while the halogen atom bears a partial negative charge ($\delta-$).

  • Bond Length: Increases down the group from C—F to C—I as the size of the halogen increases ($C-F < C-Cl < C-Br < C-I$).
  • Bond Enthalpy: Decreases down the group as bond length increases.
  • Dipole Moment: Highly influenced by electronegativity and bond length.
Methods of Preparation of Haloalkanes

From Alcohols

The hydroxyl group of an alcohol is replaced by halogen using various reagents:

  • By reaction with concentrated halogen acids in the presence of a catalyst ($ZnCl_2$): $$R-OH + HCl \xrightarrow{ZnCl_2} R-Cl + H_2O$$
  • By reaction with phosphorus halides: $$3R-OH + PX_3 \rightarrow 3R-X + H_3PO_3 \quad (X = Cl, Br)$$ $$R-OH + PCl_5 \rightarrow R-Cl + POCl_3 + HCl$$
  • By reaction with thionyl chloride (Thionyl chloride is preferred because the other products, $SO_2$ and $HCl$, are escapable gases, yielding pure alkyl halides): $$R-OH + SOCl_2 \rightarrow R-Cl + SO_2(g) + HCl(g)$$

From Hydrocarbons
  • By Free Radical Halogenation of Alkanes: Chlorination or bromination in the presence of UV light or heat yields a complex mixture of isomeric mono- and polyhaloalkanes.
  • From Alkenes:
    • Addition of hydrogen halides: Alkenes react with $HX$ ($HCl, HBr, HI$) to form alkyl halides. Unsymmetrical alkenes follow Markovnikov's rule. $$CH_3-CH=CH_2 + H-I \rightarrow CH_3-CH(I)-CH_3 \quad (Major)$$
    • Addition of halogens: Addition of bromine in $CCl_4$ to an alkene results in the discharge of the reddish-brown color of bromine, serving as an important test for unsaturation. $$CH_2=CH_2 + Br_2 \xrightarrow{CCl_4} BrCH_2-CH_2Br \quad (vic\text{-Dibromide})$$

Halogen Exchange Reactions
  • Finkelstein Reaction: Alkyl iodides are prepared by reacting alkyl chlorides/bromides with sodium iodide ($NaI$) in dry acetone. $$R-X + NaI \xrightarrow{\text{dry acetone}} R-I + NaX \quad (X = Cl, Br)$$
  • Swarts Reaction: Alkyl fluorides are synthesized by heating alkyl chlorides/bromides in the presence of metallic fluorides such as $AgF$, $Hg_2F_2$, $CoF_2$, or $SbF_3$. $$H_3C-Br + AgF \rightarrow H_3C-F + AgBr$$
Preparation of Haloarenes

From Hydrocarbons by Electrophilic Substitution

Aryl chlorides and bromides are prepared by direct halogenation of arenes with chlorine or bromine in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst like iron or iron(III) chloride ($FeCl_3$) in the dark.

  • The ortho and para isomers can be separated due to a large difference in their melting points.
  • Reactions with iodine are reversible and require the presence of an oxidizing agent ($HNO_3$, $HIO_4$) to oxidize the $HI$ formed.

From Amines by Sandmeyer's Reaction

When a primary aromatic amine dissolved in cold aqueous mineral acid is treated with sodium nitrite ($NaNO_2$), a diazonium salt is formed. Shaking the freshly prepared diazonium salt with cuprous chloride or cuprous bromide results in the replacement of the diazonium group by $-Cl$ or $-Br$.

  1. Diazotisation: $$Ar-NH_2 + NaNO_2 + 2HX \xrightarrow{273-278 K} Ar-N_2^+X^- + NaX + 2H_2O$$
  2. Sandmeyer Reaction: $$Ar-N_2^+X^- \xrightarrow{Cu_2X_2} Ar-X + N_2 \quad (X = Cl, Br)$$
  3. Iodide Replacement: Shaking the diazonium salt with potassium iodide ($KI$) yields aryl iodides without requiring cuprous halide. $$Ar-N_2^+X^- + KI \rightarrow Ar-I + K+ + N_2$$
Physical Properties
  • State and Odour: Alkyl halides are colorless when pure. Bromides and iodides develop color when exposed to light. Volatile halogen compounds often have a sweet smell.
  • Boiling Points: For a given alkyl group, the boiling points of alkyl halides decrease in the order: $$RI > RBr > RCl > RF$$ This is due to the increase in size and mass of the halogen atom, which increases the magnitude of van der Waals forces.
  • Effect of Branching: Boiling points of isomeric haloalkanes decrease with an increase in branching due to a decrease in surface area. $$\text{n-butyl bromide} > \text{isobutyl bromide} > \text{tert-butyl bromide}$$
  • Melting Points of Dihalobenzenes: The para-isomers have higher melting points than their ortho- and meta-isomers due to the symmetry of the para-isomer, which fits better into the crystal lattice.
  • Solubility: Haloalkanes are only very slightly soluble in water because they cannot form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. However, they are highly soluble in organic solvents.
Chemical Reactions of Haloalkanes

1. Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions

A nucleophile attacks the electron-deficient carbon bonded to the halogen, displacing the halide ion (leaving group):

$$Nu^- + C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-} \rightarrow C-Nu + X^-$$

Mechanisms of Substitution:

  • $S_N2$ (Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular):
    • Occurs in a single step via a transition state with no intermediate.
    • The incoming nucleophile attacks from the side opposite to the leaving group, resulting in complete inversion of configuration (Walden inversion).
    • Order of reactivity: $\text{Primary } (1^\circ) > \text{Secondary } (2^\circ) > \text{Tertiary } (3^\circ)$ (governed by steric hindrance).
  • $S_N1$ (Substitution Nucleophilic Unimolecular):
    • Occurs in two steps. Step I (slow, rate-determining) involves the cleavage of the C—X bond to form a stable carbocation. Step II (fast) involves the attack of the nucleophile.
    • Accompanied by racemisation due to the planar nature of the carbocation intermediate.
    • Order of reactivity: $\text{Tertiary } (3^\circ) > \text{Secondary } (2^\circ) > \text{Primary } (1^\circ)$ (governed by carbocation stability).

2. Elimination Reactions ($\beta$-Elimination)

When a haloalkane with a $\beta$-hydrogen atom is heated with an alcoholic solution of potassium hydroxide ($KOH$), elimination of a hydrogen atom from the $\beta$-carbon and a halogen atom from the $\alpha$-carbon occurs, forming an alkene.

  • Zaitsev (Saytzeff) Rule: In dehydrohalogenation reactions, the preferred product is the highly substituted alkene (the one with the greater number of alkyl groups attached to the doubly bonded carbon atoms).

3. Reaction with Metals
  • Formation of Grignard Reagents: Alkyl halides react with magnesium metal in dry ether to form alkyl magnesium halides ($RMgX$), known as Grignard Reagents. $$R-X + Mg \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} RMgX$$ Grignard reagents are highly reactive and decompose in the presence of any proton source (like water, alcohols) to form hydrocarbons: $$RMgX + H_2O \rightarrow R-H + Mg(OH)X$$
  • Wurtz Reaction: Alkyl halides react with sodium in dry ether to give hydrocarbons containing double the number of carbon atoms. $$2R-X + 2Na \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} R-R + 2NaX$$
Chemical Reactions of Haloarenes

Low Reactivity towards Nucleophilic Substitution

Aryl halides are extremely unreactive towards nucleophilic substitution due to:

  1. Resonance Effect: The electron pairs on the halogen atom are conjugated with the $\pi$-electrons of the benzene ring, giving the C—X bond a partial double bond character, making it difficult to break.
  2. Difference in Hybridisation: The carbon attached to the halogen is $sp^2$-hybridised in haloarenes (shorter, stronger bond) compared to $sp^3$-hybridised in haloalkanes.
  3. Instability of Phenyl Cation: Self-ionization to form a phenyl cation is highly unstable.

Dow's Process (Drastic Conditions): Chlorobenzene can be converted to phenol under high temperature and pressure: $$\text{Chlorobenzene} \xrightarrow{\text{(i) NaOH, 623K, 300 atm, (ii) } H^+} \text{Phenol}$$ The presence of electron-withdrawing groups (like $-NO_2$) at ortho- and para-positions significantly increases the reactivity of haloarenes.

Electrophilic Substitution Reactions

The halogen atom on the benzene ring is deactivating but $o, p$-directing due to resonance.

  • Halogenation: Reaction with $Cl_2$ in anhydrous $FeCl_3$ yields 1,4-dichlorobenzene (major) and 1,2-dichlorobenzene (minor).
  • Nitration: Reaction with concentrated $HNO_3$ and $H_2SO_4$ yields 1-chloro-4-nitrobenzene (major).
  • Sulphonation: Heating with concentrated $H_2SO_4$ yields 4-chlorobenzenesulfonic acid (major).
  • Friedel-Crafts Alkylation/Acylation: Reaction with alkyl/acyl halides in the presence of anhydrous $AlCl_3$.

Reaction with Metals
  • Wurtz-Fittig Reaction: A mixture of an alkyl halide and aryl halide treated with sodium in dry ether gives an alkylarene. $$Ar-X + Na + R-X \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} Ar-R + 2NaX$$
  • Fittig Reaction: Aryl halides treated with sodium in dry ether yield diaryl compounds. $$2Ar-X + 2Na \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} Ar-Ar + 2NaX$$
Polyhalogen Compounds

Carbon compounds containing more than one halogen atom are called polyhalogen compounds. Key examples include:

  • Dichloromethane (Methylene chloride, $CH_2Cl_2$): Used as a solvent, paint remover, and propellant. Harmful to the central nervous system.
  • Trichloromethane (Chloroform, $CHCl_3$): Historically used as an anesthetic; now used to produce Freon refrigerant R-22. Oxidizes slowly in air and light to form highly poisonous phosgene gas ($COCl_2$): $$2CHCl_3 + O_2 \xrightarrow{\text{light}} 2COCl_2 + 2HCl$$
  • Triiodomethane (Iodoform, $CHI_3$): Used as an antiseptic due to the liberation of free iodine.
  • Tetrachloromethane (Carbon tetrachloride, $CCl_4$): Used in refrigerants, propellants, and as an industrial solvent. Causes ozone depletion and is highly toxic to the liver.
  • Freons: Chlorofluorocarbon (CFC) compounds of methane and ethane (e.g., Freon-12, $CCl_2F_2$). Extremely stable but deplete the stratospheric ozone layer.
  • DDT (p,p'-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane): The first chlorinated organic insecticide. Highly effective against malaria-carrying mosquitoes but banned in many countries due to its high toxicity to fish, chemical stability, and bioaccumulation in fatty tissues.
