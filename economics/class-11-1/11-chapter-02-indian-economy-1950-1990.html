Economics: Class 11 (Part 1) — Chapter 2: Indian Economy 1950–1990

1. Introduction & Economic Systems

After independence in 1947, India's leaders had to decide on the type of Economic System most suitable for the nation to promote the welfare of all. While socialism appealed to Jawaharlal Nehru, the extreme Soviet model was not feasible in a democratic setup. India opted for a Mixed Economy.

Types of Economic Systems

Every society must answer three basic questions: What to produce, How to produce, and For whom to produce. The answers define the economic system:

2. Goals of Five Year Plans

In 1950, the Planning Commission was set up with the Prime Minister as its Chairperson. The goals of the five-year plans (borrowed from the Soviet Union) were designed to guide India's development over a 20-year horizon (known as a Perspective Plan).

The Four Core Goals

  • Growth: Refers to the increase in the country's capacity to produce the output of goods and services. A good indicator of economic growth is the steady increase in the Gross Domestic Product (GDP).
  • Modernisation: Adoption of new technology to increase production (e.g., using high-yielding seed varieties) and changes in social outlook (e.g., gender equality and recognizing women's rights in the workplace).
  • Self-reliance: Avoiding imports of goods that could be produced domestically, especially food grains, to reduce dependence on foreign nations and safeguard sovereignty.
  • Equity: Ensuring that the benefits of economic prosperity reach the poor sections of society rather than being enjoyed only by the rich, enabling every Indian to meet basic needs.

Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis

Known as the architect of Indian planning, statistician Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis formulated the basic ideas of the Second Five Year Plan, emphasizing industrialization and establishing the Indian Statistical Institute (ISI).

3. Agriculture (1950–1990)

At independence, 75% of India's population depended on agriculture, which suffered from low productivity and colonial-era exploitation.

Land Reforms

  • Abolition of Intermediaries: Steps were taken to abolish intermediaries (like zamindars and jagirdars) to make the actual tillers the owners of the land, giving them incentives to invest and increase output.
  • Land Ceiling: Fixing the maximum size of agricultural land that could be owned by an individual to reduce the concentration of land ownership.
  • Implementation Hurdles: Big landlords challenged the legislation in courts and used loopholes to retain land. Land reforms were highly successful only in states like Kerala and West Bengal due to strong political commitment.

The Green Revolution

The stagnation in agriculture was broken by the Green Revolution, which refers to the large increase in production of food grains resulting from the use of HYV Seeds (High Yielding Variety), especially for wheat and rice.

  1. First Phase (mid-1960s to mid-1970s): Restricted to affluent states (Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu) and primarily benefited wheat-growing regions.
  2. Second Phase (mid-1970s to mid-1980s): Spread to more states and benefited a wider variety of crops, enabling India to achieve self-sufficiency in food grains.
  3. Marketed Surplus: The portion of agricultural produce sold in the market by farmers. A high marketed surplus during the Green Revolution led to a decline in food prices, benefiting low-income groups.

The Debate Over Subsidies

Subsidies were necessary to encourage poor farmers to adopt new HYV technology. However, their continuation is highly debated:

  • Arguments Against Subsidies: Once technology is widely adopted, subsidies should be phased out. They place a huge burden on government finances and often benefit fertilizer industries and rich farmers rather than the target group.
  • Arguments For Subsidies: Farming in India remains highly risky. Eliminating subsidies would increase inequality between rich and poor farmers, violating the goal of equity. The solution is to target them better, not abolish them.
4. Industry and Trade (1950–1990)

Industrial development provides stable employment, promotes modernisation, and drives overall prosperity.

Public and Private Sectors

At independence, private industrialists lacked the capital to undertake major projects, and the market was too small. Therefore, the state had to play an extensive role and control the "commanding heights" of the economy.

Industrial Policy Resolution 1956 (IPR 1956)

Formed the basis of the Second Five Year Plan and classified industries into three categories:

  1. Category I: Industries exclusively owned by the government.
  2. Category II: Industries where the private sector could supplement state efforts, with the government taking the lead in starting new units.
  3. Category III: Remaining industries left to the private sector, regulated heavily through a system of licenses.

The Permit License Raj required private firms to obtain a government license to start, expand, or diversify production, aiming to promote regional equality by favoring backward regions.

Small-Scale Industry (SSI)

In 1955, the Karve Committee recognized the potential of Small-Scale Industry for rural development. SSIs are more labor-intensive and generate more employment, but they require protection (reservation of products, tax concessions, and low-interest bank loans) to compete with large firms.

Trade Policy: Import Substitution

India adopted an inward-looking trade strategy called Import Substitution, aiming to replace imports with domestic production. Protection from foreign competition was achieved through:

  • Tariffs: Taxes on imported goods to make them more expensive.
  • Quotas: Specifying the maximum quantity of goods that can be imported.

Achievements and Failures

  • Achievements: Industrial share of GDP increased from 13% to 24.6%; the industrial sector became highly diversified; the Green Revolution made India self-sufficient in food; land reforms abolished the zamindari system.
  • Failures: Inefficient public sector enterprises (PSUs) incurred massive losses; the Permit License Raj stifled private entrepreneurship and efficiency; protectionist policies left domestic consumers with low-quality goods due to lack of competition; the proportion of the population dependent on agriculture did not decline significantly (from 67.5% to 64.9%).
