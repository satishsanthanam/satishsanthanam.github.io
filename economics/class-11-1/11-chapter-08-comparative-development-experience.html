Class 11 (Part 1) — Chapter 8: Comparative Development Experiences of India and Its Neighbours

8.1 Introduction & Developmental Path

In an increasingly globalised world, understanding the developmental strategies of neighbouring nations is essential for developing countries as they compete within limited global economic spaces. This chapter compares the developmental trajectories of India, China, and Pakistan.

Regional and Global Groupings

Nations form regional and global economic groupings to strengthen their domestic economies. Examples include:

  • SAARC (South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation)
  • European Union (EU)
  • ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations)
  • G-8 and G-20
  • BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa)

Timeline of Developmental Paths

  • India: Attained independence in 1947. Launched its First Five-Year Plan in 1951. Followed a Five-Year Plan-based model until March 2017.
  • Pakistan: Attained independence in 1947. Launched its First Five-Year Plan (now Medium Term Development Plan) in 1956. Currently working on its 12th Five-Year Development Plan (2018–23).
  • China: Established the People’s Republic of China in 1949 under a one-party rule. Launched its First Five-Year Plan in 1953. Currently working on its 14th Five-Year Plan (2021–25).

China's Core Economic Reforms

China brought all critical sectors, enterprises, and land under government control initially, followed by major structural reforms:

  • Great Leap Forward (GLF): Initiated in 1958, aiming to industrialise the country on a massive scale by encouraging backyard industries and establishing the Commune system (collective farming).
  • Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution (1966–76): Introduced by Mao Zedong, where students and professionals were sent to work and learn from the countryside.
  • 1978 Reforms: Introduced in phases. The initial phase focused on agriculture, dividing commune lands into small plots allocated to individual households for use. The later phase focused on the industrial sector, allowing private sector firms and township/village enterprises to compete with State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs).
  • Special Economic Zones (SEZs): Established to attract foreign direct investment.

Pakistan's Core Economic Reforms

Pakistan adopted a mixed economy model with the co-existence of public and private sectors:

  • 1950s–1960s: Implemented import substitution-based industrialisation policies, combining tariff protection for consumer goods with direct import controls.
  • Green Revolution: Led to mechanisation and increased public investment in infrastructure, boosting foodgrain production.
  • 1970s: Nationalisation of capital goods industries took place.
  • Late 1970s–1980s: Shifted towards denationalisation and encouragement of the private sector, supported by financial aid from western nations and remittances from emigrants in the Middle East.
  • 1988: Structural reforms were formally initiated.

Key Demographic Trends

  • Population Growth: Pakistan has the highest annual population growth rate, while China has registered a negative growth rate due to its historical one-child norm introduced in the late 1970s.
  • Sex Ratio: Low and biased against females in all three countries, primarily due to a historical preference for male children.
  • Urbanisation: China is highly urbanised at 65%, compared to India (36%) and Pakistan (38%).

Structural Shifts and Sectoral Trends

  • China's Transition: China followed the classical development pattern, shifting workforce and output from agriculture to manufacturing, and subsequently to services. Only 10% of China's land is suitable for cultivation.
  • India and Pakistan's Transition: Both countries experienced a direct structural shift from agriculture to the service sector, bypassing a dominant manufacturing phase.
  • Service Sector Dominance: The service sector has emerged as the largest contributor to GDP in all three economies.

8.5 Appraisal of Development Strategies

An assessment of the successes and failures of the three economies over the last seven decades.

China's Performance

  • Success Factors: Pre-reform public interventions in social infrastructure (education, health), land reforms, and the equitable distribution of food grains through the commune system laid a strong foundation.
  • Market Mechanism: China successfully integrated the market system with collective ownership, creating economic opportunities without losing political control.
  • Concerns: Lack of political freedom and human rights issues remain major areas of concern.

Pakistan's Performance

  • Challenges: Political instability, over-dependence on foreign loans, volatile agricultural performance based on weather conditions, and reliance on remittances from the Middle East led to a slowdown and re-emergence of poverty.
  • Recent Trends: The economy has shown signs of recovery with stable macroeconomic indicators in recent years.

India's Performance

  • Success Factors: Sustained moderate growth within a robust democratic framework and a secular constitution.
  • Challenges: A large portion of the workforce remains dependent on agriculture, and progress in critical human development indicators (like sanitation, maternal health, and undernourishment) lags behind China.
