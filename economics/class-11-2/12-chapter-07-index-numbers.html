Economics: Class 11 (Part 2) — Chapter 7: Index Numbers

1. Introduction to Index Numbers

An index number is a statistical device designed to measure changes in a group of related variables over time, geographical location, or other characteristics. It serves as a single summary measure to represent the general trend of diverging ratios.

Core Characteristics & Concepts
  • Base Period: The reference period with which comparisons are made. It is conventionally assigned an index value of 100.
  • Current Period: The period for which the comparison or change is being calculated.
  • Percentage Expression: Index numbers are conventionally expressed as percentages to facilitate easy comparison, though the percentage sign (%) is usually omitted.
  • Types of Indices:
    • Price Index: Measures and compares the prices of certain goods over time.
    • Quantity Index: Measures changes in the physical volume of production, construction, or employment.
2. Methods of Constructing Index Numbers

There are two primary methods used to construct index numbers: the Aggregative Method and the Method of Averaging Relatives.

A. The Aggregative Method

This method aggregates the prices (or quantities) of various commodities in the current year and compares them with the aggregate in the base year.

1. Simple Aggregative Price Index

Treats all items with equal importance, regardless of their actual economic weight.

$$P_{01} = \frac{\sum P_1}{\sum P_0} \times 100$$

Where:

  • $P_{01}$ = Price index of the current year (1) relative to the base year (0)
  • $\sum P_1$ = Sum of prices of all commodities in the current period
  • $\sum P_0$ = Sum of prices of all commodities in the base period

2. Weighted Aggregative Price Index

Assigns weights to different commodities based on their relative importance (usually quantities consumed or produced).

  • Laspeyre's Price Index: Uses base period quantities ($q_0$) as weights.
    $$P_{01} = \frac{\sum P_1 q_0}{\sum P_0 q_0} \times 100$$
  • Paasche's Price Index: Uses current period quantities ($q_1$) as weights.
    $$P_{01} = \frac{\sum P_1 q_1}{\sum P_0 q_1} \times 100$$

B. Method of Averaging Relatives

This method computes price relatives for each individual commodity first, and then averages those relatives.

1. Simple Average of Price Relatives

$$P_{01} = \frac{1}{n} \sum \left( \frac{P_1}{P_0} \times 100 \right)$$

Where $n$ is the number of commodities.

2. Weighted Average of Price Relatives

$$P_{01} = \frac{\sum W_i \left( \frac{P_{1i}}{P_{0i}} \times 100 \right)}{\sum W_i}$$

Where $W_i$ represents the weight (percentage of expenditure or value share) assigned to commodity $i$.

3. Important Index Numbers in India

Several specialized index numbers are regularly prepared by government agencies to monitor economic performance and guide policy decisions.

A. Consumer Price Index (CPI)

Also known as the Cost of Living Index, CPI measures the average change in retail prices paid by consumers for a specified basket of goods and services.

$$\text{CPI} = \frac{\sum WR}{\sum W}$$

Where $W$ is the weight and $R = \frac{P_1}{P_0} \times 100$ is the price relative.

Major CPI Series in India:

  • CPI for Industrial Workers (CPI-IW)
  • CPI for Agricultural Labourers (CPI-AL)
  • CPI for Rural Labourers (CPI-RL)
  • All-India Rural, Urban, and Combined CPI (Base: 2012 = 100)

B. Wholesale Price Index (WPI)

The Wholesale Price Index measures changes in the general price level of goods traded at the wholesale stage. Unlike CPI, it does not include services.

  • Base Year: 2011-12 = 100
  • Major Groups & Weights:
    Major Groups Weight (%)
    Primary Articles 22.62
    Fuel and Power 13.15
    Manufactured Products 64.23
    All Commodities (Headline Inflation) 100.00
  • Core Inflation: A measure of inflation that excludes volatile items like food and fuel (comprising about 55% of the WPI weight).

C. Index of Industrial Production (IIP)

The IIP is a quantity index that measures changes in the volume of industrial production in the economy.

  • Base Year: 2011-12 = 100
  • Formula (Laspeyre's variant):
    $$\text{IIP}_{01} = \frac{\sum q_{i1} W_i}{\sum W_i} \times 100$$
  • Sectoral Classification: Mining (14.4%), Manufacturing (77.6%), and Electricity (8.0%).
  • Eight Core Industries: Coal, crude oil, natural gas, refinery products, fertilizers, steel, cement, and electricity. These have a combined weight of 40.27% in the IIP.

D. SENSEX

The SENSEX (Bombay Stock Exchange Sensitive Index) is the benchmark stock market index of India. It consists of 30 stocks representing 13 key sectors of the economy, with 1978-79 as its base period.

4. Issues in Constructing Index Numbers

Constructing a reliable index number requires addressing several methodological challenges:

  1. Purpose of the Index: The design, selection of items, and weights must align with the specific objective (e.g., measuring retail inflation vs. wholesale inflation).
  2. Selection of Items: It is impossible to include all items; hence, a representative sample of commodities must be selected based on the target population's consumption patterns.
  3. Choice of Base Year: The base year should be a normal year, free from extreme events like wars, natural disasters, or hyperinflation, and should not belong to the too-distant past.
  4. Choice of Formula: The selection of aggregative or relative formulas (and the choice of weights) depends on data availability and the desired precision.
  5. Reliability of Data: The accuracy of the index depends heavily on the quality and reliability of the primary or secondary price data collected.
5. Economic Applications of Index Numbers

Index numbers are indispensable tools for macroeconomic analysis and policy formulation:

  • Measuring Inflation: Weekly or monthly inflation rates are calculated using WPI or CPI.
    $$\text{Weekly Inflation Rate} = \frac{X_t - X_{t-1}}{X_{t-1}} \times 100$$
    Where $X_t$ and $X_{t-1}$ refer to the WPI for the $t^{\text{th}}$ and $(t-1)^{\text{th}}$ weeks respectively.
  • Purchasing Power of Money: Measures the value of a currency unit in terms of its buying capacity relative to the base period.
    $$\text{Purchasing Power of Money} = \frac{1}{\text{Cost of Living Index}}$$
  • Deflating Nominal Wages (Real Wage Calculation): Used to adjust nominal wages for inflation to determine actual purchasing power.
    $$\text{Real Wage} = \frac{\text{Money Wage}}{\text{Cost of Living Index}} \times 100$$
  • Policy Formulation: CPI guides dearness allowance (DA) adjustments for employees, while WPI and IIP guide monetary and fiscal interventions.
