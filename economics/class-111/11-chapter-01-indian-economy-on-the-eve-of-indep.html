Class 111 — Chapter 1: Indian Economy on the Eve of Independence

1.1 Introduction & Low Level of Economic Development

The primary objective of studying the Indian economy on the eve of independence is to understand its historical roots under colonial rule, which lasted for nearly two centuries before ending on 15 August 1947.

Colonial Economic Exploitation

The sole purpose of the British colonial rule was to reduce India to a mere raw material supplier for Great Britain's rapidly expanding modern industrial base.

  • Prior to British rule, India had an independent economy with agriculture as the primary livelihood, alongside flourishing handicraft industries (cotton/silk textiles, metal, and precious stone works).
  • Muslin, particularly Daccai Muslin (the finest variety being malmal), was a world-famous textile originating in Bengal.

National and Per Capita Income Estimates

The colonial government never made any sincere attempt to estimate India's national income and per capita income. Notable individual estimators included:

  • Dadabhai Naoroji
  • William Digby
  • Findlay Shirras
  • V.K.R.V. Rao (whose estimates were considered the most systematic and significant)
  • R.C. Desai

Most studies indicated that during the first half of the twentieth century, India's aggregate real output growth was less than $2\%$ per year, with a meager per capita output growth of about $0.5\%$ per year.

1.2 Agricultural Sector

Under British rule, India's economy remained fundamentally agrarian, with about $85\%$ of the population deriving their livelihood directly or indirectly from agriculture.

Causes of Agricultural Stagnation

  • Zamindari System: Implemented in the Bengal Presidency, where the profit accruing from agriculture went to the zamindars rather than the cultivators. The main interest of the zamindars was rent collection, driven by the strict terms of the revenue settlement (fixed dates for depositing revenue, failing which they lost their rights).
  • Commercialisation of Agriculture: A shift from food crops to cash crops (like indigo) to feed British industries, which left farmers vulnerable to food shortages.
  • Low levels of technology, lack of irrigation facilities, and negligible use of fertilizers.
1.3 Industrial Sector

India could not develop a sound industrial base under colonial rule, resulting in systematic de-industrialisation.

De-industrialisation Motives & Impact

  1. To reduce India to an exporter of primary raw materials for British industries.
  2. To turn India into a consumer market for finished British manufactured goods.

This led to massive unemployment due to the decline of traditional handicrafts and created a new demand for cheap imports from Britain.

Modern Industry Growth

  • Modern industries began to emerge slowly in the second half of the 19th century.
  • Cotton textile mills (dominated by Indians) were located in Maharashtra and Gujarat, while jute mills (dominated by foreigners) were concentrated in Bengal.
  • The Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO) was incorporated in 1907.
  • There was a severe lack of capital goods industries to promote further industrialisation.
  • The public sector's role was highly restricted, confined only to railways, power generation, communications, and ports.
1.4 Foreign Trade

The restrictive trade and tariff policies of the colonial government transformed India's trade structure.

Trade Monopoly & Suez Canal

Britain maintained a monopoly control over India's exports and imports. More than half of India's foreign trade was restricted to Britain, with the remainder allowed with countries like China, Ceylon (Sri Lanka), and Persia (Iran).

The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 served as a direct trade route, reducing transportation costs and intensifying British control over Indian trade.

Drain of Indian Wealth

Although India generated a large export surplus, it came at a huge cost. Essential commodities became scarce domestically, and the export surplus was used to pay for British administrative expenses, war costs, and invisible imports, leading to a massive drain of wealth.

1.5 Demographic Condition

Demographic data collected through the first official census in 1881 highlighted severe underdevelopment.

Demographic Transition

  • Before 1921, India was in the first stage of demographic transition.
  • The year 1921 is regarded as the defining year (the "Year of Great Divide") marking the transition to the second stage.

Water and air-borne diseases were rampant due to highly inadequate public health facilities, leading to high mortality rates and extensive poverty.

1.6 Occupational Structure & Infrastructure

Occupational Structure

The distribution of the workforce across sectors showed little change:

  • Agricultural Sector: Accounted for the largest share ($70\text{--}75\%$).
  • Manufacturing Sector: Accounted for about $10\%$.
  • Services Sector: Accounted for $15\text{--}20\%$.
  • Regional variations: Madras, Bombay, and Bengal Presidencies saw a decline in agricultural dependence, while Orissa, Rajasthan, and Punjab saw an increase.

Infrastructure Development

Basic infrastructure (railways, ports, water transport, posts, and telegraphs) was developed not for public welfare, but to subserve colonial interests:

  • Roads were built primarily to mobilize the army and transport raw materials to ports.
  • The British introduced railways in 1850. This broke geographical barriers but also led to the commercialisation of agriculture, hurting village self-sufficiency.
  • The electric telegraph was introduced to maintain law and order, while postal services remained inadequate.
