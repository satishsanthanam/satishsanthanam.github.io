Class 111 — Chapter 4: Human Capital Formation in India

Introduction to Human Capital

The evolution of mankind highlights the unique human capacity to store and transmit knowledge. To perform tasks efficiently, societies require specialized training and skills. The labor skill of an educated person is significantly higher than that of an uneducated person, enabling the former to generate higher income and contribute more substantially to economic growth.

What is Human Capital?

Just as a country converts physical resources like land into physical capital (such as factories and machinery), it can also transform human resources into human capital (such as engineers, doctors, teachers, and nurses). Human capital represents the stock of skill, ability, expertise, education, and knowledge embodied in the nation's people at any given point in time.

Investment in human capital is essential to produce further human capital. We require competent, highly trained professionals (like professors and doctors) to train the next generation of human resources.

Sources of Human Capital

Human capital formation is the process of acquiring and increasing the number of persons who have the skills, education, and experience essential for the economic and political development of a country. The primary sources include:

  • Education: Individual investment in education is analogous to a company's investment in capital goods. It is undertaken to increase future productivity and income.
  • Health: Health is a critical input for development. A sick worker faces a loss of productivity and must abstain from work. Health expenditures directly increase the supply of a healthy labor force. Key forms of health expenditure include:
    • Preventive Medicine (e.g., vaccination)
    • Curative Medicine (medical intervention during illness)
    • Social Medicine (spread of health literacy)
    • Provision of clean drinking water and good sanitation
  • On-the-job Training: Firms invest in training their workers either in-house under supervision or off-campus. This expenditure is recovered through enhanced labor productivity that exceeds the cost of training.
  • Migration: People migrate from rural to urban areas or across countries in search of higher salaries and employment. Although migration involves transport and higher living costs, the enhanced earnings typically outweigh these costs.
  • Information: Spending to acquire information about the labor market, education, and health helps individuals make informed decisions regarding human capital investments and utilization.
Physical Capital vs. Human Capital

While both forms of capital result from conscious investment decisions, they differ significantly in their characteristics and economic nature:

Human Capital, Economic Growth, and Human Development

Human Capital and Economic Growth

Economic growth is defined as the increase in a country's real national income. An educated and healthy population contributes more to national income because of enhanced productivity, adaptability to new technologies, and a higher capacity for innovation. The Seventh Five Year Plan of India explicitly recognized that a trained and educated population is a vital asset for accelerating economic growth and social change.

Human Capital vs. Human Development

Though often used interchangeably, there is a clear distinction between these two concepts:

  • Human Capital: Views education and health as a means to increase labor productivity. Under this view, investment in humans is considered unproductive if it does not lead to an increase in the output of goods and services.
  • Human Development: Posits that education and health are integral to human well-being. Humans are viewed as ends in themselves. Welfare must be improved even if it does not directly lead to higher economic productivity, as every individual has a fundamental right to be literate and healthy.
State of Human Capital Formation in India

In India, expenditures on education and health are carried out simultaneously by the Union government, State governments, and local bodies. Government intervention is essential because these sectors require substantial, long-term investments that cannot be easily reversed, and private providers can easily acquire monopoly power and exploit consumers due to asymmetric information.

Regulatory Bodies in India

  • Education Sector: Facilitated by the Ministry of Education, NCERT (National Council of Educational Research and Training), UGC (University Grants Commission), and AICTE (All India Council for Technical Education).
  • Health Sector: Facilitated by the Ministry of Health, NMC (National Medical Commission), and ICMR (Indian Council for Medical Research).

Education Sector in India

Government expenditure on education is analyzed in two ways: as a percentage of total government expenditure (indicating its priority in government policy) and as a percentage of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) (indicating the proportion of national income committed to education).

  • During 1952-2020, education expenditure as a percentage of total government expenditure increased from 7.92% to 16.54%.
  • As a percentage of GDP, it increased from 0.64% to 4.47% over the same period. This remains below the 6% target recommended by the Education Commission (1964-66).
  • In 2009, the Government of India enacted the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, making free education a fundamental right for children aged 6-14 years.
  • The government also levies a 2% 'education cess' on all Union taxes to fund elementary education.

Future Prospects and Challenges
  • Education for All: Despite progress, the absolute number of illiterates in India remains high. Achieving 100% adult literacy is still a distant goal.
  • Gender Equity: The gap between male and female literacy rates is narrowing, which is positive for women's economic independence and social status. However, sustained efforts are needed to achieve complete equity.
  • Higher Education: The Indian education pyramid is steep, meaning very few students reach higher education. Furthermore, unemployment among educated youth (especially graduates) is high, highlighting the need to align higher education with employable skills.
