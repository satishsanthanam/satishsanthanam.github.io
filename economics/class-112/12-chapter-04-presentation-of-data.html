Class 112 — Chapter 4: Presentation of Data

1. Introduction to Data Presentation

Once data is collected and organised, it must be presented in a compact and comprehensible form to make it usable for analysis. There are three primary forms of presentation:

  • Textual or Descriptive Presentation: Data is described within the text.
  • Tabular Presentation: Data is presented in rows and columns.
  • Diagrammatic Presentation: Data is represented visually using diagrams, charts, or graphs.
2. Textual Presentation of Data

In a textual presentation, data is integrated directly into the narrative text. This method is highly suitable when the volume of data is small.

Drawbacks of Textual Presentation

  • Requires the reader to go through the entire text to comprehend the data.
  • Not suitable for large datasets as it becomes tedious and difficult to compare different data points.
3. Tabular Presentation of Data

Tabulation is the systematic organisation of data in rows (horizontal) and columns (vertical). It simplifies complex data and facilitates comparison and statistical analysis.

Types of Classification in Tabulation

Data used in tabulation can be classified into four distinct categories:

  • Qualitative Classification: Based on non-measurable attributes such as gender, nationality, social status, or physical status.
  • Quantitative Classification: Based on characteristics that can be measured numerically, such as age, height, income, or production.
  • Temporal Classification: Categorised according to time intervals (e.g., years, months, weeks, or hours).
  • Spatial Classification: Categorised based on geographical location (e.g., country, state, district, or town).

4. Parts of a Statistical Table

A well-constructed statistical table must contain the following essential components:

  1. Table Number: Assigned for identification and reference.
  2. Title: A clear, brief description of the table's contents, placed at the top.
  3. Captions (Column Headings): Designations given to explain the figures in each column.
  4. Stubs (Row Headings): Designations given to explain the figures in each row, located in the leftmost column.
  5. Body of the Table: The core part containing the actual numerical data.
  6. Unit of Measurement: Stated clearly (e.g., in crores, percentages, kilograms) to avoid ambiguity.
  7. Source Note: Indicates where the data was obtained, placed at the bottom.
  8. Footnote: Explains any specific features or exceptions in the data that are not self-explanatory.
5. Diagrammatic Presentation of Data

Diagrammatic presentation translates abstract numerical data into concrete, easily understandable visual forms. While diagrams may be less precise than tables, they are highly effective for visual comparison and identifying trends.

Types of Diagrams

  • Geometric Diagrams: Bar diagrams and Pie diagrams.
  • Frequency Diagrams: Histograms, Frequency Polygons, Frequency Curves, and Ogives.
  • Arithmetic Line Graphs: Time series graphs.
6. Geometric Diagrams

Bar Diagrams

Comprise equispaced and equiwidth rectangular bars where the height represents the magnitude of the data.

  • Simple Bar Diagram: Represents a single set of numerical data.
  • Multiple Bar Diagram: Used for comparing two or more sets of data simultaneously (e.g., import and export values over time).
  • Component Bar Diagram: Subdivides a single bar into its constituent parts to show both the total and the relative share of each component.

Pie Diagram

A circle whose area is proportionally divided among various components. Absolute values are converted into percentages and then into angular degrees:

$$\text{Angular Component} = \text{Percentage} \times 3.6^\circ$$
7. Frequency Diagrams

Used to represent grouped frequency distributions visually.

Histogram

A two-dimensional diagram consisting of adjacent rectangles. The class intervals are plotted on the X-axis, and the frequencies are represented on the Y-axis. It is drawn only for continuous variables. The highest point of a histogram can be used to graphically locate the Mode.

Frequency Polygon

A plane bounded by straight lines, constructed by joining the midpoints of the tops of the rectangles in a histogram.

Frequency Curve

A smooth, freehand curve drawn to pass as closely as possible through the points of a frequency polygon.

Ogive (Cumulative Frequency Curve)

Constructed by plotting cumulative frequencies against class limits. There are two types:

  • "Less than" Ogive: Cumulative frequencies are plotted against upper class limits in an increasing trend.
  • "More than" Ogive: Cumulative frequencies are plotted against lower class limits in a decreasing trend.

The intersection point of the "less than" and "more than" ogives graphically determines the Median.

8. Arithmetic Line Graphs (Time Series Graphs)

In an arithmetic line graph, time (hours, days, months, years) is plotted on the X-axis, and the value of the variable is plotted on the Y-axis. It is highly effective for understanding long-term trends, fluctuations, and periodicity in time-series data.
