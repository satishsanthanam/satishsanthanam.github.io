Economics: Class 12 (Part 1) — Chapter 2: National Income Accounting

2.1 Some Basic Concepts of Macroeconomics

Final Goods vs Intermediate Goods

In an economy, goods are classified based on their ultimate use:

  • Final Goods: Goods meant for final use that will not pass through any more stages of production or transformation. Examples include consumer purchases of food or clothing, and capital equipment purchased by firms.
  • Intermediate Goods: Goods used as raw materials or inputs for the production of other commodities (e.g., wheat used by a baker, steel sheets used to make automobiles).
  • Double Counting: To avoid exaggerating the value of economic activity, only the value of final goods is counted, as it already includes the value of intermediate inputs.

Consumption Goods, Capital Goods, and Durables

  • Consumption Goods: Goods and services consumed directly by ultimate consumers (e.g., food, recreation).
  • Consumer Durables: Consumption goods that are not extinguished immediately and have a relatively long life (e.g., televisions, automobiles).
  • Capital Goods: Durable goods used in the production process (e.g., machinery, tools, buildings) that form the backbone of production but do not get transformed themselves.

Stocks and Flows

  • Flows: Variables defined over a period of time (e.g., income, output, profits, change in stock).
  • Stocks: Variables defined at a particular point of time (e.g., total capital stock, inventory).

Depreciation and Net Investment

Gross Investment is the part of final output comprising capital goods. However, capital stock undergoes wear and tear, requiring maintenance and replacement. This regular wear and tear is called Depreciation.

$$\text{Net Investment} = \text{Gross Investment} - \text{Depreciation}$$
2.2 Circular Flow of Income & Methods of Calculating National Income

The Circular Flow Model

In a simplified economy without government, savings, or external trade, households provide factor services to firms and receive factor payments. Households then spend their entire income on goods and services produced by firms.

The four factors of production and their remunerations are:

Factor of ProductionRemuneration
Human LabourWage
CapitalInterest
EntrepreneurshipProfit
Land (Natural Resources)Rent

Three Methods of GDP Calculation

Because income flows in a circular fashion, aggregate economic activity can be measured at three different points:

  1. Product Method (Value Added Method): Measures the aggregate value of final goods and services produced by all firms.
  2. Expenditure Method: Measures the aggregate final spending received by firms.
  3. Income Method: Measures the sum total of all factor payments.
2.3 Detailed Estimation Methods

Product or Value Added Method

Value added is the net contribution of a firm to production:

$$\text{Value Added} = \text{Value of Production} - \text{Value of Intermediate Goods Used}$$
  • Gross Value Added (GVA): Includes depreciation.
  • Net Value Added (NVA): Excludes depreciation ($$\text{NVA} = \text{GVA} - \text{Depreciation}$$).
  • Inventory: Unsold finished/semi-finished goods or raw materials. It is a stock variable.
  • $$\text{Change in Inventories} \equiv \text{Production} - \text{Sales}$$
  • $$\text{GDP} \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{N} \text{GVA}_i$$

Expenditure Method

GDP is calculated by summing final expenditures on domestic output:

$$\text{GDP} \equiv C + I + G + (X - M)$$

Where:

  • $C$: Private final consumption expenditure
  • $I$: Investment expenditure (capital formation)
  • $G$: Government final consumption expenditure
  • $X - M$: Net exports (Exports minus Imports)

Income Method

GDP is calculated by summing all factor incomes earned by households:

$$\text{GDP} \equiv W + P + In + R$$

Where:

  • $W$: Wages and salaries
  • $P$: Gross profits
  • $In$: Interest payments
  • $R$: Rents

Factor Cost, Basic Prices, and Market Prices

  • $$\text{GVA at basic prices} = \text{GVA at factor cost} + \text{Net production taxes}$$
  • $$\text{GDP at market prices} = \text{GVA at basic prices} + \text{Net product taxes}$$
  • Production Taxes/Subsidies: Paid/received in relation to production, independent of volume (e.g., land revenue, stamp duty).
  • Product Taxes/Subsidies: Paid/received per unit of product (e.g., excise tax, GST, service tax).
2.4 Macroeconomic Identities & Aggregates

Key Identities

  • Gross National Product (GNP): $$\text{GNP} \equiv \text{GDP} + \text{Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)}$$
  • Net National Product (NNP): $$\text{NNP} \equiv \text{GNP} - \text{Depreciation}$$
  • National Income (NI): NNP at factor cost ($$\text{NNP}_{FC}$$).
  • Personal Income (PI): $$\text{PI} \equiv \text{NI} - \text{Undistributed profits} - \text{Net interest payments by households} - \text{Corporate tax} + \text{Transfer payments}$$
  • Personal Disposable Income (PDI): $$\text{PDI} \equiv \text{PI} - \text{Personal tax payments} - \text{Non-tax payments}$$
2.5 Nominal and Real GDP & Price Indices

Nominal vs Real GDP

  • Nominal GDP: Value of GDP at current prevailing prices.
  • Real GDP: Value of GDP evaluated at a constant set of prices (base year prices).
  • GDP Deflator: The ratio of nominal GDP to real GDP, serving as an index of price changes.
  • $$\text{GDP Deflator} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}} \times 100$$

Consumer Price Index (CPI) & Wholesale Price Index (WPI)

  • Consumer Price Index (CPI): Index of prices of a given basket of commodities bought by a representative consumer.
  • Wholesale Price Index (WPI): Index measuring change in the price of goods traded in bulk (wholesale).
2.6 GDP and Welfare

Limitations of GDP as a Welfare Indicator

GDP is often used as an index of well-being, but this can be misleading due to:

  1. Distribution of GDP: If GDP growth is concentrated in a few hands, overall welfare may not rise.
  2. Non-monetary Exchanges: Many productive activities (e.g., domestic work, barter transactions) are excluded from GDP.
  3. Externalities: Benefits or harms caused by an economic activity for which no payment or penalty is made (e.g., industrial pollution harming a river).
