Class 12 (Part 1) — Chapter 6: Open Economy Macroeconomics

Introduction to Open Economy

An open economy is one that interacts with other countries through various channels. Unlike a closed economy, it establishes linkages with the rest of the world through three primary markets:

  • Output Market: Trade in goods and services allows consumers and producers to choose between domestic and foreign options.
  • Financial Market: Allows the purchase of foreign financial assets, giving investors choices between domestic and foreign assets.
  • Labour Market: Allows firms to choose where to locate production and workers to choose where to work, though restricted by immigration laws.

Foreign trade influences aggregate demand in two ways: leakages (spending on imports escapes the circular flow, decreasing aggregate demand) and injections (exports enter the circular flow, increasing aggregate demand).

6.1 The Balance of Payments (BoP)

The Balance of Payments (BoP) records all economic transactions in goods, services, and assets between residents of a country and the rest of the world for a specified period, typically a year. It consists of two main accounts:

6.1.1 Current Account

The Current Account records trade in goods and services, as well as transfer payments. Its components include:

  • Trade in Goods: Exports and imports of physical goods (also called visible trade).
  • Trade in Services: Includes factor income (net earnings on land, labour, capital) and non-factor income (shipping, banking, tourism, software services).
  • Transfer Payments: Receipts that residents receive for free, without providing any goods or services in return (gifts, remittances, grants).

The balance on the current account can be classified as:

Current Account StatusConditionEconomic Meaning
Current Account Surplus$$\text{Receipts} > \text{Payments}$$The nation is a net lender to the rest of the world.
Balanced Current Account$$\text{Receipts} = \text{Payments}$$Equilibrium in current transactions.
Current Account Deficit$$\text{Receipts} < \text{Payments}$$The nation is a net borrower from the rest of the world.

Key components of the Current Account Balance:

  • Balance of Trade (BOT): The difference between the value of exports and imports of physical goods. $$BOT = \text{Value of Exports} - \text{Value of Imports}$$
  • Net Invisibles: The difference between the value of exports and imports of invisibles (services, transfers, and income flows).

6.1.2 Capital Account

The Capital Account records all international transactions of assets (money, stocks, bonds, government debt, real estate). It tracks how a country finances its current account imbalances.

  • Capital Inflows: Receipts from abroad (loans, sale of domestic assets/shares to foreigners). These are entered as credit items.
  • Capital Outflows: Payments made abroad (repayment of loans, purchase of foreign assets/shares). These are entered as debit items.

Components of the Capital Account include:

  • Investments: Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) and Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI).
  • External Borrowings: External commercial borrowings and short-term debt.
  • External Assistance: Government aid, multilateral, and bilateral loans.

6.1.3 Balance of Payments Surplus and Deficit

A current account deficit must be financed by a capital account surplus or net capital inflow:

$$\text{Current Account} + \text{Capital Account} \equiv 0$$

If the sum of current and capital accounts is not zero, the gap is bridged using foreign exchange reserves managed by the central bank:

  • Official Reserve Transactions: The purchase or sale of foreign exchange reserves by the central bank to balance the BoP.
  • Autonomous Transactions: Transactions undertaken for independent economic reasons (e.g., profit-maximisation). These are called "above the line" items.
  • Accommodating Transactions: Transactions determined by the net consequences of autonomous transactions to bridge the BoP gap. These are called "below the line" items.
  • Errors and Omissions: A balancing item representing statistical discrepancies in recording international transactions.
6.2 The Foreign Exchange Market

The foreign exchange market is the market in which national currencies are traded for one another. The price of one currency in terms of another is the foreign exchange rate.

6.2.1 Demand and Supply of Foreign Exchange

  • Demand for Foreign Exchange arises from the need to purchase foreign goods/services, send gifts abroad, or buy foreign financial assets. A rise in the exchange rate reduces the demand for foreign exchange.
  • Supply of Foreign Exchange flows into the home country from exports, foreign tourists, inward gifts/transfers, and foreign investment. A rise in the exchange rate increases the supply of foreign exchange.

6.2.2 Determination of the Exchange Rate

Exchange rates can be determined through different systems:

  • Flexible Exchange Rate (Floating Rate): Determined by the market forces of demand and supply without central bank intervention.
    • Depreciation: A fall in the value of domestic currency in terms of foreign currency (e.g., exchange rate rises from Rs 50 to Rs 70 per dollar).
    • Appreciation: A rise in the value of domestic currency in terms of foreign currency.
  • Fixed Exchange Rate: The government or central bank fixes the exchange rate at a particular level.
    • Devaluation: An official government action that increases the exchange rate, making the domestic currency cheaper.
    • Revaluation: An official government action that decreases the exchange rate, making the domestic currency costlier.
  • Managed Floating (Dirty Floating): A hybrid system where the exchange rate is primarily market-determined, but the central bank intervenes occasionally to moderate extreme fluctuations.

6.2.3 Long-Run Exchange Rates: Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

According to the Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) theory, exchange rates between two currencies adjust in the long run to reflect differences in the price levels of the two countries, ensuring that a basket of goods costs the same in both nations when measured in a common currency.

Appendix 6.1: Determination of Equilibrium Income in an Open Economy

In an open economy, the national income identity includes net exports ($$NX$$):

$$Y = C + I + G + X - M$$

Where the import function is defined as:

$$M = \bar{M} + mY$$

Here, $$m$$ is the marginal propensity to import, representing the fraction of an extra unit of income spent on imports ($$0 < m < 1$$).

The equilibrium income ($$Y^*$$) is determined as:

$$Y^* = \frac{1}{1 - c + m} \bar{A}$$

Where $$\bar{A}$$ represents total autonomous expenditure ($$\bar{C} - cT + \bar{I} + \bar{G} + \bar{X} - \bar{M}$$).

The open economy multiplier is given by:

$$\text{Multiplier} = \frac{1}{1 - c + m}$$

Because $$m > 0$$, the open economy multiplier is smaller than the closed economy multiplier ($$\frac{1}{1-c}$$). Imports act as an additional leakage from the circular flow of domestic income.
