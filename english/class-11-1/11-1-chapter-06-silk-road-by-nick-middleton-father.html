English: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 6: Silk Road & Father to Son

Silk Road: Narrative & Travelogue Analysis

Nick Middleton's Silk Road is a detailed travelogue documenting the author's journey from the rolling hills of Ravu to the high-altitude settlement of Darchen, aiming to complete the sacred kora (pilgrimage) around Mount Kailash. The narrative explores the physical, geographical, and psychological challenges of high-altitude travel along ancient trade routes.

Chronological Journey & Key Milestones
  1. Departure from Ravu: The journey begins on a crisp morning. Lhamo , a local woman, presents the narrator with a long-sleeved sheepskin coat ( drokba coat) to protect him against the biting high-altitude winds.
  2. The Changtang Plains: Tsetan takes a shortcut southwest across vast, arid plains. The travelers encounter wild gazelles and large herds of wild asses, known locally as kyang , galloping in tight, synchronized formations.
  3. Nomadic Settlements & Tibetan Mastiffs: Passing isolated nomad tents, they encounter ferocious Tibetan mastiffs acting as guard dogs. These massive, shaggy beasts chase the vehicle fearlessly, demonstrating why they were prized as tribute in China's imperial courts.
  4. High-Altitude Passes & Snow Blockages: The trail climbs steeply, hugging frozen riverbanks. At $5,210$ meters, they encounter a major snow drift blocking the road. Tsetan, Daniel, and the narrator throw dirt over the icy surface to gain traction. They eventually reach the top of the pass at $5,515$ meters, marked by a traditional cairn of rocks festooned with prayer flags.
  5. The Grim Town of Hor: Descending to the plateau, they reach Hor on the shores of the sacred Lake Manasarovar . Contrary to historical, idealized accounts by travelers like Ekai Kawaguchi and Sven Hedin, the narrator finds Hor to be a grim, dusty, and rubbish-strewn settlement. Here, Daniel departs for Lhasa.
  6. Arrival at Darchen & Medical Crisis: They reach Darchen, where the narrator suffers a severe respiratory crisis due to the cold, dust, and extreme altitude ($4,760$ meters). Unable to breathe while lying down, he spends a sleepless night sitting upright, fearing he might die.
  7. Darchen Medical College: Tsetan takes the narrator to a local medical college. A Tibetan doctor in ordinary clothes diagnoses him with a cold and altitude sickness, prescribing a five-day course of traditional Tibetan medicine (including a cinnamon-tasting powder and brown pellets resembling sheep dung) which successfully cures him.
  8. Meeting Norbu: Left alone after Tsetan returns to Lhasa, the narrator feels isolated due to the lack of English-speaking pilgrims. He meets Norbu , a Tibetan academic working in Beijing, at a local cafe. They decide to team up to complete the kora together.
Character & Literary Profiles

Literary Profile: Nick Middleton (The Narrator)

  • Traits & Temperament: Observant, academic, physically vulnerable to extreme environments, yet determined to complete his spiritual and physical quest.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A geographer and travel writer accustomed to analytical observation, looking at landscapes through both scientific and aesthetic lenses.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the modern, rational outsider seeking to understand ancient spiritual traditions ( kora ) while grappling with the harsh realities of nature.

Literary Profile: Tsetan (The Guide & Driver)

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly resourceful, calm under pressure, deeply familiar with the treacherous terrain, and practical.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A local Tibetan driver and guide whose livelihood depends on navigating the high-altitude routes for tourists and pilgrims.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Embodies the resilient, spiritual, yet pragmatic nature of the local hill-folk. His Buddhist worldview is highlighted when he notes that while death is not a spiritual tragedy, it is "bad for business."

Literary Profile: Norbu (The Academic Companion)

  • Traits & Temperament: Jovial, self-deprecating, highly educated, and practical. He openly admits his physical limitations ("too fat" and "tummy too big").
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A Tibetan scholar working at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences in Beijing, who has written extensively about the Kailash kora but never performed it.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the intellectualization of culture. His partnership with the narrator highlights a shared secular, academic approach to a deeply religious pilgrimage.
Thematic & Environmental Commentary

The travelogue presents a stark contrast between the idealized, sacred descriptions of Tibetan landscapes in religious cosmology and the grim, polluted reality of modern settlements:

  • The Myth vs. Reality of Lake Manasarovar: While ancient Hindu and Buddhist cosmology venerates Manasarovar as the source of four great rivers (Indus, Ganges, Sutlej, and Brahmaputra), the narrator finds its immediate shore-town, Hor, to be a miserable, rubbish-strewn wasteland devoid of vegetation.
  • The Commercialization of Sacred Spaces: The presence of plastic, refuse, and rudimentary concrete buildings highlights the ecological footprint of modern tourism and trade along the ancient Silk Road.
  • Human Resilience and Adaptation: The lifestyle of the drokbas (nomads) and the resourcefulness of drivers like Tsetan demonstrate how human communities adapt to survive in one of the most inhospitable environments on Earth.

Father to Son: Poetic Analysis

Elizabeth Jennings' poem Father to Son is a poignant, universal exploration of the generational gap, emotional alienation, and the breakdown of communication between a father and his growing son, despite living under the same roof.

Stanza-by-Stanza Thematic Breakdown
  • Stanza 1: The Void of Non-Understanding: The father laments that he does not understand his child, despite having lived together in the same house for years. He attempts to reconstruct a relationship based on memories of the son as a small child, but realizes those early connections are lost.
  • Stanza 2: The Stranger Dynamic: The father questions whether he has mismanaged the child's upbringing ("sown the seed" on land that is not his). They speak like strangers with no mutual understanding. Although the child is physically "built to [the father's] design," they share no common interests or emotional ground.
  • Stanza 3: The Prodigal Son Motif: Silence surrounds their interactions. The father expresses a desperate willingness to accept his son even if he is "prodigal" (rebellious or wasteful), preferring him to return home rather than strike out independently to build a separate world. The father is ready to forgive and rebuild their bond.
  • Stanza 4: Mutual Grief and Longing: Both father and son must coexist on the same globe. The son speaks, expressing his own frustration: his anger grows out of his grief over the emotional distance. The poem ends on a bittersweet note of mutual longing, with both putting out an "empty hand," searching for a path to forgiveness.
Key Excerpts & Interpretations

"This child is built to my design / Yet what he loves I cannot share."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This couplet highlights the tragic irony of parenthood. While the son inherits the father's physical traits and genetic blueprint ("built to my design"), his internal world, passions, and values ("what he loves") remain completely inaccessible to the father, emphasizing that biological connection does not guarantee emotional intimacy.

"We each put out an empty hand, / Longing for something to forgive."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The closing lines of the poem symbolize the tragic stalemate of their relationship. The "empty hand" represents both their lack of meaningful connection and their mutual desire to reach out. The paradox of "longing for something to forgive" suggests that both parties are searching for a gesture of vulnerability or an excuse to break the silence and reconcile.

Literary Devices & Poetic Techniques
Literary Device Exact Textual Example Contextual Meaning & Stylistic Purpose
Metaphor "The seed I spent or sown it where / The land is his and none of mine?" Compares the upbringing and nurturing of the child to sowing a seed, highlighting the father's feeling of having lost control over the "soil" (the son's mind).
Allusion "I would have / Him prodigal, returning to / His father's house" Alludes to the biblical parable of the Prodigal Son, emphasizing the father's unconditional love and readiness to forgive.
Personification "Silence surrounds us." Personifies silence as an active, oppressive force that physically isolates the father and son from one another.
Synecdoche / Imagery "We each put out an empty hand" The "empty hand" represents the physical and emotional yearning for reconciliation and the void left by their lack of communication.
Textbook Exercises & Discussion Points
Understanding the Text (Silk Road)
  • Why is the article titled 'Silk Road'? The title refers to the historic network of Eurasian trade routes that connected East and West. The narrator travels along these ancient paths, which are now used for pilgrimage ( kora ) and local trade, highlighting the historical and cultural continuity of the region.
  • Why were Tibetan mastiffs popular in China's imperial courts? These dogs were highly valued for their ferocity, massive size, and hunting capabilities. They were brought along the Silk Road from Tibet as tribute to the Chinese emperors.
  • Contrast of Hor with earlier accounts: Earlier travelers like Ekai Kawaguchi and Sven Hedin were deeply moved by the pristine, sacred beauty of Lake Manasarovar. In contrast, the narrator finds Hor to be a grim, dusty, and polluted town, reflecting the modern ecological degradation of the area.
  • The Narrator's disappointment with Darchen: The narrator arrived at the very beginning of the pilgrimage season, hoping to find a bustling town filled with pilgrims to accompany him. Instead, the town was quiet, dusty, and lacked English speakers, leaving him feeling isolated and helpless.
Think It Out (Father to Son)
  • Is the experience personal or universal? While written from a personal perspective, the poem addresses the highly universal theme of the generational gap and the breakdown of communication between parents and their adolescent or adult children.
  • How is the father's helplessness portrayed? The father's helplessness is conveyed through his admission of complete ignorance regarding his son's inner life, his inability to share his son's interests, and the oppressive silence that dominates their shared living space.
  • Phrases indicating distance: Key phrases include "We speak like strangers," "there's no sign of understanding in the air," "what he loves I cannot share," and "Silence surrounds us."
