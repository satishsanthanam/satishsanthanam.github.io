English: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 2: The Address

Chapter Overview & Historical Context

The short story The Address by Marga Minco is a poignant, semi-autobiographical narrative set in the aftermath of World War II in Holland . It depicts the profound psychological and material devastation experienced by a Jewish family during the German occupation. The plot follows a young woman, the daughter of Mrs. S , who returns to her homeland after the war to reclaim her family's cherished household belongings. These items had been taken for "safekeeping" by an opportunistic acquaintance, Mrs. Dorling , before the family was forced to flee or was deported. The narrative explores themes of grief, the pain of remembrance, the cold reality of post-war survival, and the ultimate necessity of letting go of the past to move forward.

Literary Profile: Marga Minco (Author)

  • Traits & Temperament: Observant, deeply reflective, emotionally restrained, and resilient. Her writing style is characterized by minimalist prose, stark realism, and a lack of sentimentalism.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born into a Jewish family in the Netherlands, Minco was the sole survivor of her family during the Holocaust. Her experiences of hiding, loss, and survival form the core of her literary oeuvre.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Existential displacement, the burden of memory, the cold indifference of survivors, and the transformation of familiar domestic spaces into alien landscapes.
Detailed Plot Narrative & Chronological Beats
1. The First Visit: Cold Reception at Number 46

The story opens in media res with the narrator arriving at Marconi Street, Number 46 . She confronts Mrs. Dorling , who opens the door only a chink, displaying absolute coldness and a refusal to recognize her.

  • The narrator introduces herself as Mrs. S's daughter , but Mrs. Dorling pretends not to know her, attempting to close the door.
  • The narrator's doubts about having the wrong address are instantly dispelled when she notices Mrs. Dorling is wearing her mother's green knitted cardigan , its wooden buttons faded from washing.
  • Recognizing the narrator's realization, Mrs. Dorling half-hides behind the door and dismisses her, saying, "I can't see you. Another time."
  • The narrator is left standing on the step, realizing that Mrs. Dorling never expected any of her family to survive and return.
2. Flashback: The Origin of the Address and Mrs. Dorling's Opportunism

As the narrator walks back to the station, she recalls how she obtained the address during the first half of the war.

  • During a brief visit home, the narrator noticed that several valuable household items were missing. Her mother then introduced her to Mrs. Dorling , an old acquaintance who had suddenly re-entered their lives.
  • Mrs. Dorling insisted on taking their valuable possessions—including table silver, antique plates, large vases, and fine crockery—to her own house to "save" them from being lost or destroyed if the family had to leave.
  • The narrator's mother, characterized by her naive trust and gratitude, felt insulted when the narrator questioned Mrs. Dorling's intentions, emphasizing the physical risk Mrs. Dorling took by carrying heavy suitcases of their belongings out of their house.
  • Before the narrator left, her mother made her memorize the address: Marconi Street, Number 46 .
3. The Second Visit: Confronting the Ghostly Past

After the war, the narrator initially avoids reclaiming her family's belongings, fearing the emotional trauma of confronting objects associated with her deceased family. However, as life gradually normalizes, curiosity and a desire to touch her past lead her back to the address.

  • On her second visit, Mrs. Dorling is out, and her fifteen-year-old daughter opens the door, inviting the narrator inside.
  • The narrator is led down a passage where she spots an old-fashioned iron Hanukkah candle-holder , an object they rarely used because it was too cumbersome.
  • Upon entering the living room, the narrator is struck by a wave of horror and oppression. She finds herself in a room she "knew and did not know," surrounded by her family's possessions arranged in a tasteless, ugly, and muggy atmosphere.
  • She sits at the table and rubs the woollen tablecloth , finding the familiar burn mark on its edge that had never been repaired.
  • The girl serves tea from her family's white tea-pot with a gold border on the lid, using the family's antique silver spoons and cutlery, completely unaware of their true history and ownership.
4. The Resolution: The Act of Letting Go

The surreal experience of seeing her family's sacred memories reduced to mundane, tasteless household utility items for strangers triggers a profound psychological shift in the narrator.

  • She realizes that these objects, once deeply linked to her mother and her former life, have lost their value and meaning now that they are severed from their original context and placed in a strange environment.
  • She looks at her current reality: she lives in a small rented room where the shreds of black-out paper still hang on the windows, and there is no space for large antique plates or heavy silver cutlery.
  • Abruptly jumping up to catch her train, she leaves the house without waiting for Mrs. Dorling, leaving all her family's physical legacy behind.
  • She resolves to forget the address, concluding that of all the painful memories she must discard, forgetting Marconi Street, Number 46 will be the easiest.
Character Profiles

Literary Profile: The Narrator (Mrs. S's Daughter)

  • Traits & Temperament: Sensitive, observant, initially traumatized, but ultimately pragmatic and strong-willed. She possesses a deep emotional intelligence that allows her to recognize the futility of clinging to material remnants of a lost world.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A Jewish war survivor who lost her entire family and her home during the Holocaust. She now lives a modest, solitary life in a small rented room.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the psychological journey of post-war survivors—moving from trauma and denial to confrontation, and finally to acceptance and liberation.

Literary Profile: Mrs. Dorling

  • Traits & Temperament: Cold, opportunistic, materialistic, manipulative, and devoid of empathy. She exploits the vulnerability of her Jewish acquaintances for material gain under the guise of benevolence.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A non-Jewish Dutch woman who used the war to acquire high-value antique furniture, silver, and crockery from fleeing families, successfully integrating these stolen goods into her post-war middle-class life.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the moral degradation, greed, and complicity of bystanders who profited from the persecution of their neighbors during wartime.

Literary Profile: Mrs. S (The Mother)

  • Traits & Temperament: Kind, trusting, naive, and deeply attached to her domestic possessions. She is unable to foresee the true depth of human malice or the impending catastrophe of the war.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A wealthy Jewish woman of refined taste, proud of her antique collections and silver, who perished during the war.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the pre-war bourgeois domesticity, innocence, and the tragic vulnerability of those who placed their trust in fair-weather friends.
Thematic & Symbolic Matrix

The following matrix details the key literary devices, symbols, and thematic contrasts utilized by Marga Minco to convey the emotional weight of the narrative:

Literary Device / Symbol Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
The Green Knitted Cardigan "She was wearing my mother’s green knitted cardigan. The wooden buttons were rather pale from washing." Serves as irrefutable physical proof of Mrs. Dorling's identity and her appropriation of the family's intimate personal belongings. Acts as a catalyst for the narrator's realization of betrayal, bridging the gap between memory and current reality.
The Hanukkah Candle-holder "An old-fashioned iron Hanukkah candle-holder hung next to a mirror. We never used it because it was much more cumbersome than a single candlestick." A symbol of Jewish religious identity and heritage, hanging inappropriately in a non-Jewish home, stripped of its sacred context. Highlights the cultural and spiritual displacement of the Jewish community's legacy in post-war Europe.
The Burn Mark on the Tablecloth "I followed the lines of the pattern. Somewhere on the edge there should be a burn mark that had never been repaired." Represents the physical scars of the past. The unrepaired burn mark mirrors the narrator's own internal, unhealed trauma from the war. Establishes a sensory connection to the narrator's childhood, validating her memories amidst a hostile environment.
Situational Irony "I didn’t know the cutlery we ate off every day was silver." / "I bet you don’t know it is either." The narrator grew up using silver cutlery without realizing its material value, while Mrs. Dorling's family now uses it ostentatiously, yet remains ignorant of its emotional history. Contrasts the narrator's innocent, unpretentious relationship with her home against the greedy, superficial materialism of Mrs. Dorling.
Key Excerpts & Critical Commentary

‘Have you come back?’ said the woman. ‘I thought that no one had come back.’

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This statement, uttered by Mrs. Dorling upon realizing the narrator's identity, is the emotional and historical crux of the story. It reveals the dark underbelly of post-war European society. Mrs. Dorling's words betray her expectation that the entire Jewish family had perished in concentration camps or during the war, which would have allowed her to keep their valuable belongings permanently without guilt or consequence. The quote highlights the cold, transactional indifference of bystanders who viewed the tragedy of the Holocaust as an opportunity for material self-enrichment, showing no joy or relief at the survival of a human being.

I resolved to forget the address. Of all the things I had to forget, that would be the easiest.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This concluding line marks the narrator's psychological liberation. Throughout the story, she is burdened by the duty of memory and the desire to reclaim her past. However, seeing her family's sacred objects in Mrs. Dorling's tasteless home makes her realize that physical objects are only valuable when they are animated by the love and presence of the people who owned them. In her small, post-war rented room, these objects would only serve as painful, suffocating reminders of a dead family. By choosing to forget the address, she actively rejects the victimhood of nostalgia, choosing instead to survive and rebuild her life on her own terms.

NCERT Textbook Questions & Analytical Solutions
  1. ‘Have you come back?’ said the woman. ‘I thought that no one had come back.’ Does this statement give some clue about the story? If yes, what is it?
    Yes, this statement provides a vital clue about the historical and emotional backdrop of the story. It indicates that the narrative is set in the aftermath of a devastating war (specifically, the Holocaust in occupied Holland). The woman, Mrs. Dorling, had taken away the valuable possessions of the narrator's Jewish family under the pretext of safekeeping. Her remark reveals her complicit expectation that the family would not survive the war, thereby leaving her in permanent, uncontested possession of their wealth. It exposes the greed, lack of empathy, and moral decay of people who profited from the tragedies of others during wartime.
  2. The story is divided into pre-War and post-War \times. What hardships do you think the girl underwent during these \times?
    The narrator underwent distinct physical and psychological hardships across both eras:
    • Pre-War Hardships: She faced the constant threat of persecution, the anxiety of losing her home, and the gradual dismantling of her family's secure life. She had to watch her mother hand over their cherished heirlooms to a manipulative acquaintance out of fear of losing everything. She also experienced the disruption of her normal life, living in fear of deportation.
    • Post-War Hardships: She suffered the devastating loss of her entire family, leaving her completely alone. She faced severe material deprivation, living in a small, bleak rented room with black-out papers still on the windows. Psychologically, she had to cope with survivor's guilt, the trauma of bereavement, and the painful process of confronting her past before she could find the strength to move on.
  3. Why did the narrator of the story want to forget the address?
    The narrator wanted to forget the address because she realized that the physical objects she had sought to reclaim were no longer connected to the warm, loving past she remembered. Severed from her mother and their original home, and arranged in Mrs. Dorling's tasteless, alien environment, the objects had lost their soul and value. Furthermore, her current reality—a small rented room—had no space for such grand material possessions. Keeping them would only prolong her grief and anchor her to a tragic past. Forgetting the address was an act of self-preservation, allowing her to break free from trauma and step into the future.
  4. ‘The Address’ is a story of human predicament that follows war. Comment.
    War does not end with the signing of peace treaties; its psychological and social devastation persists for generations. "The Address" beautifully illustrates this human predicament:
    • Loss of Identity and Belonging: The narrator is left homeless and familyless, searching for her identity in the material remnants of her past.
    • The Devaluation of Memories: The story shows how war dehumanizes relationships. Sacred family heirlooms are reduced to mere commodities used by opportunistic strangers.
    • The Moral Crisis of Society: It highlights the ethical degradation of survivors like Mrs. Dorling, who lose their humanity and empathy in the pursuit of material gain.
    • The Struggle to Rebuild: The ultimate human predicament is the choice between clinging to a painful, unrecoverable past or summoning the courage to let go and start anew in a scarred world.
