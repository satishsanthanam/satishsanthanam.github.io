English: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 4: Birth

Chapter Overview & Context

This chapter, titled Birth , is an excerpt from A.J. Cronin 's famous novel, The Citadel . It follows the intense, life-altering experience of a young medical graduate, Andrew Manson , in the mining town of Blaenelly .

Character Profiles

Literary Profile: Andrew Manson

  • Traits & Temperament: Dedicated, emotionally conflicted, exhausted, intuitive, and highly persistent under extreme pressure.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Newly graduated medical assistant to Dr. Edward Page in the impoverished Welsh mining town of Blaenelly.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the transition from textbook theory to practical, life-saving medical reality; struggles with the disillusionment of love vs. the harsh realities of domestic life.

Literary Profile: Joe Morgan

  • Traits & Temperament: Anxious, deeply loving, humble, and trusting.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A burly driller in Blaenelly, married to Susan for nearly twenty years, eagerly awaiting their first child.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents working-class hope, domestic devotion, and the profound vulnerability of expectant parenthood.
Narrative Progression & Plot Beats
1. The Midnight Call & Emotional Conflict
  1. Andrew Manson returns to Bryngower late at night after a disappointing evening with his beloved, Christine .
  2. He finds Joe Morgan waiting anxiously outside his surgery. Joe's wife, Susan Morgan , is in labor before her expected time.
  3. Andrew accompanies Joe to 12 Blaina Terrace . Joe refuses to enter, expressing his deep anxiety and trust in Andrew.
  4. Inside, Andrew is met by Susan's mother (a wise, grey-haired woman of nearly seventy) and an elderly midwife.
  5. Andrew sits by the kitchen fire, drinking tea. He is consumed by heavy, muddled thoughts about failed marriages around him (Bramwell, Edward Page, Denny) while desperately wanting to believe in the idyllic nature of his love for Christine.
2. The Crisis of Birth & Medical Dilemma
  1. At half-past three, the midwife calls Andrew upstairs. After a long, harsh struggle, the child is born lifeless.
  2. Andrew is horrified. He faces a severe medical dilemma: the mother, Susan , is collapsing and nearly pulseless, while the newborn child is completely unresponsive.
  3. Instinctively, Andrew prioritizes the mother. He injects medicine, works frantically to restore her heart, and successfully stabilizes her.
  4. He then turns his attention to the child, which the midwife had hidden under the bed, assuming it to be stillborn.
3. The Miracle of Resuscitation
  1. Andrew retrieves the child, noting its limp, warm, and white body—a classic case of asphyxia pallida (suffocation due to lack of oxygen).
  2. Recalling a clinical case from his training at the Samaritan Hospital , he demands hot and cold water in separate basins.
  3. He plunges the baby alternately into the icy and steaming water, acting like a "crazy juggler" for fifteen minutes, but the child remains lifeless.
  4. Refusing to give up, Andrew rubs the child with a rough towel and performs manual chest compressions.
  5. Suddenly, the child's chest heaves, a bubble of mucus forms, the skin turns pink, and the baby cries, signaling life.
Thematic & Stylistic Analysis
Key Themes
  • Textbook Medicine vs. Practical Instinct: Andrew's success relies not just on academic knowledge, but on desperate, intuitive persistence and physical effort that goes beyond standard textbook procedures.
  • The Sanctity of Life: The intense struggle to save both mother and child highlights the profound value of human life and the heavy responsibility borne by medical practitioners.
  • Emotional Maturity & Professional Duty: Andrew sets aside his personal romantic disillusionment and physical exhaustion to perform his duty, achieving a state of self-actualization.
Literary Devices & Vocabulary
Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Colloquialism "Doctor, bach" "Bach" is a Welsh term of endearment meaning "little" or "dear". Establishes the authentic regional Welsh setting and the grandmother's affectionate, pleading tone.
Simile "white and soft as tallow" Comparing the lifeless baby's body to animal fat. Conveys the cold, limp, and seemingly hopeless state of the newborn, emphasizing the severity of asphyxia pallida.
Simile "like some crazy juggler" Describing Andrew's frantic movement of the baby between hot and cold water. Highlights the desperate, unorthodox, and highly physical nature of his medical intervention.
Metaphor "a frantic race against her ebbing strength" Comparing the medical resuscitation of Susan to a physical race. Heightens the narrative tension and underscores the life-or-death stakes of the situation.
Key Excerpt & Interpretation

"I’ve done something; oh, God! I’ve done something real at last."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This final internal monologue marks Andrew's transition from a cynical, exhausted assistant to a true healer. It represents his realization that clinical success and saving a life provide a profound, existential fulfillment that transcends his personal romantic anxieties and academic theories.

Study Questions & Comprehension
  1. Why does Andrew say, "I've done something real at last"? It signifies his triumph over death, proving his worth as a doctor beyond mere textbook knowledge and giving him a sense of genuine purpose.
  2. What is the difference between textbook medicine and practical medicine as shown in the story? Textbook medicine provides the diagnosis (asphyxia pallida) and basic concepts, but practical medicine requires relentless effort, quick decision-making under pressure, and intuitive persistence when standard methods seem to fail.
  3. How does the setting of Blaenelly influence the mood of the story? The dark, quiet, and isolated mining town creates a somber, tense atmosphere that mirrors Andrew's internal exhaustion and the high-stakes struggle inside the humble home.
