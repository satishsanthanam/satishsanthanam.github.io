English: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 5: The Tale of Melon City

Chapter Overview & Literary Context

Published in Vikram Seth's 1981 collection Mappings and later included in his Collected Poems , The Tale of Melon City is a humorous, narrative poem written in rhyming couplets. The poem is a biting satire on governance, blind adherence to law, and the absurdity of absolute power. Set in an ancient, fictional kingdom, it mocks the concepts of justice, bureaucracy, and the public's indifference to the nature of their rulers, provided they are left in peace.

Literary Profile: Vikram Seth

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly versatile, witty, and observant. Seth uses light-hearted verse to convey deep socio-political critiques.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: An acclaimed Indian poet and novelist, educated at Oxford, Stanford, and Nanjing University. His works often blend traditional poetic forms with modern, ironic sensibilities.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Irony, social satire, human folly, and the critique of institutional systems.
Chronological Plot Analysis
1. The Proclamation and Construction of the Arch

The just and placid King decrees that a triumphal arch must be built across the major thoroughfare. The purpose of this arch is to edify spectators and project the majesty of the crown. The workmen build it according to the royal command.

2. The Crown Incident and the Chain of Blame
  1. The King rides down the thoroughfare to inspect the arch and edify the crowd.
  2. Because the arch is built too low, it knocks the King's crown off his head.
  3. Perceiving this as a major disgrace, the King demands that the chief of builders be hanged.
  4. The chief of builders shifts the blame to the workmen , claiming they built it poorly.
  5. The King halts his execution and orders the workmen to be hanged instead.
  6. The workmen argue that the bricks were made of the wrong size, shifting the blame to the masons .
  7. The masons claim they only followed the plans drawn up by the architect .
  8. The architect boldly points out that the King himself made amendments to the original plans when they were shown to him.
3. The Search for Counsel and the Wisest Man

Faced with the accusation that he is responsible for his own disgrace, the King is stumped. He demands that the wisest man in the country be brought to court to resolve this tricky legal dilemma. The wisest man, so old that he can neither walk nor see, is carried to the Royal Court. He decrees that since the arch knocked the crown off, the arch itself must be hanged .

4. The Restless Crowd and the Royal Execution

As the arch is led to the scaffold, a Councillor objects, arguing that it would be a shameful disgrace to hang something that has touched the royal head. The King agrees, but notices that the gathered crowd is growing restless and muttering. Fearing a public revolt, the King declares that the nation demands a hanging, and someone must be executed immediately. A noose is set up high, and every citizen is measured against it. The only person tall enough to fit the high noose is the King himself. By royal decree, the King is hanged.

5. The Coronation of the Melon

The Ministers are relieved that an execution took place, preventing the town from turning against the Crown. Following custom, they declare that the next person to pass the City Gate will choose the new ruler. An idiot passes by, and when asked who should be King, he replies, A melon (his standard answer to all questions because he likes melons). The Ministers crown the melon, place it on the throne, and the kingdom becomes Melon City .

Character Profiles

Literary Profile: The King

  • Traits & Temperament: Described ironically as "just and placid." In reality, he is foolish, easily swayed, obsessed with public appearances, and lacks genuine leadership or critical thinking skills.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Absolute monarch of a kingdom where his word is law, yet he is entirely dependent on the whims of his subjects and advisors.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the absurdity of autocratic rulers who prioritize superficial rituals of justice over actual governance.

Literary Profile: The Ministers & Councillors

  • Traits & Temperament: Practical-minded, sycophantic, and survival-oriented. They care little for justice or the quality of their ruler, focusing solely on maintaining public order and their own positions.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: The administrative class of the kingdom, responsible for executing the King's decrees and managing the transition of power.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represent bureaucratic opportunism and the willingness to accept absurd systems to preserve personal safety.
Thematic & Satirical Analysis
The Satire on Justice and Bureaucracy

The poem exposes the hollow nature of state justice when it is reduced to a mechanical process of finding a scapegoat. The King's insistence on hanging "someone" to appease the crowd shows how political systems prioritize public relations and the illusion of order over truth and morality.

The Principle of Laissez-Faire

The ending of the poem highlights the concept of laissez-faire (a policy of non-interference). The citizens do not care if their king is a melon, as long as the government leaves them in peace and liberty. This suggests that a completely passive, non-functional government is sometimes preferred by the public over an active, intrusive, and foolish autocracy.

Literary Devices & Vocabulary Matrix
Literary Device / Phrase Exact Textual Example Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical Purpose
Irony "There was a just and placid King." The King is neither truly just nor placid; his actions are erratic, foolish, and lead to his own execution. To establish the satirical tone of the narrative from the very first couplet.
Alliteration "The workmen went and built the thing." Repetition of the 'w' sound emphasizes the mechanical, unquestioning obedience of the subjects. To create a rhythmic, nursery-rhyme-like flow that contrasts with the dark themes of execution.
Idiom "The King saw red. In fact he nearly lost his head" To become extremely angry. This foreshadows the literal loss of his head (execution) later in the poem. To introduce double entendre and dark humor into the narrative progression.
Key Excerpts & Interpretations

“Let us postpone consideration / Of finer points like guilt. The nation / Wants a hanging. Hanged must be / Someone, and that immediately.”

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This passage is the climax of the poem's political satire. It demonstrates how the state abandons the pursuit of actual guilt or innocence in favor of satisfying public bloodlust and maintaining the appearance of authority. The survival of the political structure is prioritized over human life and justice.

“What he should be as long as he / Leaves us in Peace and Liberty? / The principles of laissez faire / Seem to be well-established there.”

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The concluding lines of the poem reflect on the relationship between the ruler and the ruled. It suggests that the citizens are content with an absurd, silent ruler (a melon) because it guarantees them freedom from state interference. It is a humorous commentary on how low the bar for good governance can be set.
