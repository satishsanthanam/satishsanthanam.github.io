English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 4: Telephone Conversation

Poem Overview & Core Narrative

The poem Telephone Conversation by Wole Soyinka is a powerful, satirical critique of racial prejudice and systemic discrimination in mid-20th-century society. It depicts a telephone call between an African prospective tenant and a white British landlady, exposing the absurdity, superficiality, and dehumanizing nature of racism through sharp, witty dialogue.

Chronological Plot Beats
  1. The Initial Agreement: The speaker finds an advertisement for an apartment with a reasonable price and an indifferent location. The landlady lives off-premises, which is a major advantage.
  2. The Self-Confession: Driven by past experiences of discrimination, the speaker decides to preemptively disclose his race, stating, "I am African" .
  3. The Silenced Transmission: A sudden, heavy silence ensues, representing the landlady's internal conflict between her polite upbringing and her deep-seated racial prejudice.
  4. The Absurd Interrogation: The landlady breaks the silence not by asking about his character or financial standing, but by demanding clarification on his skin tone: "HOW DARK?" and "ARE YOU LIGHT OR VERY DARK?"
  5. The Speaker's Disbelief: Dumbfounded by the clinical, detached nature of her questions, the speaker experiences a sensory overload, noticing the red telephone booth, the red pillar-box, and the smell of rancid breath in the public space.
  6. The Satirical Subversion: The speaker decides to play along with her absurd categorization, adjusting his "wave-length" to describe his skin tone using highly specific, scientific, and artistic terms like "West African sepia" and "brunette" .
  7. The Final Blow: The speaker describes his body in detail, pointing out that his palms and soles are "peroxide blonde" while his bottom is "raven black" due to friction. Sensing her about to slam the receiver down, he delivers a final, mocking invitation: "wouldn't you rather see for yourself?"
Key Excerpts & Literary Interpretations

"‘Madam,’ I warned, / ‘I hate a wasted journey—I am African.’ / Silence. Silenced transmission of / Pressurised good-breeding."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This excerpt highlights the speaker's pragmatism and weariness of facing rejection after traveling. The "silence" that follows is highly symbolic; it represents the polite facade of British high society ("good-breeding") masking deep-seated racial prejudice. The word "pressurised" suggests that her politeness is artificial and forced, barely containing her underlying bias.

"‘ARE YOU DARK? OR VERY LIGHT?’ Revelation came. / ‘You mean—like plain or milk chocolate?’"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The landlady's insistence on categorizing the speaker's skin color is met with a brilliant satirical response. By comparing human skin tones to varieties of chocolate ("plain or milk"), the speaker exposes the ridiculous, consumerist, and reductive nature of her racial classification, reducing her complex prejudice to a simple choice of confectionery.

"‘Madam,’ I pleaded, ‘wouldn’t you rather / See for yourself ?’"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The closing lines of the poem shift the power dynamic entirely. The speaker, initially the vulnerable party seeking housing, becomes the dominant, mocking figure. By inviting her to look at him directly, he challenges her cowardice and forces her to confront the physical reality of the human being she has spent the conversation dehumanizing over the phone.

Author Profile & Contextual Background

Literary Profile: Wole Soyinka

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly satirical, intellectually sharp, defiant, and deeply committed to human rights and social justice. He uses humor, irony, and linguistic subversion to dismantle oppressive structures.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in Nigeria in 1934, Soyinka was educated at Government College in Ibadan and Leeds University in England. Living in London during the mid-20th century, he experienced firsthand the subtle, polite, yet deeply entrenched racism of British society, which inspired this poem.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Anti-colonial resistance, the hypocrisy of Western civilization, the power of language as a weapon of the oppressed, and the celebration of African identity. He became the first African to receive the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1986.
Thematic Analysis & Literary Devices
Core Themes
  • Systemic and Polite Racism: The poem exposes "polite" or institutionalized racism, where prejudice is masked by social etiquette, sophisticated accents, and clinical detachment rather than overt hostility.
  • The Absurdity of Racial Categorization: Through the landlady's obsession with shades of darkness, Soyinka mocks the arbitrary and unscientific nature of racial classification.
  • Language and Wit as Tools of Resistance: The speaker uses his superior vocabulary, scientific knowledge, and sharp wit to disarm the landlady, turning her own prejudice into a source of comedy and self-defeat.
  • Isolation and Dehumanization: The setting of the telephone booth and the medium of the phone call emphasize the distance, lack of connection, and cold impersonality of racial interactions.
Literary Devices & Expressions Matrix
Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Sensory Imagery "Stench / Of rancid breath of public hide-and-speak." The literal smell of the public phone booth combined with the metaphorical rot of societal hypocrisy. Evokes physical disgust to mirror the moral disgust of racial prejudice.
Irony & Satire "Silence for spectroscopic / Flight of fancy..." The landlady trying to scientifically analyze and visualize "West African sepia" in her mind. Highlights the absurdity of trying to apply pseudo-scientific rigor to racial bias.
Metaphor "Voice, when it came, / Lipstick coated, long gold-rolled / Cigarette-holder pipped." The landlady's voice is described through her physical, upper-class accessories. Conveys her superficiality, wealth, and social status without needing to describe her face.
Color Symbolism "Red booth. Red pillar-box. Red double-tiered / Omnibus squelching tar." The overwhelming presence of the color red, typical of London's urban landscape. Grounds the poem in a realistic British setting while symbolizing danger, anger, and systemic confinement.
Pun / Wordplay "hide-and-speak" A play on the children's game "hide-and-seek". Critiques how people hide their true prejudices behind polite telephone conversations.
Comprehension & Discussion Guide
Understanding the Poem: Key Questions Answered
  • 1. State the central issue in the poem: The central issue is racial discrimination and the hypocrisy of white landlords who practice housing segregation under the guise of polite social interactions.
  • 2. What are the reasons for the intervals of silence? The silences represent moments of cultural and moral confrontation. For the landlady, silence is a struggle to maintain her "good-breeding" while processing her prejudice. For the speaker, it is a moment of shock, disbelief, and subsequent preparation of a witty, satirical response.
  • 3. How is color highlighted in the poem and why? Color is highlighted through contrasting terms: red (booth, pillar-box, omnibus), black, white, sepia, brunette, blonde, and chocolate. This is done to show how the landlady reduces a human being's entire identity to a mere shade on a color spectrum, and how the speaker subverts this by using overly complex, humorous color descriptions.
  • 4. Why are certain words in capital letters? Words like "HOW DARK?" , "ARE YOU LIGHT OR VERY DARK?" , and "WHAT'S THAT?" are capitalized to emphasize the landlady's loud, demanding, and aggressive tone, which pierces through her polite facade. It also highlights her ignorance and insensitivity.
  • 5. What does "hide-and-speak" mean? It refers to the act of communicating while hiding one's physical presence and true intentions. The landlady hides her racism behind the telephone line, while the speaker is forced to play a game of revealing his identity in stages.
Try This Out: Creative Exercises
  • Character Descriptions:
    • The Place: A cramped, smelly, public red telephone booth in mid-20th-century London, surrounded by busy streets and wet asphalt.
    • The Lady: An upper-class, wealthy British woman holding a long gold-rolled cigarette holder, wearing heavy lipstick, speaking with a cold, clinical, and patronizing accent.
    • The Speaker: An educated, highly articulate, and observant African man who possesses a sharp sense of humor and refuses to be humiliated by prejudice.
