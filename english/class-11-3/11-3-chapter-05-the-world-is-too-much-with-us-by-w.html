English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 5: The World is too Much With Us

Poem Text & Structural Overview

The poem The World is too Much With Us is a classic Petrarchan sonnet written by William Wordsworth . It serves as a powerful critique of the First Industrial Revolution, lamenting humanity's growing disconnect from the natural world in favor of materialism and consumerism.

"The World is too much with us; late and soon,
Getting and spending, we lay waste our powers:
Little we see in Nature that is ours;
We have given our hearts away, a sordid boon!"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: These opening lines establish the central conflict of the sonnet: the tragic trade-off of human spiritual capacity for material gain. The phrase getting and spending encapsulates the endless cycle of consumerism that desensitizes humanity to the sublime beauty of nature. By declaring we have given our hearts away , the poet highlights a self-inflicted emotional and spiritual bankruptcy, calling this transaction a sordid boon —a contradictory gift that degrades the soul.

"The Sea that bares her bosom to the moon;
The Winds that will be howling at all hours,
And are up-gathered now like sleeping flowers,
For this, for everything, we are out of tune;"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: Wordsworth uses vivid imagery and personification to depict nature as an active, living entity. The sea exposing her bosom to the moon and the howling winds represent the dynamic, emotional forces of the natural world. However, humanity remains completely unaffected ( out of tune ), unable to harmonize with these cosmic rhythms due to our obsession with artificial, industrial lives.

"It moves us not.—Great God! I’d rather be
A Pagan suckled in a creed outworn;
So might I, standing on this pleasant lea,
Have glimpses that would make me less forlorn;
Have sight of Proteus rising from the sea;
Or hear old Triton blow his wreathèd horn."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: In the sestet, the poet's frustration culminates in a passionate exclamation. He declares a preference for ancient Paganism over modern, sterile commercialism. Even though Pagan beliefs are considered obsolete ( a creed outworn ), they fostered a deeply spiritual, animistic connection to nature. By invoking mythological figures like Proteus and Triton , Wordsworth expresses a yearning to perceive the divine within the natural landscape, which would alleviate his profound sense of isolation and melancholy ( forlorn ).

About the Poet

Literary Profile: William Wordsworth (1770-1850)

  • Traits & Temperament: Deeply contemplative, nature-worshipping, and critical of urban industrialization. He believed that poetry should arise from the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings recollected in tranquility.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Spent most of his life in the scenic Lake District of northern England. His close relationship with the natural landscape heavily influenced his poetic philosophy. In 1798, he co-authored Lyrical Ballads with Samuel Taylor Coleridge, marking the formal beginning of the English Romantic Movement .
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Pantheism (seeing God in all aspects of nature), rustic simplicity, childhood innocence, and the spiritual degradation caused by urban development and consumerism.
Thematic Analysis & Literary Devices
Core Themes
  • Industrialization vs. Nature: The poem serves as an early warning against the ecological and psychological costs of industrial progress. Humanity's obsession with material wealth ( getting and spending ) alienates us from our natural environment.
  • Spiritual Bankruptcy: By trading our emotional connection to nature for material goods, we have surrendered our capacity for awe and wonder, resulting in a state of being out of tune with the universe.
  • The Nostalgia for Mythological Animism: Wordsworth's preference for Paganism highlights a desire to return to a time when nature was viewed as sacred and populated by divine entities, rather than merely a resource to be exploited.
Literary Devices Matrix

The following table outlines the key figures of speech and literary devices employed by Wordsworth to convey his message:

Literary Device / Phrase Exact Textual Example Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Oxymoron "sordid boon" A "boon" is a blessing, while "sordid" means dirty or ignoble. Highlights the self-contradictory nature of material progress, which enriches us physically but impoverishes us spiritually.
Personification "The Sea that bares her bosom to the moon" The sea is depicted as a woman exposing herself intimately to the moon. Emphasizes the living, breathing, and emotional qualities of nature, making humanity's indifference seem even more tragic.
Simile "up-gathered now like sleeping flowers" The howling winds are compared to quiet, sleeping flowers. Illustrates the temporary calm of nature, showing that even when nature is peaceful, humanity remains unresponsive.
Allusion "Proteus rising... Triton blow his wreathèd horn" References to Greek sea deities. Evokes an ancient, animistic worldview where nature is alive with divinity, contrasting sharply with modern secular materialism.
Metaphor "we are out of tune" Humanity is compared to an instrument that cannot play in harmony with an orchestra. Conveys the profound spiritual discord between human civilization and the natural world.
Glossary & Vocabulary
  • Sordid: Ignoble, dirty, or morally degraded. In the poem, it describes the "boon" of material wealth.
  • Boon: A timely benefit or blessing.
  • Lea: A grassy field, meadow, or open pasture.
  • Forlorn: Pitifully sad, abandoned, or lonely.
  • Pagan: A follower of a polytheistic or nature-based religion, particularly in ancient \times, distinct from the major world monotheisms.
  • Proteus: An ancient Greek sea god capable of changing his shape at will.
  • Triton: A Greek sea messenger god, son of Poseidon, typically depicted as a merman blowing a conch shell to calm or raise waves.
Understanding the Poem: Questions & Answers
  1. Why does the poet prefer to be a primitive Pagan rather than a member of civilised society?

    The poet prefers to be a primitive Pagan because, despite their "creed outworn" (obsolete beliefs), Pagans maintained a deeply spiritual, reverent, and harmonious relationship with the natural world. In contrast, modern "civilised" society is obsessed with materialism, industrialization, and consumerism ("getting and spending"), which completely alienates individuals from nature. By being a Pagan, the poet would be able to perceive divinity in nature—such as seeing Proteus or hearing Triton—thereby escaping the spiritual isolation and melancholy ("forlorn" state) of modern life.

  2. What, according to the poet, are human beings out of tune with?

    According to the poet, human beings are out of tune with the entire natural universe and its spiritual rhythms. Specifically, they are unresponsive to the majestic movements of nature, such as the sea reflecting the moonlight ("The Sea that bares her bosom to the moon") and the powerful winds ("The Winds that will be howling at all hours"). Because humanity is consumed by artificial economic pursuits, these natural phenomena "move us not."

Try This Out & Comparative Analysis
Sonnet Structure Comparison: Petrarchan vs. Shakespearean

Wordsworth's "The World is too Much With Us" is structured as a classical Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet , which differs significantly from a Shakespearean (English) sonnet :

  • Petrarchan Sonnet Structure (Wordsworth):
    • Divided into an Octave (first 8 lines) and a Sestet (last 6 lines).
    • The octave presents a problem or situation (humanity's alienation from nature), while the sestet presents a resolution or reaction (the poet's desire to be a Pagan).
    • Rhyme scheme: ABBAABBA (octave) and CDCDCD (sestet).
    • Features a Volta (turn of thought) around line 9 ("It moves us not.—Great God!").
  • Shakespearean Sonnet Structure (Shakespeare):
    • Divided into three Quatrains (4 lines each) and a concluding Couplet (2 lines).
    • The quatrains progressively develop an idea or metaphor, and the final couplet provides a sharp, witty, or summarizing resolution.
    • Rhyme scheme: ABAB CDCD EFEF GG .
    • The volta typically occurs later, often at the beginning of the final couplet (line 13).
