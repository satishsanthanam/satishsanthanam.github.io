English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 6: Mother Tongue

Poetic Text & Core Narrative

The poem Mother Tongue by Padma Sachdev is a deeply allegorical piece exploring the relationship between a writer, her medium, and her native language, Dogri . The narrative unfolds as a dialogue between the poet and a reed/stem, symbolizing the natural resources and historical tools used to preserve written culture.

"No, I don’t work for a Shah / I said, but for a Shahni, very kind, / Very well off / And I am not the only one / Working for her / She has many servants / Ever ready to do her bidding / That Shahni is my mother tongue / Dogri"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This central stanza redefines the act of writing. Instead of serving a commercial master ( Shah ) for financial accounting, the poet serves a benevolent, rich matriarch ( Shahni ), who represents her mother tongue, Dogri. This shift elevates writing from a transactional chore to a sacred, communal duty of cultural preservation.

Narrative Progression & Plot Beats
  1. The poet approaches a stem swinging on a reed, requesting a quill (traditional writing instrument).
  2. The reed responds with irritation, questioning why the poet needs a new pen so quickly, assuming she is an accountant working for a merchant ( Shah ).
  3. The poet clarifies her purpose: she does not write for commercial gain, but serves a kind, wealthy mistress ( Shahni )—her mother tongue, Dogri .
  4. Upon learning that the quill is for the service of the mother tongue, the reed's attitude transforms from irritation to absolute devotion.
  5. The reed cuts off its own hand (splitting itself to make a quill) and offers it gladly, declaring itself a fellow servant of the language.
Literary Profile: Padma Sachdev

Literary Profile: Padma Sachdev (born 1940)

  • Traits & Temperament: Deeply passionate about linguistic heritage, nostalgic, and fiercely protective of her native culture. She writes extensively in both her mother tongue, Dogri , and Hindi .
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A pioneer of modern Dogri poetry, she received the prestigious Sahitya Akademi Award at the young age of thirty for her first collection of Dogri poems.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Her poetry frequently laments the marginalization of regional languages, the loss of native scripts, and the erosion of cultural identity under modern socio-political pressures.
Thematic Analysis & Symbolism
The Quill as a Symbol of Preservation

The quill represents the physical medium of writing, artistic expression, and historical documentation. The poet's urgent quest for a quill highlights the critical need to write down and preserve a language that is losing its written tradition. Without the quill, the oral language risks fading into obscurity.

The "Shahni" vs. "Shah" Dichotomy

The poet contrasts the Shah (a male merchant or ruler representing commercialism, bureaucracy, and alien administrative languages) with the Shahni (a female queen or mistress representing the nurturing, rich, and maternal nature of the mother tongue). Serving the Shahni brings honor and joy, whereas serving the Shah is depicted as repetitive, dry bookkeeping.

The Sacrifice of Nature

The reed's act of cutting off its own hand to provide a quill symbolizes the symbiotic relationship between nature, culture, and the artist. Nature willingly sacrifices itself to provide the tools necessary for human expression, recognizing the sacred value of preserving linguistic heritage.

Literary Devices & Stylistic Features

The poem employs simple yet highly evocative literary devices to convey its message of cultural devotion.

Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Personification "The reed cut off its hand / Gave it to me and said / Take it / I too am her servant." The reed is given human anatomy, speech, and a sense of loyalty. To show that all elements of the native land are united in serving and preserving the mother tongue.
Extended Metaphor "That Shahni is my mother tongue / Dogri" Comparing the Dogri language to a wealthy, benevolent queen. Elevates the status of a regional language, emphasizing its richness, dignity, and maternal warmth.
Colloquialism "Shah" and "Shahni" Traditional terms for a wealthy merchant/ruler and his wife. Grounds the poem in the regional, cultural, and historical landscape of Northern India.
Socio-Linguistic Context of Dogri
The Loss of the Sharade Script

The poem serves as a lament for the deprivation of Dogri's native script, Sharade . Sharade evolved from the ancient Brahmi script around the time Dogri developed. Once widely used by people of all religions in the Kashmir valley, it was gradually replaced by the Persian script due to political and administrative shifts. Today, Dogri is written using either the Devanagari or Persian scripts, leaving it without its original unique visual identity.

Geographical & Constitutional Status
  • Geographical Distribution: Dogri is spoken predominantly in parts of Jammu and Kashmir , Himachal Pradesh , and Punjab .
  • Historical Mention: Its earliest recorded mention is found in the medieval poet Amir Khusro's list of Indian languages.
  • Constitutional Recognition: Dogri is officially recognized as one of the languages listed in Schedule VIII of the Constitution of India.
Textual Exercises & Discussion Points
Understanding the Poem: Key Questions
  1. What does the quill symbolise? The quill symbolises the tool of artistic creation, the preservation of written culture, and the physical act of keeping a language alive through literature.
  2. What is the reason for the poet's sense of urgency? The urgency stems from the threat of linguistic extinction. As the native script dies out and oral traditions fade, the poet feels a pressing duty to write and document her mother tongue before it is lost forever.
  3. How does the poet show her emotional attachment? By personifying Dogri as a beloved, kind, and wealthy "Shahni" whom she gladly serves without demanding material wealth, contrasting this with dry commercial labor.
  4. How is personification used in the poem? The reed is personified as an interlocutor who speaks, feels irritation, understands cultural duty, and ultimately sacrifices its "hand" to serve the language.
