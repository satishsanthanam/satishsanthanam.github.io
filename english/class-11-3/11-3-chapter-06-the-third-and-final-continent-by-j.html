English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 6: The Third and Final Continent

Chapter Overview & Historical Context

This chapter, written by Pulitzer Prize-winning author Jhumpa Lahiri , serves as the poignant final story in her acclaimed collection, Interpreter of Maladies . It chronicles the journey of a young Bengali man as he navigates life across three distinct continents: Asia (India), Europe (England), and North America (the United States). Set against the backdrop of the historic 1969 Apollo 11 moon landing, the narrative explores themes of cultural displacement, the quiet resilience of immigrants, the evolution of arranged marriages, and the unexpected, profound connections forged between strangers across generational and cultural divides.

Literary Profile: Jhumpa Lahiri

  • Traits & Temperament: Elegant, introspective, deeply observant of domestic spaces, cultural displacement, and the quiet emotional undercurrents of immigrant lives.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in London to Bengali parents and raised in the United States, Lahiri frequently explores the nuances of the Indian diaspora, the tension between heritage and assimilation, and generational divides.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Food as cultural currency, domestic routines as anchors, the contrast between historical milestones and personal struggles, and the quiet resilience of the displaced.
Detailed Narrative Progression
Phase 1: The European Interlude (London, 1964–1969)

The narrator leaves India in 1964 with a commerce certificate and very little money, sailing on an Italian cargo vessel to England. In London, he lives a frugal, communal life with other penniless Bengali bachelors in Finsbury Park, sharing rooms, cooking egg curry, and attending lectures at the LSE . This period represents a collective struggle for education and survival, marked by shared domestic routines and a slow adaptation to Western life.

Phase 2: Arrival in America & The YMCA Stay (Boston, 1969)

In 1969, at the age of thirty-six, the narrator's marriage is arranged in Calcutta. Around the same time, he secures a full-time library position at MIT . He flies to Boston, staying initially at the YMCA in Central Square, Cambridge. He experiences the overwhelming sensory overload of American urban life—the blaring sirens, heavy traffic, and unfamiliar currency—while surviving on a simple diet of cornflakes, milk, and bananas.

Phase 3: Lodging with the Centenarian Mrs. Croft

Seeking a quieter environment before his wife's arrival, the narrator rents a room for eight dollars a week from Mrs. Croft , an eccentric, fiercely independent 103-year-old widow. He is subjected to her strict household rules, including locking the door securely and sitting with her on the piano bench. Their daily interactions revolve around a rigid, repetitive ritual where she declares the American flag on the moon to be "splendid," demanding that he repeat the word in agreement.

"There is an American flag on the moon!" ... "Say 'splendid'!" ... "Splendid," I murmured.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This recurring interaction highlights the generational and cultural bridge between the narrator and Mrs. Croft. While the narrator initially finds the demand childish and reminiscent of rote learning in India, it becomes a comforting ritual. The moon landing—a monumental, boundary-breaking human achievement—contrasts sharply with Mrs. Croft's static, confined existence and the narrator's own quiet, ground-level journey of crossing continents. It underscores how monumental historical events are processed through intimate, highly localized human connections.

Phase 4: Helen's Visit & Reflections on Motherhood

The narrator meets Mrs. Croft's daughter, Helen , who visits weekly to bring groceries. From Helen, he learns that Mrs. Croft is actually 103 years old and was once a piano teacher who supported her family after her husband's death. This revelation fills the narrator with awe and concern for her fragility. It also triggers painful memories of his own mother's tragic mental decline and death following his father's passing in Calcutta, highlighting the universal vulnerabilities of widowhood and aging.

Phase 5: Mala's Arrival & The Transition to Domesticity

At the end of August, the narrator's wife, Mala , arrives in Boston. They move into a furnished apartment near MIT. Initially, they feel like complete strangers, bound only by duty and the traditional markers of their arranged marriage. The narrator struggles to adjust to her presence, her traditional attire, and the domestic changes she introduces, such as cooking elaborate Bengali meals and cleaning the apartment.

Phase 6: The Turning Point & Final Reflections

The emotional distance between the couple begins to dissolve during a Friday evening walk when the narrator takes Mala to meet Mrs. Croft. Upon meeting Mala, Mrs. Croft scrutinizes her and declares her to be a "perfect lady." This unexpected validation causes both the narrator and Mala to share a genuine, spontaneous laugh, marking the true beginning of their emotional union. Decades later, now naturalized American citizens with a son who graduated from Harvard, the narrator reflects on his extraordinary journey across three continents, finding solace in the ordinary yet miraculous nature of human survival and adaptation.

"She is a perfect lady!" ... Now it was I who laughed. I did so quietly and Mrs Croft did not hear me. But Mala had heard, and, for the first time, we looked at each other and smiled.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This moment serves as the emotional turning point of the narrative. Mrs. Croft's unexpected validation of Mala, despite her traditional Indian attire (sari, vermilion, and dyed feet), bridges the gap between the old American widow and the young Indian bride. It acts as a catalyst that dissolves the emotional distance between the narrator and Mala, transforming them from strangers bound by duty into partners united by shared amusement and mutual understanding.

Character Profiles & Analysis

Literary Profile: The Narrator

  • Traits & Temperament: Pragmatic, disciplined, emotionally reserved, deeply respectful of tradition and elders, resilient in the face of isolation.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A Bengali immigrant who leaves India in 1964 with minimal funds, studies and works in London, and eventually secures a professional library position at MIT in Boston.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: The transition from communal bachelor living to solitary boarding, and finally to domestic partnership; his journey represents the classic immigrant trajectory of survival and quiet triumph.

Literary Profile: Mrs. Croft

  • Traits & Temperament: Fierce, highly disciplined, eccentric, fiercely independent, unyielding in her adherence to Victorian-era social decorum.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in 1866, a centenarian widow living alone in Cambridge, Massachusetts; she supported her family for forty years by giving piano lessons after her husband's death.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: An anchor to a bygone era; her obsession with the American flag on the moon represents her awe of a rapidly changing world that she observes from her static piano bench.

Literary Profile: Mala

  • Traits & Temperament: Traditional, sensitive, initially homesick and vulnerable, yet quietly adaptable and deeply committed to her marital duties.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: The daughter of a schoolteacher in Calcutta, married to the narrator through an arranged match; she travels to America to join a husband she barely knows.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: The preservation of cultural identity (sari, vermilion, cooking traditional meals) while navigating a foreign landscape; her evolution from a weeping bride to a confident American citizen.
Thematic & Symbolic Interpretations
  • The Moon Landing vs. Personal Journeys: The narrative constantly parallels the macro-achievement of the Apollo 11 astronauts with the micro-achievements of the narrator's immigrant journey. While the astronauts travel to the moon, the narrator travels across three continents, suggesting that adapting to a new culture is its own form of heroic exploration.
  • The Three Continents as Stages of Life: India represents the narrator's roots, family duties, and early losses; London represents a transitional phase of communal survival and youth; America represents the final destination of maturity, domesticity, and permanent settlement.
  • The Piano Bench & The Cane: These objects symbolize Mrs. Croft's past independence and her current physical vulnerability. The piano bench, once a tool for her livelihood, becomes her static throne, while her cane represents her refusal to completely surrender to her physical limitations.
  • Food as Cultural Currency: The transition from communal egg curry in London, to solitary cornflakes at the YMCA, and finally to the elaborate chicken curry prepared by Mala, maps the narrator's emotional state and his journey toward domestic stability and cultural preservation.

Study Guide & Comprehension Exercises
  1. Financial Constraints in London: The narrator's financial struggles are highlighted by his communal living arrangements (three or four to a room), sharing a single icy toilet, eating simple egg curry with hands on newspaper-covered tables, and using the bathtub to soak dirty dishes.
  2. Adaptation to New Environments: In London, the narrator adapts by finding a community of fellow Bengali bachelors. In Cambridge, USA, he adapts by establishing a rigid routine, converting ounces to grams at the supermarket, and learning to navigate American terminology (e.g., "elevator" instead of "lift").
  3. Character of Mrs. Croft: Mrs. Croft is a fiercely independent, traditional, and eccentric centenarian. Her insistence on Victorian decorum and her awe of the moon landing reveal a character anchored in the past yet fascinated by the progress of the modern world.
  4. Mother-Daughter Relationship: Helen and Mrs. Croft share a relationship marked by practical care and generational tension. Helen respects her mother's independence but must step in to manage her physical needs, navigating her mother's strict, outdated social rules with patient exasperation.
  5. Cultural Contrast & Mala's Adaptation: The story contrasts the communal, traditional Indian way of life with the individualistic, fast-paced American society. Mala's adaptation is marked by initial homesickness, followed by a quiet, successful effort to recreate a sense of home through traditional cooking, domestic care, and shared exploration of the city with her husband.
