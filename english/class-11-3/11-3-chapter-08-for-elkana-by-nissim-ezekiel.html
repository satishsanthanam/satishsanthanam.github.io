English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 8: For Elkana

Poem Overview & Narrative Arc

The poem For Elkana by Nissim Ezekiel is a beautifully crafted, realistic portrayal of a quiet evening in an Indian household. It captures the delicate, humorous, and deeply affectionate dynamics between a husband, a wife, and their seven-year-old son. Rather than focusing on grand romantic gestures, Ezekiel highlights the beauty of the mundane, showing how small domestic frictions dissolve into shared laughter and familial warmth.

The Setting and Initial Atmosphere

The poem opens on a warm April evening . The family drags their chairs down the stone steps to the lawn to enjoy the breeze. The atmosphere is initially peaceful but marked by a comfortable, companionable silence that is soon broken by the mundane realities of domestic life. The physical act of "sprawling" in silence suggests a deep level of comfort and familiarity between the couple.

The Domestic Friction

The silence is broken when the wife, "as is her way," surveys the scene and points out a broken window-pane . She suggests repairs, subtly implying that while other husbands in the neighborhood would know exactly how to fix it, her own husband (the speaker) does not. The speaker, unwilling to dispute this obvious truth, chooses to escape into his own thoughts, shutting her voice out to maintain his inner peace.

The Child's Intervention and Logic

The gentle tension is interrupted by their seven-year-old son, who demands his dinner "now" with "masterly determination." When the mother tells him to wait five minutes, the child presents a remarkably logical argument: "in five minutes / I won’t be hungry any more." This clever, immediate logic appeals to the father, who recognizes himself in the boy, and ultimately breaks the tension, leading to shared family laughter.

About the Poet

Literary Profile: Nissim Ezekiel (1924–2004)

  • Traits & Temperament: Observant, ironic, self-deprecating, and deeply interested in the nuances of everyday Indian life. He is celebrated for his conversational style, clarity of thought, and realistic depictions of domestic and social realities.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in Mumbai into a Bene Israel Jewish family. Educated at Wilson College, Bombay, and Birbeck College, London. He served as a Professor of American Literature at Bombay University and was a foundational figure in post-independence Indian English poetry.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Everyday urban existence, domestic relationships, cultural alienation, skepticism, and the search for spiritual and emotional integration in modern India.
Literary Devices & Style Matrix

Ezekiel employs a conversational, low-key style that relies heavily on understatement, irony, and precise observation of human behavior. The table below analyzes the key literary devices used in the poem:

Key Excerpts & Interpretations

"Wife and husband in unusual rapport / state one unspoken thought: / Children Must be Disciplined. / She looks at me. I look away."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This passage highlights the momentary solidarity between parents when faced with a child's defiance. The "unusual rapport" is ironic, suggesting that they are normally divided on minor issues but unite under the banner of parental authority. However, the speaker's action of "looking away" reveals his reluctance to enforce this discipline, showing his soft-heartedness and silent support for his son's independent spirit.

"My wife’s delightful laughter / holds the three of us together. / We rise and go into the house."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The poem concludes not with a lecture or a punishment, but with the mother's laughter. This laughter acts as a binding agent, dissolving the minor tensions of the evening (the broken window, the husband's passive escape, the child's stubbornness). It celebrates the power of humor and love in maintaining domestic harmony, bringing the family back inside the house in perfect unity.

Understanding the Poem: Discussion & Analysis
  1. The Pattern of Communication: Ezekiel captures family communication with extreme subtlety. It is not dramatic; instead, it consists of comfortable silences, gentle nagging about household chores (the broken window-pane), passive-aggressive mental retreats, and sudden moments of shared parental responsibility. The communication is realistic, showing how families navigate minor frictions through silence, compromise, and humor.
  2. Understatement and Asides: The poem relies on these devices to build its quiet, realistic tone. The husband's mental retreat ("shuts her out") and his self-deprecating acknowledgment of his own domestic shortcomings are presented as quiet asides. This prevents the poem from becoming overly sentimental or dramatic, keeping it grounded in everyday reality.
  3. Juxtaposition of the Idyllic and the Pedestrian: The poem begins with an idyllic setting—a warm April evening, breezes sauntering across a lawn. This peaceful, poetic imagery is immediately juxtaposed with the pedestrian reality of a broken window-pane and a child demanding dinner. This contrast highlights how beauty and poetry can be found within the ordinary, sometimes messy details of daily life.
  4. The Logic of the Child's Demand: The child's argument is highly logical in its simplicity. If hunger is a physical sensation felt "now," waiting five minutes means the immediate state of hunger will change or become frustrating. By arguing that "in five minutes / I won't be hungry any more," the child cleverly subverts the adult concept of time, winning over his father's admiration and his mother's laughter.
The Limerick (Supplementary Section)

The chapter concludes with an introduction to The Limerick , a distinct poetic form designed for humor and lighthearted entertainment.

Definition and Structure

A limerick is a small, five-line poem that expresses a single, humorous thought. It is characterized by:

  • A strict rhyme scheme of a a b b a .
  • Lines 1, 2, and 5 are longer and rhyme with each other.
  • Lines 3 and 4 are shorter and rhyme with each other.
  • A final line that contains a punchline or a humorous twist.
  • An association with lighthearted, slapstick comedy in poetic form.
Analysis of Provided Limericks

The textbook provides four examples of limericks to demonstrate the form's versatility and humor:

  1. The Novice Driver: A humorous take on a father driving too slowly, whose son urges him to drive faster, resulting in them being late because they likely got into an accident or were stopped ("He did, and they are").
  2. Earth's Plan: A slightly philosophical yet lighthearted limerick about how human sin has spoiled the Earth's hopeful beginnings, concluding that "at present the other side's winning!"
  3. The Man from Peru: A classic absurd limerick about a man who dreamed he was eating his shoe, only to wake up and find it was perfectly true.
  4. Ms Brass and Her Class: A relatable school-themed limerick about a teacher with an unruly, sleeping class that completely ignores mathematical theorems like Pythagoras.
