English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 8: The Luncheon

Chapter Overview & Author Profile

Literary Profile: William Somerset Maugham (1874–1965)

  • Traits & Temperament: Distinguished British author, playwright, and novelist; celebrated for his sharp observation of human foibles, dry humor, and mastery of situational irony.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in Paris, spent his early childhood in a French-speaking society. After his father's death, he moved to England at age 10. He studied medicine at Heidelberg and St. Thomas's Hospital, London, qualifying as a doctor, but abandoned medicine to pursue his passion for writing.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Explores the gap between social pretense and inner reality, the comedy of human self-delusion, and the psychological impact of financial anxiety. Famous works include Of Human Bondage , The Moon and Sixpence , and Cakes and Ale .

The short story The Luncheon is a brilliant, first-person narrative that satirizes social hypocrisy, superficial politeness, and the agonizing comedy of financial embarrassment. Set against the backdrop of a high-class Parisian restaurant, the story details a young, struggling writer's encounter with an insincere, self-absorbed admirer who systematically exploits his inability to say "no" under the guise of "eating light."

Character Profiles
The Narrator (The Struggling Writer)

Literary Profile: The Narrator

  • Traits & Temperament: Polite, easily flattered, non-confrontational, and deeply self-conscious. He suffers from a youthful inability to refuse a woman's request, leading him to prioritize social appearance over financial survival.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A young writer living in a tiny apartment in the Latin Quarter of Paris, earning barely enough money to keep "body and soul together." He has only eighty gold francs to last him the entire month.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the vulnerability of youth, the burden of false pride, and the silent panic of the middle class trying to maintain appearances in elite spaces.
The Lady Guest (The Admirer)

Literary Profile: The Lady Guest

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly talkative, manipulative, self-absorbed, and deeply hypocritical. She repeatedly asserts that she "never eats more than one thing" while systematically ordering the most expensive delicacies on the menu.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A woman of forty, imposing rather than attractive, who uses her interest in art and literature to flatter and exploit the young author. She is completely oblivious (or indifferent) to her host's financial distress.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Symbolizes gluttony masked as dietary discipline, bourgeois pretentiousness, and the parasitic nature of superficial literary patrons.
Chronological Plot Progression
  1. The Chance Encounter: Twenty years after the event, the narrator spots his former guest at a play. She recognizes him, prompting him to recall their first meeting in Paris.
  2. The Flattering Letter: Two decades prior, the lady reads a book by the narrator and writes to him. Flattered by her attention, the young writer agrees to host her for a "little luncheon" at Foyot's —an expensive restaurant frequented by French senators, far beyond his modest means.
  3. The Financial Calculation: With eighty gold francs to last the month, the narrator estimates that a modest lunch should cost no more than fifteen francs. He decides to cut out coffee for two weeks to manage the expense.
  4. The Meeting and First Shock: Upon meeting her, the narrator finds her older and more talkative than expected. When the menu arrives, he is startled by the exorbitant prices, but she reassures him with her mantra: "I never eat anything for luncheon."
  5. The Escalation of Delicacies: Despite her claims of eating nothing, she systematically orders:
    • Salmon: Not on the bill of fare early in the year, but ordered anyway.
    • Caviare: Ordered casually while waiting for the salmon. The narrator, unable to afford it, orders a cheap mutton chop for himself.
    • Champagne: She claims her doctor forbids anything else. The narrator orders a half-bottle, while drinking plain water himself.
    • Giant Asparagus: Horribly expensive and out of season, which she devours voluptuously while lecturing him on overeating.
    • Ice-cream and Coffee: Ordered as a sweet finish to her "light" meal.
    • Peaches: Taken absentmindedly from a basket brought by the headwaiter, despite being out of season.
  6. The Silent Panic: Throughout the meal, the narrator is seized by panic, calculating whether he has enough money to pay the bill or if he will be forced to pretend his pocket was picked or leave his watch as collateral.
  7. The Aftermath: The narrator pays the bill, leaving a meager three-franc tip that makes him look mean. He is left with absolutely no money for the rest of the month, while she departs in a cab, calling him a "humorist."
  8. The Ultimate Revenge: Twenty years later, the narrator observes with satisfaction that the "immortal gods" have taken revenge: the lady now weighs twenty-one stone .

Key Textual Excerpts & Interpretations

“I never eat more than one thing. I think people eat far too much nowadays. A little fish, perhaps. I wonder if they have any salmon.”

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This quote is the cornerstone of the story's situational irony. The guest uses the rhetoric of moderation ("people eat far too much") to justify her self-indulgence. By framing expensive delicacies like salmon and caviare as mere "bites" or "one thing," she maintains a self-image of refined restraint while acting with complete gluttony and disregard for her host's financial limits.

“Panic seized me: it was not a question now how much money I should have left over for the rest of the month but whether I had enough to pay the bill. It would be mortifying to find myself ten francs short and be obliged to borrow from my guest.”

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This passage highlights the internal conflict of the narrator. The comedy of the situation is balanced by genuine middle-class dread. The fear of social humiliation ("mortifying") outweighs his concern for actual physical survival (having no money for food for the rest of the month), illustrating how social expectations and pride can paralyze an individual.

“But I have had my revenge at last. I do not believe that I am a vindictive man, but when the immortal gods take a hand in the matter it is pardonable to observe the result with complacency. Today she weighs twenty-one stone.”

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The resolution of the story shifts the power dynamic. The narrator, who was once helpless and impoverished by her greed, finds poetic justice in her physical transformation. The reference to "immortal gods" elevates his petty satisfaction to cosmic justice, providing a humorous, satisfying, and highly ironic conclusion to the narrative.

Study Questions & Discussion Points
Understanding the Text
  • Question 1: Although the author was not a vindictive man, he was very happy to see the twenty-one stone lady who had impoverished him twenty years ago. What makes him say this?
    Answer Hint: The narrator feels that cosmic justice has been served. The woman who shamelessly exploited his meager finances under the pretense of "eating light" has grown excessively obese, which serves as a physical manifestation of her greed and hypocrisy.
  • Question 2: Why does the author use expressions like "my heart sank" and "panic seized me"?
    Answer Hint: These expressions reflect the narrator's growing financial terror as his guest continues to order expensive, out-of-season delicacies, threatening to exceed his total monthly budget of eighty francs.
Appreciation & Style
  • Humor and Irony: The story's humor relies heavily on the contrast between the guest's verbal assertions ("I never eat more than one thing") and her physical actions (consuming salmon, caviare, champagne, asparagus, ice-cream, coffee, and peaches).
  • First-Person Narrative: The first-person perspective allows the reader to experience the narrator's internal panic, calculations, and embarrassment in real-time, heightening the comedic and dramatic tension of the luncheon.
