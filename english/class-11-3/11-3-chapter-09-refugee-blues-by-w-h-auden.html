English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 9: Refugee Blues

Poet Profile & Historical Context

Literary Profile: Wystan Hugh Auden (1907–1973)

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly intellectual, deeply compassionate, politically engaged, and celebrated for his technical virtuosity, irony, and wit.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Educated at Oxford, later serving as a Professor of Poetry there. He lived through the turbulent mid-20th century, witnessing the rise of totalitarianism, fascism, and the massive displacement of people during World War II.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Frequently explored themes of social injustice, political apathy, existential isolation, and the struggle of the individual against oppressive state machinery.
Historical Context: The Plight of German Jewish Refugees

Written in 1939 on the eve of the Second World War, the poem captures the harrowing experience of German Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi persecution. Deprived of their citizenship, passports, and basic human rights, these individuals found themselves trapped in a bureaucratic limbo, rejected by foreign nations fearing economic strain or political complications.

The Ballad and Blues Form

Auden masterfully combines the traditional European ballad form (characterized by simple, narrative stanzas and a regular rhythm) with the African-American blues tradition (a musical style expressing deep sorrow, longing, and systemic oppression). This fusion creates a haunting, rhythmic lament that universalizes the pain of exile.

Stanza-by-Stanza Thematic Analysis
Stanzas 1–4: The Paradox of Abundance and Bureaucratic Death

"Say this city has ten million souls, / Some are living in mansions, some are living in holes: / Yet there’s no place for us, my dear, yet there’s no place for us."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This opening stanza establishes a stark contrast between the vast, thriving urban population and the absolute isolation of the refugee couple. The city has room for extreme wealth ("mansions") and extreme poverty ("holes"), yet it has absolutely no space—legal or physical—for the displaced. The repetition of "no place for us" emphasizes their complete exclusion from the human community.

"The consul banged the table and said: / ‘If you’ve got no passport you’re officially dead’..."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This stanza highlights the absurdity of modern statecraft, where human existence is validated solely by paper documents. The consul's aggressive gesture ("banged the table") and cold declaration reveal a system that prioritizes legal status over biological life. The refugees' poignant response ("But we are still alive") asserts their humanity against a dehumanizing bureaucratic apparatus.

Stanzas 5–8: Institutional Apathy and Public Hostility

"Went to a committee; they offered me a chair; / Asked me politely to return next year..."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The "committee" represents institutionalized indifference. While maintaining a facade of politeness ("offered me a chair", "asked me politely"), they offer no real help, telling the desperate refugees to return in a year—a timeline that ignores the immediate, life-threatening urgency of their situation.

"Came to a public meeting; the speaker got up and said: / ‘If we let them in, they will steal our daily bread’..."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This stanza exposes the xenophobic rhetoric used by politicians to exploit public anxiety. By framing refugees as economic threats who will "steal our daily bread," the speaker dehumanizes them, turning public sympathy into hostility and fear.

Stanzas 9–12: The Freedom of Nature vs. Human Captivity

"Saw a poodle in a jacket fastened with a pin; / Saw a door opened and a cat let in: / But they weren’t German Jews, my dear..."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: Auden uses sharp situational irony by comparing the treatment of domestic pets to that of human refugees. The pampered poodle and the welcomed cat enjoy warmth, shelter, and care, while the German Jews are left exposed to the elements, highlighting a profound moral inversion where animals are granted more dignity than certain classes of humans.

"Stood on a great plain in the falling snow; / Ten thousand soldiers marched to and fro: / Looking for you and me, my dear, looking for you and me."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The final stanza shifts to a chilling, atmospheric climax. The vast, cold "great plain" and "falling snow" symbolize a silent, indifferent universe. The "ten thousand soldiers" represent the overwhelming, mechanized power of the fascist state actively hunting down two defenseless individuals, ending the poem on a note of imminent doom and inescapable terror.

Literary Devices & Stylistic Matrix

The following matrix outlines the primary poetic devices employed by Auden to convey the emotional and political weight of the refugees' plight:

Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Refrain "my dear, yet there’s no place for us" / "looking for you and me" Emphasizes the personal, intimate bond between the two speakers amidst a hostile, uncaring world. Establishes the musical, repetitive rhythm of the blues/ballad form, reinforcing the inescapable cycle of displacement.
Irony "Old passports can’t do that, my dear" A dead passport cannot renew itself like a natural yew tree, yet it holds absolute power over human life and death. Highlights the absurdity of modern bureaucracy, where paper documents are valued over living human beings.
Pathos "But where shall we go today, my dear" The simple, desperate question of survival asked after being politely turned away by a committee. Evokes deep sympathy and emotional resonance in the reader by highlighting the immediate, physical vulnerability of the refugees.
Sarcasm / Satire "If we let them in, they will steal our daily bread" The public speaker's xenophobic claim that impoverished, homeless refugees will somehow ruin the local economy. Exposes the irrational fear, selfishness, and moral bankruptcy of host societies hiding behind economic excuses.
Juxtaposition "Saw a poodle in a jacket... Saw a door opened and a cat let in" Domestic animals are treated with warmth, shelter, and care, while human refugees are left out in the cold. Underscores the dehumanization of German Jews, who are denied the basic dignity and rights afforded to common pets.
Critical Commentary & Discussion Prompts
Thematic Analysis of Key Motifs
  • The Color 'Blue': Suggests melancholy, depression, and mourning, while also referencing the musical genre of the blues, which historically emerged from the suffering and oppression of marginalized communities.
  • "Two Tickets to Happiness": Represents the simple, universal human desire for safety, peace, and a normal life, which is rendered impossible ("every coach was full") by a world closed off to refugees.
  • Animal Imagery: The fish swimming freely "only ten feet away" and the birds singing "at their ease" highlight how nature is free from the artificial, cruel political boundaries created by humans.
Textbook Questions & Analytical Solutions
  1. How does the title 'Refugee Blues' encapsulate the theme of the poem?

    The title perfectly marries the political reality of displacement ( Refugee ) with a musical form of lamentation ( Blues ). It signals that the poem is not just a political critique, but a deeply personal, emotional expression of sorrow, exile, and existential despair.

  2. What do the references to birds and animals suggest?

    They highlight the unnatural cruelty of human political systems. Animals, lacking politicians, passports, and borders, enjoy absolute freedom of movement and existence, whereas human beings are hunted, restricted, and denied shelter simply due to their lack of legal status.

  3. How does the poet juxtapose the human condition with the behavior of the political class?

    The political class is depicted as cold, bureaucratic, and hostile (the table-banging consul, the stalling committee, the xenophobic public speaker, and the marching soldiers). In contrast, the refugees represent vulnerable, raw human life seeking nothing more than survival and "happiness," exposing the moral failure of the political systems of the time.
