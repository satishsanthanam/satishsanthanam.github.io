English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 10: Felling of the Banyan Tree

Poem Overview & Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis

The poem Felling of the Banyan Tree by Dilip Chitre is an autobiographical and deeply moving exploration of the conflict between traditional reverence for nature and the relentless march of modernization and urbanization. It details the systematic destruction of trees surrounding the poet's ancestral home in Baroda , culminating in the dramatic and painful felling of a massive, ancient banyan tree, followed by the family's relocation to the concrete jungle of Bombay .

Stanza 1: The Demolition and the Sacred Trees

In this opening section, the poet establishes the setting and the conflict:

  • The poet's father orders the tenants living in the houses surrounding their hilltop home to leave, and their structures are systematically demolished.
  • Only the family's house and the surrounding trees remain, highlighting the isolation of the natural world before its destruction.
  • The poet contrasts the traditional belief of his grandmother—who held that trees are sacred and felling them is a crime—with his father's pragmatic, destructive actions, described as a massacre .
  • Various trees like the sheoga (drumstick), oudumber (wild fig), and neem are cut down easily, but the massive banyan tree stands as a formidable obstacle, a problem whose roots run deeper than the lives of the inhabitants.
Stanza 2: The Grandeur and Dismantling of the Banyan Tree

This stanza focuses on the physical scale of the banyan tree and the arduous process of its destruction:

  • The tree is described as three \times as tall as the house, with a massive trunk circumference of fifty feet and scraggy aerial roots reaching down from thirty feet or more.
  • The destruction begins with the branches, which takes fifty men sawing for seven days, creating a massive heap of wood.
  • The ecological impact is immediate as insects and birds are forced to abandon their ancient sanctuary, symbolizing the disruption of the local ecosystem.
Stanza 3: The Slaughter and Migration

The final section depicts the ultimate destruction of the trunk and the aftermath:

  • Fifty men with axes chop at the massive trunk, eventually revealing its growth rings, which indicate an age of two hundred years .
  • The children watch this slaughter with a mixture of terror and fascination , as if a raw mythology is revealing its ancient secrets.
  • Shortly after this environmental destruction, the family moves from Baroda to Bombay (Mumbai), a highly urbanized city devoid of trees, where the memory of the banyan tree continues to haunt the poet's dreams.
Author Profile & Literary Context

Literary Profile: Dilip Chitre

  • Traits & Temperament: A bilingual modernist poet, painter, and filmmaker who wrote extensively in both Marathi and English. His work is characterized by intense visual imagery, a deep engagement with existential themes, and a critical view of modern urban alienation.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in Baroda (Vadodara) in 1938, Chitre witnessed the rapid post-independence industrialization of India. His relocation to Mumbai (Bombay) deeply influenced his poetry, highlighting the stark contrast between semi-rural heritage and urban decay.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: The poem is taken from his collection Travelling in a Cage (1980). It uses the banyan tree as a symbol of ancestral heritage, ecological balance, and the spiritual roots of Indian culture, which are severed by modern commercialism.
Thematic Analysis & Literary Devices
Core Themes
  • Tradition vs. Modernity: Represented by the clash between the grandmother's traditional, eco-spiritual belief that trees are sacred and the father's modern, utilitarian, and rationalist drive to clear land for development.
  • Ecological Destruction and Anthropocentrism: The poem highlights humanity's destructive dominance over nature. The felling of the tree is described as a slaughter and a massacre , emphasizing the violence of environmental degradation.
  • Displacement and Loss of Roots: The physical uprooting of the banyan tree mirrors the family's displacement from their ancestral home in Baroda to the sterile, tree-less environment of Bombay.
  • Memory and the Subconscious: The final lines show that while the physical tree can be destroyed, its spiritual essence remains alive, seething in the poet's subconscious dreams, representing unresolved guilt and cultural loss.

Textual Excerpts & Deep Interpretations

"Trees are sacred my grandmother used to say / Felling them is a crime but he massacred them all"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This excerpt highlights the generational and ideological divide within the family. The grandmother represents traditional Indian ethos, which views nature as sacred and interconnected with human life. The father represents the modern, patriarchal, and pragmatic mindset that views nature merely as property or an obstacle to be cleared. The word 'massacred' elevates the act from simple clearing to a moral crime against living beings.

"But the huge banyan tree stood like a problem / Whose roots lay deeper than all our lives"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The banyan tree is not just a physical entity but a temporal and spiritual anchor. Its roots, both physical and metaphorical, extend deeper into history and the earth than the temporary lives of the human family living near it. Describing it as a 'problem' reflects the modern developer's view of history and nature as inconveniences that must be resolved or eliminated.

"The great tree revealed its rings of two hundred years / We watched in terror and fascination this slaughter / As a raw mythology revealed to us its age"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The 'rings of two hundred years' represent a tangible record of time, history, and endurance. The destruction of the tree is a 'slaughter' of history itself. The phrase 'raw mythology' suggests that the tree was a living myth, a sacred connection to the ancient past, whose violent dismantling exposes the raw, vulnerable core of sacred traditions to the cold reality of modern axes.

"Where there are no trees except the one / Which grows and seethes in one’s dreams, its aerial roots / Looking for the ground to strike."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The poem ends with a powerful shift to the urban landscape of Bombay, characterized by its lack of nature. The uprooted banyan tree finds a new, restless home in the poet's subconscious. It 'seethes' (boils with anger or agitation), representing the unresolved trauma of ecological destruction. Its aerial roots search desperately for ground to strike, symbolizing the human search for identity, stability, and connection in a sterile, concrete world.

Comprehensive Study Guide & Exercises
Understanding the Poem: Question & Answer Key
  1. Identify the lines that reveal the critical tone of the poet towards the felling of the tree.
    Answer: The critical tone is evident in lines such as: "Felling them is a crime but he massacred them all", "But the huge banyan tree stood like a problem", and "We watched in terror and fascination this slaughter". The use of words like 'crime', 'massacred', and 'slaughter' clearly conveys the poet's disapproval and horror.
  2. Identify the words that help you understand the nature of the poet’s father.
    Answer: The father is depicted as authoritative, pragmatic, and unsentimental. Words and phrases like "My father told the tenants to leave", "demolished", "he massacred them all", and "My father ordered it to be removed" show his decisive, commanding, and nature-subduing character.
  3. ‘Trees are sacred my grandmother used to say’— what does the poet imply by this line?
    Answer: The poet \implies a traditional Indian worldview where nature is revered, protected, and considered divine. It contrasts sharply with the modern, utilitarian perspective of the father, highlighting the loss of traditional values that protected the environment.
  4. ‘No trees except the one which grows and seethes in one’s dreams’— why is the phrase ‘grows and seethes’ used?
    Answer: 'Grows' indicates that the memory and impact of the tree continue to expand in the poet's mind. 'Seethes' suggests anger, agitation, and pain. The phrase reflects the unresolved trauma, guilt, and restless energy of a destroyed natural world seeking justice or a place to root itself in the human conscience.
  5. How does the banyan tree stand out as different from other trees? What details of the tree does the poet highlight in the poem?
    Answer: Unlike the sheoga, oudumber, and neem trees which were easily cut down, the banyan tree is massive and deeply rooted. The poet highlights its immense height (three \times the house), its huge trunk circumference (fifty feet), its long scraggy aerial roots (thirty feet or more), and its ancient age of two hundred years.
  6. What does the reference to raw mythology imply?
    Answer: It \implies that the ancient tree was a living embodiment of sacred myths, folklore, and natural history. Its destruction is like tearing open a sacred \text or dismantling a living legend, exposing its ancient, raw, and mysterious core to violent modern reality.
  7. ‘Whose roots lay deeper than all our lives’— what aspect of human behaviour does this line reflect?
    Answer: It reflects human short-sightedness and arrogance. Humans, with their relatively short lifespans, arrogantly destroy ancient natural systems (like a 200-year-old tree) that have existed long before them and possess a far deeper connection to the earth.
  8. Comment on the contemporary concern that the poem echoes.
    Answer: The poem echoes the urgent contemporary concern of rapid urbanization, deforestation, and ecological imbalance. It highlights how modern development often comes at the cost of destroying our natural heritage, leading to environmental degradation and psychological alienation in concrete cities.
Vocabulary & Linguistic Exercises
  • Botanical & Language Equivalents:
    • Sheoga: Drumstick tree / Moringa (Moringa oleifera). In Marathi: शेवगा (Shevga).
    • Oudumber: Cluster Fig / Gular tree (Ficus racemosa). In Marathi: औदुंबर (Audumbar).
    • Neem: Margosa tree (Azadirachta indica). In Marathi: कडुनिंब (Kadunimb).
    • Banyan: Indian Fig tree (Ficus benghalensis). In Marathi: वटवृक्ष (Vatavruksha) / वड (Vad).
  • Adjective Usage Analysis:
    • Scraggy: Used to describe the rough, thin, and irregular aerial roots of the banyan tree. Other items: scraggy beard, scraggy neck.
    • Raw: Used to describe mythology, indicating something ancient, unrefined, and primal. Other items: raw materials, raw emotions.
    • Aerial: Used to describe roots growing above the ground. Other items: aerial photography, aerial acrobatics.
    • Sacred: Used to describe the holy status of trees. Other items: sacred texts, sacred rituals.
