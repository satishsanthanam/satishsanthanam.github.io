English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 12: Ajamil and the Tigers

Poet Profile & Literary Context

Literary Profile: Arun Kolatkar

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly observant, satirical, bilingual (writing in English and Marathi), blending modernist sensibilities with traditional Indian themes.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in 1932, educated in Pune, earned a diploma in painting from the J.J. School of Arts, Mumbai. His work bridges local Maharashtrian culture and global avant-garde styles.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Skepticism towards religious and political authority, focus on the mundane and marginalized, use of colloquial language, and deep engagement with local myths (as seen in his masterpiece Jejuri ).
Context of the Poem

The poem Ajamil and the Tigers is an excerpt from Jejuri , a celebrated long poem in thirty-one sections by Arun Kolatkar. It functions as a modern political fable, using simple, colloquial language to deliver a sharp, satirical critique of the political class, diplomatic treaties, and the systemic exploitation of the common people.

Narrative Synopsis & Plot Breakdown
Chronological Plot Beats
  1. The tiger people complain to their king that they are starving because Ajamil has hired a new, highly efficient sheep dog who prevents them from getting anywhere near the sheep.
  2. The tiger king, filled with false bravado, promises to teach the sheep dog a lesson and sets off alone into the darkness before dawn.
  3. An hour later, the king returns humiliated, sporting a black patch on his eye and his tail in a sling, but claims he has formulated a strategic plan.
  4. The tiger king leads an attack with fifty tigers, but the sheep dog, acting "quick as lightning," captures all fifty-one tigers before they can touch a single sheep.
  5. The dog binds them in a "daisy chain" and dumps them in a heap before his master, Ajamil.
  6. The captured tiger king quickly invents a diplomatic lie, claiming their aggressive intrusion was merely a "misunderstanding" and they actually came as friends.
  7. The honest sheep dog is disgusted and tries to warn Ajamil, but Ajamil refuses to meet the dog's eyes, pretending to believe the tiger king's lies.
  8. Ajamil releases the tigers, hosts them for a lavish dinner of lamb chops, and signs a long-term friendship treaty, sending them back with gifts of sheep, leather jackets, and wool.

Literary Profile: Ajamil (The Shepherd)

  • Traits & Temperament: Pragmatic, politically astute, calculating, and seemingly naive but deeply cunning. He prioritizes long-term peace and personal leisure over absolute moral justice.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A wealthy shepherd who owns a large herd of sheep and employs a highly efficient sheepdog. He represents the ruling political class or state authority.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the compromise of justice for systemic stability, the co-optation of adversaries, and the transactional nature of political alliances.

Literary Profile: The Sheep Dog

  • Traits & Temperament: Uncompromisingly honest, fiercely loyal, highly efficient, and morally upright. He is "built along simpler lines" and cannot tolerate deception or political posturing.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: The protector of the herd, representing the executive machinery, law enforcement, or the idealistic common citizen who does the actual work of defense.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Symbolizes absolute duty, uncorrupted truth, and the tragic alienation of the honest worker whose efforts are undermined by political compromises.
Thematic Analysis & Satirical Subtext
Literal vs. Figurative Levels

On a literal level, the poem is a humorous story about a shepherd, his dog, and a pack of tigers. On a figurative level, it is a profound political allegory. The tigers represent political predators, exploiters, or hostile neighboring states. The sheep represent the innocent, silent, and exploited masses whose resources are sacrificed. The sheep dog represents the honest administrative or defense machinery that protects the state. Ajamil represents the political leadership that strikes backroom deals with exploiters to maintain power and comfort.

"But Ajamil, the good shepherd / refused to meet his eyes / and pretended to believe every single word / of what the tiger king said."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This passage highlights the core political satire of the poem. Ajamil's refusal to meet the honest dog's eyes shows his conscious choice to ignore the truth in favor of a convenient political compromise. By pretending to believe the tiger king's obvious lies, Ajamil avoids a perpetual state of conflict, choosing instead to co-opt the enemy through a transactional treaty, demonstrating that political survival often relies on deliberate blindness and mutual deception.

Literary Devices & Vocabulary Matrix

The poem utilizes colloquial language and satirical devices to critique political systems. Below is an analysis of these elements:

Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Colloquialism "cramps our style" The tigers complain that the dog restricts their freedom to hunt. Establishes a modern, casual, and humorous tone, stripping the tigers of their traditional majestic aura.
Irony "the good king" Used to describe the tiger king returning beaten up with a black patch and tail in a sling. Satirizes the incompetence and false bravado of political leaders who fail in their aggressive pursuits.
Metaphor / Idiom "lie of the land" The tiger king claims to understand the situation after his defeat. Highlights the political spin-doctoring where a humiliating defeat is reframed as a strategic reconnaissance mission.
Synecdoche / Metaphor "daisy chain" The dog strings the 51 tigers together effortlessly. Demystifies the fearsome nature of the tigers, reducing them to a harmless, decorative chain, highlighting the dog's absolute superiority.
Euphemism / Political Jargon "misunderstanding" The tiger king claims they came to see Ajamil as "friends". Parodies diplomatic double-speak where hostile aggression is reframed as a peaceful diplomatic visit when caught.
Comprehension & Analytical Discussion
Textbook Question Guide
  • 1. Literal vs. Figurative Levels: The poet uses 'tigers' (predators/exploiters) and 'sheep' (vulnerable citizens) because they are classic archetypes of the hunter and the prey. This makes the political allegory of exploitation and systemic compromise universally understandable.
  • 2. Facet of Political Life: The poem illustrates the culture of political compromise, appeasement, and backroom treaties. Instead of eliminating threats, leaders often feed and appease exploiters using public resources to maintain a superficial peace.
  • 3. Use of 'Pretended' and 'Seemed': These words connect directly to "Ajamil wasn't a fool." They show that Ajamil's acceptance of the tigers' lies was a calculated, deliberate political strategy, not ignorance. He chose to play along to secure a peaceful status quo.
  • 4. Refusal to Meet Dog's Eyes: Ajamil avoids the dog's eyes because of political guilt and pragmatism. The dog represents uncompromised duty and truth, which Ajamil is about to betray by releasing and rewarding the captured tigers.
  • 5. Key Phrases Analysis: "Play the flute all day" refers to the ruler's state of carefree leisure and security. "A common bond" refers to the mutual, transactional agreement between the ruler and the predators, sustained by sacrificing the sheep (public resources).
  • 6. Satire Against Political Class: The poem powerfully conveys the anguish of the common man (the dog whose hard work is undermined, and the sheep who are sacrificed) trapped in a system where their security is traded for elite political convenience.
Literary Forms Defined
  • (a) Fable: A short story, typically with animals as characters, conveying a clear moral lesson.
  • (b) Allegory: A narrative that can be interpreted to reveal a hidden, deeper symbolic meaning (typically moral, social, or political).
  • (c) Satire: The use of humor, irony, exaggeration, or ridicule to expose and criticize people's stupidity or vices, particularly in contemporary politics.
