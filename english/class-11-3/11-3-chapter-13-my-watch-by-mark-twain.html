English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 13: My Watch

Introduction to the Essay Genre

An essay is a short composition in prose that undertakes to discuss a matter, express a point of view, or persuade the reader to accept an idea on any subject. It is addressed to a general rather than a specialised audience, discussing its subject in a non-technical fashion with a liberal use of anecdote, illustration, and humour.

Formal vs. Informal Essays
  • Formal Essay : Relatively impersonal, logically organised, and filled with serious purpose.
  • Informal Essay : Personal, written in a relaxed, often whimsical fashion, and tends to deal with everyday things.
Historical Evolution
  • The French writer Montaigne wrote short literary pieces called Essaies (meaning 'attempts').
  • Francis Bacon inaugurated the English use of the term in his own essays.
  • The rise of literary periodicals and magazines in the 18th and 19th centuries gave great impetus to the writing of essays, which were previously published only in books.
Narrative Breakdown of 'My Watch'
Part I: The Perfect Watch and the First Intervention

The narrator's beautiful new watch ran for eighteen months perfectly, without losing or gaining time, and without any mechanical failures. The narrator came to believe it was infallible and imperishable. However, one night, he accidentally let it run down. After setting it by guess, he took it to the chief jeweller to set it by the exact time.

'she is four minutes slow—regulator wants pushing up'

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This marks the beginning of the watch's downfall. The chief jeweller, whom the narrator calls a human cabbage , ignores the narrator's protests that the watch keeps perfect time and insists on adjusting the regulator, initiating a disastrous cycle of incompetent repairs.

Part II: The Escalation of Incompetent Repairs
  1. The Feverish Watch: After the jeweller's adjustment, the watch begins to gain time rapidly, 'sickening to a raging fever' with its pulse going up to 150 in the shade. It runs thirteen days ahead of the almanac, forcing the narrator to pay house rent and bills early.
  2. The Vicious Happiness Watchmaker: The next watchmaker looks with 'vicious happiness', pries the watch open, and claims it needs cleaning, oiling, and regulating. After this, the watch slows down so much that the narrator misses appointments and dinners, feeling like a 'mummy in a museum'.
  3. The Swelled Barrel: Another watchmaker claims the barrel is 'swelled' and reduces it. The watch then behaves erratically—barking, wheezing, and sneezing for half the day, and slowing down the other half to show a deceptive 'average' time.
  4. The King-Bolt and the Musket Kick: A subsequent watchmaker claims the 'king-bolt' is broken. After repair, the watch runs and stops at its own discretion, kicking back 'like a musket' whenever it starts.
  5. The Scissors Hands: Another watchmaker repairs it, but at ten minutes to ten, the hands shut together like a pair of scissors and travel as one.
  6. The Buzzing Bee: After another repair, the watch works quietly for eight hours, then suddenly buzzes like a bee, spinning its hands so fast they look like a spider's web, reeling off twenty-four hours in six minutes before stopping with a bang.
Part III: The Climax and Uncle William's Wisdom

The narrator takes the watch to one final watchmaker, only to recognise him as an old acquaintance—an incompetent former steam-boat engineer . The engineer examines the watch and delivers his verdict: 'She makes too much steam—you want to hang the monkey-wrench on the safety-valve!' Outraged, the narrator 'brained him on the spot' and had him buried at his own expense.

'My uncle William used to say that a good horse was a good horse until it had run away once, and a good watch was a good watch until the repairers got a chance at it.'

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This concluding maxim highlights the central theme of the essay: the folly of trying to 'improve' or constantly tinker with things that are already functioning perfectly, and the destructive nature of self-proclaimed 'experts' who ruin what they do not understand.

Literary Analysis & Character Profiles

Literary Profile: Mark Twain (Samuel L. Clemens)

  • Traits & Temperament: Celebrated American humorist, satirist, and novelist. Known for his sharp wit, whimsical exaggeration, and deep skepticism of human authority and expertise.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Grew up in Hannibal, Missouri; had less than ten years of formal schooling. Worked as a printer's apprentice, steamboat pilot, prospector, and journalist, gaining a rich, practical understanding of human nature.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Uses everyday objects (like a watch) as micro-allegories for larger human institutions, satirising the industrial age's obsession with mechanical perfection and 'expert' intervention.
Thematic Analysis
  • The Satire of Expertise : Twain mocks the absolute confidence of professionals who diagnose non-existent problems and end up destroying the systems they are paid to fix.
  • Personification of Technology : The watch is consistently treated as a living, breathing entity that gets 'sick', has a 'pulse', 'sneezes', and 'makes too much steam', highlighting humanity's complex, often adversarial relationship with machines.
  • The Illusion of the 'Average' : Twain satirises the idea of a 'correct average' in a watch that runs wildly fast and then stops completely, showing that statistical averages can mask total functional failure.
Vocabulary & Literary Devices Matrix
Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Human cabbage 'all this human cabbage could see was that the watch was four minutes slow' An unintelligent, dense, or unthinking person. To humorously convey the narrator's intense frustration with the jeweller's lack of common sense.
Vicious happiness 'He looked a look of vicious happiness and eagerly pried the watch open' The sadistic joy an 'expert' feels when finding a flaw to exploit or repair for money. Satirises the predatory nature of repairmen who thrive on others' mechanical misfortunes.
Personification 'Within a week it sickened to a raging fever and its pulse went up to a hundred and fifty in the shade.' Describing the watch's rapid time-gaining as a biological illness. Creates a vivid, comic image of a mechanical object suffering from human-like ailments.
Simile 'every time it went off it kicked back like a musket.' The watch's sudden, violent mechanical jerks. Exaggerates the physical danger of the malfunctioning watch to heighten the comedy.
Brained him 'I brained him on the spot and had him buried at my own expense.' To hit someone violently on the head; here used as a hyperbolic expression of absolute exasperation. Serves as the absurd, violent climax of the narrator's frustration with incompetent tinkerers.
Study Guide & Comprehension Questions
Understanding the Text
  1. What was the importance of the watch to the author?
    Answer: The watch was highly prized by the author because it had run for eighteen months without losing or gaining time, and without any mechanical failures. He had come to believe it was infallible and imperishable.
  2. What were the attempts made by the author to get his watch repaired?
    Answer: The author took his watch to a succession of watchmakers. The first adjusted the regulator; the second cleaned and oiled it; the third reduced the 'swelled' barrel; the fourth repaired the 'king-bolt'; the fifth adjusted the bent crystal and mainspring; and the final one, a former steamboat engineer, tried to treat it like a steam engine.
  3. Why did the author finally give up on his watch?
    Answer: The author gave up because every repair made the watch's behavior more erratic and absurd, costing him thousands of dollars in repairs for a watch that originally cost two hundred dollars, culminating in an engineer suggesting it was 'making too much steam'.
  4. What was Uncle William's comment on the 'tinkerers' of the world?
    Answer: Uncle William believed that a good watch remains good only until repairers get a chance at it, comparing it to a good horse that is ruined once it runs away. He wondered what became of all the unsuccessful tinkers, gunsmiths, and shoemakers who ruined things under the guise of fixing them.
