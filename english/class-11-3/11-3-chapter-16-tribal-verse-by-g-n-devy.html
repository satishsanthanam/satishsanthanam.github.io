English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 16: Tribal Verse

Introduction to Tribal Literature and Orality

The roots of India’s literary traditions are deeply embedded in the rich oral literatures of its tribes/adivasis . Usually manifested as songs, chants, or performance-based verses, these oral traditions represent an intimate, organic connection between the natural world and tribal existence. Unlike written literature, these works have been transmitted orally across generations, surviving for centuries through memory and performance.

The Threat of Marginalisation

Despite their historical depth, a vast portion of tribal oral literature has been lost. The rapid expansion of urbanisation , print culture , and commercial forces has marginalized tribal communities, leading to the decline of their unique languages and literary heritages. G.N. Devy argues that without concerted, accelerated conservation efforts, India risks losing an invaluable part of its cultural history.

Redefining 'Literature'

A central argument of the chapter is the need to expand the definition of "literature" beyond written texts. Because academic institutions privilege canonized written texts , oral compositions are often dismissed as casual utterances or mere dialects. Devy advocates for a new critical methodology that recognizes oral traditions as legitimate, highly evolved literary forms.

The Tribal Imagination vs. Modern Worldview

The tribal worldview and creative imagination differ fundamentally from those of modern, secular societies. Tribal communities live in cohesive, organically unified groups, showing little interest in capital accumulation or treating labor as a commodity. They perceive an intimate link between nature, humanity, and the divine.

Literary Profile: The Tribal Mindset

  • Traits & Temperament: Guided by intuition over objective reason; views space as sacred rather than secular; possesses a highly acute, non-linear sense of time.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Rooted in communal living, close coexistence with nature, and a lack of interest in accumulating material wealth.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Animism, ancestral worship, and a fluid boundary between the human, natural, and spiritual realms.
The 'Hallucinatory' Nature of Tribal Art

Tribal art and narratives are characterized by a hallucinatory quality where standard spatial and temporal boundaries dissolve. In tribal stories, physical laws do not restrict the narrative: oceans fly, mountains swim, and animals speak. This represents a fusion of different planes of existence, where the principle of association between emotion and narrative motif takes precedence over logical sequence.

The Dominance of Time over Space

Devy notes that while modern societies obsess over dominating territorial space, tribal communities have historically focused on gaining domination over time . This is demonstrated through their rituals of conversing with dead ancestors, using art and memory to bridge the gap between the past, present, and future.

Analysis of Selected Tribal Songs

The chapter presents three distinct songs from different Indian tribes, illustrating the diversity, depth, and nature-centric philosophy of tribal verse.

1. A Munda Song: Song of Birth and Death

My mother, the sun rose / A son was born. / My mother, the moon rose / A daughter was born. / A son was born / The cowshed was depleted; / A daughter was born / The cowshed filled up.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This song, translated from the original Mundari , highlights the high status of women in Munda society. The birth of a son is associated with the rising sun but symbolically linked to the depletion of the cowshed (representing economic or social liability). Conversely, the birth of a daughter is associated with the gentle moon and the filling of the cowshed, indicating that women are viewed as precious assets who play dominant roles in economic, social, and ritual activities.

Tribal Profile: The Munda Tribe

  • Geographical Distribution: Jharkhand, West Bengal, Assam, Tripura, Madhya Pradesh, and Odisha.
  • Historical Significance: Known as Horohon or Mura. They were among the first adivasis to resist British colonialism, notably during the Tamar insurrection (1819–20) and the millennial movement led by Birsa Munda (1874–1901).
  • Cultural Features: Deeply synchronized with natural rhythms rather than clock time; rich oral tradition documented in the 16-volume Encyclopaedia Mundarica .
2. A Kondh Song

This we offer to you. / We can, / Because we are still alive; / If not, / How could we offer at all, / And what? / We give a small baby fowl. / Take this and go away / Whichever way you came...

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: Sung on the occasion of death, this song reflects the Kondh belief that spirits love their earthly homes and are reluctant to leave. To prevent these spirits from becoming malevolent and troubling the living, the community makes a humble offering (a baby fowl) and gently implores the spirit to depart peacefully. It highlights a pragmatic, reciprocal relationship between the living and the dead.

Tribal Profile: The Kondh Tribe

  • Etymology & Location: Derived from the Dravidian word konda (hill). Distributed across Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Odisha.
  • Social & Religious Practices: Religion is a blend of traditional animism and Hinduism. They practice the custom of bride price paid by the groom, and strictly forbid dowry.
3. Adi Song for the Recovery of Lost Health

Oh my beloved one / If you lost your health due to ill luck / I come forward here to save you / With this Emul / To call back your lost health... / I tie this Ridin creeper / To fasten your soul to your body.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This song is a ritualistic mantra chanted in Miri Agom , a sacred, highly rhythmic language used exclusively for religious ceremonies. The Adi believe that illness occurs when the spirit of good health abandons the body due to shock. The maternal uncle of the sick person performs this ritual, using an Emul (amulet) and a Ridin creeper to symbolically bind the soul back to the physical body.

Tribal Profile: The Adi Tribe

  • Location: Concentrated in the East and West Siang districts of Arunachal Pradesh, around the Brahmaputra valley.
  • Linguistic Dualism: They use two distinct languages: Adi Agom for daily, routine conversation, and Miri Agom for highly stylized, rhythmic ritual chants.
  • Ecological Philosophy: Believe that all objects in the universe possess a spirit. They practice hunting strictly for survival, never for greed, maintaining ecological equilibrium.
