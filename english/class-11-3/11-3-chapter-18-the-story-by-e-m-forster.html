English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 18: The Story

Introduction & The Three Voices

Literary Profile: E.M. Forster

  • Traits & Temperament: Analytical, colloquial yet deeply critical, reflective, skeptical of pure plot but acknowledges its necessity.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Prominent English novelist and critic (1879–1970), member of the Bloomsbury Group. This essay is an excerpt from his famous 1927 Clark Lectures at Trinity College, Cambridge, published as Aspects of the Novel .
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: The tension between chronological time ("life in time") and aesthetic/moral value ("life by values"); the primitive, "low atavistic" nature of suspense.

Forster begins by asserting that the fundamental, inescapable aspect of the novel is its story-telling function. However, different readers and critics view this aspect with varying degrees of respect. He illustrates this through three distinct hypothetical voices:

  • The Bus Driver : Placid, vague, and good-tempered. He represents the average, uncritical reader who casually accepts that a novel "kind of tells a story, so to speak," without giving much thought to literature.
  • The Golf-Course Man : Aggressive, brisk, and anti-intellectual. He demands a straightforward story ("give me a good story") and dismisses art, literature, and music as secondary or irrelevant. Forster admits to detesting and fearing this type of reader.
  • The Critic/Author (Forster himself) : Drooping and regretful. He reluctantly admits that "the novel tells a story," wishing instead that its fundamental aspect could be something more elevated, such as melody or perception of truth , rather than this "low atavistic form."

"Yes—oh dear yes—the novel tells a story. That is the fundamental aspect without which it could not exist. That is the highest factor common to all novels, and I wish that it was not so..."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: Forster establishes a critical tension: while he values high-art elements like melody and truth, he acknowledges that the novel is structurally bound to the primitive mechanism of chronological storytelling. This creates a dualism between the "low" necessity of plot and the "high" aspiration of art.

The Story as Backbone: Primitive Origins & Scheherazade

Forster strips the story of its artistic embellishments to examine its raw, biological function in human culture, comparing it to a backbone or a tape-worm .

The Neanderthal Audience

Storytelling is immensely old, dating back to Neolithic or Palaeolithic \times. Forster imagines primitive humans ("shock-heads") gathered around a campfire, exhausted from fighting mammoths, kept awake solely by the suspense of "What would happen next?":

  • The primitive storyteller's survival depended entirely on suspense.
  • If the audience guessed what happened next, they either fell asleep or killed the storyteller.
The Analogy of Scheherazade

Forster uses the legendary narrator of the 1001 Arabian Nights to illustrate suspense as a survival tool:

  • Scheherazade saved her life from her murderous husband, the king, not through her exquisite descriptions, moral advancement, or character delineation, but purely by wielding the weapon of suspense .
  • By stopping her narrative at the crack of dawn in the middle of a sentence, she kept the king wondering "what would happen next," preserving her life day after day.
  • The simple phrase, "At this moment Scheherazade saw the morning appearing and, discreet, was silent," serves as the literal backbone of the entire epic.
Defining the Story & The Double Allegiance

Forster provides a precise definition of a story and explores how human life is divided between two competing frameworks of experience.

The Definition of a Story

A story is defined as: "a narrative of events arranged in their time sequence" (e.g., dinner coming after breakfast, Tuesday after Monday, decay after death).

  • The Sole Merit: Making the audience want to know what happens next.
  • The Sole Fault: Failing to make the audience want to know what happens next.
The Two Lives: Time vs. Value

Forster argues that daily human life is composed of a double allegiance:

  1. The Life in Time : Measured chronologically by minutes, hours, and days. It is the sequence of events that we cannot escape in practical existence.
  2. The Life by Values : Measured not by time, but by intensity, significance, and emotional impact. In memory and anticipation, time is non-linear; a five-minute encounter can outweigh years of mundane existence.

While mystics, dreamers, and artists can occasionally deny or escape the "tyranny of Father Time" in daily life, a novelist cannot. The novel must pay allegiance to both: the story narrates the life in time, while the deeper elements of the novel capture the life by values.

Time Manipulation in the Novel

Although a novelist must remain anchored to the chronological sequence of time to remain intelligible, great writers have attempted to hide, subvert, or manipulate the "clock" within their works:

  • Emily Brontë in Wuthering Heights : Attempted to hide the clock, masking the passage of time to create a mythic, timeless atmosphere.
  • Laurence Sterne in Tristram Shandy : Turned the clock upside down, playing with digressions and non-linear narrative structures.
  • Marcel Proust in In Search of Lost Time : Ingeniously altered the hands of the clock, blending past and present so that his hero could simultaneously experience different stages of life.

Despite these brilliant artistic manipulations, Forster emphasizes that none of these devices contravene the basic thesis: the fundamental basis of a novel remains a story, and a story is always bound to a sequence in time.

Vocabulary & Literary Devices

The following table analyzes key vocabulary and metaphorical expressions used by Forster to critique the nature of storytelling:

Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Atavistic (Adjective) "...not this low atavistic form." Relating to or characterized by reversion to something ancient or ancestral; primitive. Emphasizes that storytelling appeals to our most basic, unevolved instincts of curiosity.
Tape-worm (Metaphor) "It runs like a backbone—or may I say a tape-worm..." An interminable, parasitic, unlovely internal organism that stretches through the body. De-romanticizes the plot, highlighting its raw, repetitive, and potentially tedious nature when stripped of artistic value.
Shock-heads (Noun) "...an audience of shock-heads, gaping round the campfire..." People with rough, unkempt, shaggy hair; primitive cave-dwellers. Creates a vivid, slightly humorous historical caricature of early human audiences.
Qua (Preposition) "Qua story, it can only have one merit..." In the capacity of; as being; from the standpoint of. Allows Forster to isolate the story element from other literary qualities like characterization or style.
Ingenious (Adjective) "...ingenious in her incidents..." Clever, original, and inventive. Highlights the intellectual skill required to construct plots that maintain suspense.
Study Guide & Comprehension Questions
Understanding the Text
  1. What do you understand of the three voices in response to the question 'What does a novel do'?

    The three voices represent different tiers of literary appreciation: the casual reader (the bus driver) who vaguely accepts the story; the anti-intellectual reader (the golf-course man) who aggressively demands only a plot; and the critical writer (Forster) who reluctantly acknowledges the story as a necessary but primitive foundation.

  2. What would you say are 'the finer growths' that the story supports in a novel?

    'The finer growths' refer to the artistic, intellectual, and emotional dimensions of a novel—such as character development, thematic depth, moral philosophy, atmosphere, and aesthetic style ("melody" and "perception of truth")—which are built upon the basic framework of the plot.

  3. How does Forster trace the human interest in the story to primitive \times?

    Forster traces it back to Neanderthal \times, where primitive humans gathered around campfires were kept awake solely by the suspense of "what happens next." This basic curiosity was a matter of life and death for the storyteller, establishing suspense as our oldest literary tool.

  4. Discuss the importance of time in the narration of a story.

    Time is the inescapable sequence of narrative. A story, by definition, is events arranged chronologically. While daily life allows us to focus on "value" over "time," a novel cannot exist without a chronological thread; denying time entirely makes a narrative unintelligible.
