English: Class 11 (Book 3) — Chapter 19: Bridges

Author Profile & Choreographic Philosophy

Literary Profile: Kumudini Lakhia

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly inquisitive, independent, rebellious against unexamined traditions, and deeply reflective. She views dance not as an innate, effortless gift but as an intense, lifelong struggle.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in 1930; raised during a volatile era marked by World War II and India's independence movement. Her father was an engineer, and her mother was a determined woman who pushed her into classical dance.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Bridges between life experiences and artistic creation; translation of physical and emotional sensations (like hunger, confusion, and power) into abstract Kathak choreography.

Kumudini Lakhia rejects the romanticized notion that dancers are simply born to dance . Instead, she asserts that she was born to live , viewing her choreographic work as a direct translation of her life's struggles, confusion, and sensory experiences.

Childhood, Family Dynamics, and Early Lessons
The Reluctant Dancer and Maternal Determination

As a child in Bombay, Kumudini did not want to dance. The pursuit was entirely driven by her doting mother, who was inspired by a film performance of Mumtaz Ali . Her mother endured grueling daily commutes on overcrowded local trains to take her to lessons with Guru Sunder Prasad in Chowpatty, demonstrating an unwavering belief in her daughter's innate talent.

Historical Backdrop and Political Lessons

Growing up during World War II and the Indian Independence movement, Kumudini's family relocated frequently due to her father's engineering assignments. While living in Delhi, their neighbor was Liaquat Ali (later the Prime Minister of Pakistan). An incident involving stealing guavas from his garden taught Kumudini an early lesson about political posturing: while Liaquat Ali offered a generous, polite invitation to take the fruit, his gardener's vengeful expression made it clear that the offer was a social game.

Boarding School, Discipline, and the Trauma of Loss
Queen Mary's College and British Discipline

At age nine, Kumudini was sent to Queen Mary's College in Lahore. Her mother, determined to keep her dancing, sent a personal dance teacher, Radhelal Misra , to live nearby and conduct lessons. Kumudini resented these lessons, finding them repetitive and lacking intellectual explanation. However, she grew to appreciate the strict discipline of the school's British principal, Miss Cox , whose dignified demeanour deeply influenced her own future teaching style.

The Death of Her Mother and Choreographic Translation

"Mother was already dead when I arrived, 36 hours and three train-rides later... I did not know where to turn or what to do with my hands, which hung loose from my body. Then suddenly they clutched my stomach. Hunger pangs? I hadn't eaten for three days and there was an emptiness I wanted to fill."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This profound trauma at age fourteen conflated emotional grief with physical hunger and physical awkwardness. Decades later, Kumudini translated this specific physical sensation of dangling, empty arms and internal void into her acclaimed choreographic piece Duvidha (Conflict), which explores the restricted, conflicted lives of middle-class Indian women torn between tradition and modern independence.

Higher Education and Challenging Social Norms
An Off-Beat Choice: Agriculture College

Encouraged by her father to pursue something "off-beat" rather than conventional degrees, Kumudini enrolled in an agricultural college in Naini, Allahabad . As the only female student in a class of thirty, she navigated complex gender dynamics, such as male classmates deflating her bicycle tires to walk with her, and the scandal of wearing shorts to work in the muddy fields.

Clashes with Traditionalism and the Five Senses

Kumudini frequently clashed with her grandmother's conservative views regarding female modesty and religious rituals. She questioned why women were taught to underplay their bodies and rejected the idea of "bargaining" with God at temples for exam success. Instead, she viewed temple visits as rich sensory experiences—architecture, incense, bells, and touch—which later inspired her choreographic work Panch Paras (The Five Senses). Her search for the meaning of power and life's direction also culminated in her composition Atah Kim .

Professional Breakthrough: The Ram Gopal Company
A Chance Encounter and Global Exposure

After graduating, Kumudini met Komlata Dutt at a Bombay train station. Dutt, a well-connected figure in the arts, invited her to join the prestigious Ram Gopal Dance Company in London. This marked a turning point, fusing her years of tedious training into a new creative synergy. She toured internationally, learning diverse folk forms like kummi , ghumar , and dandia , alongside classical Bharatanatyam .

Ram Gopal's Philosophy and the Power of Context

"You've perfected the technique, now throw it overboard and dance."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This advice from Ram Gopal became a cornerstone of Kumudini's artistic philosophy. It emphasizes that rigorous technical mastery is merely a prerequisite for true creative experimentation. Additionally, touring post-war Germany taught her the importance of context: seeing hungry children begging in a devastated European nation evoked a completely different, shocking sensation compared to the same sight in India, illustrating how environment alters artistic and emotional perception.

Domestic Balance and Later Reflections

Upon returning to India, Kumudini married Rajanikant , a supportive husband who instilled in her a deep love for music. She balanced her domestic responsibilities as a mother to Shriraj and Maitreyi with her demanding career. She compares managing a diverse family under one roof to performing with other artists on stage, where one must develop collaborative skills and look beyond solo performance to belong to a larger, harmonious image.
