English: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 4: Tomorrow

Author & Contextual Background

Literary Profile: Joseph Conrad (1857-1924)

  • Traits & Temperament: Master of atmospheric depth, psychological realism, and complex narrative structures. Known for his ability to evoke intense moods through meticulous attention to detail and the use of nested narratives.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born to Polish parents in Ukraine, Conrad led a seafaring life before settling in England, learning English in his twenties. His maritime experiences heavily influenced his literary focus on isolation, duty, and the human psyche.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Universal themes of isolation , the destructive nature of romanticized delusions, the conflict between land-bound domesticity and the lawless freedom of the sea, and the tragic irony of human hope.
Character Profiles

Literary Profile: Captain Hagberd

  • Traits & Temperament: Obsessive, eccentric, and deeply delusional. He has constructed an impenetrable psychological defense mechanism centered on the belief that his runaway son will return "tomorrow."
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A retired coasting-skipper who settled in the small seaport of Colebrook . He lives in one of two cheap, yellow-brick cottages he built, living as a miser to save everything for his son.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Wears a rigid suit made of sailcloth , symbolizing his emotional armor and refusal to adapt to the present. His constant digging of his barren front yard represents his futile, repetitive preparation for an unreachable future.

Literary Profile: Bessie Carvil

  • Traits & Temperament: Patient, self-sacrificing, weary, and deeply compassionate. She is trapped in a life of endless drudgery but harbors a quiet, desperate desire for escape and affection.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Daughter of the blind, abusive retired boat-builder Josiah Carvil. She lives next door to Captain Hagberd and acts as his sole confidante, humoring his delusions out of pity.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: The fence between the cottages represents her confinement and the boundaries of her life. Her red hands and tired face symbolize her domestic martyrdom, while her mahogany hair represents her hidden, vibrant vitality.

Literary Profile: Harry Hagberd

  • Traits & Temperament: Restless, wild, independent, and fiercely anti-domestic. He is a wanderer who lives entirely in the present, rejecting any form of confinement or long-term responsibility.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Captain Hagberd's long-lost son who ran away to sea sixteen years prior to escape his father's tyrannical plans to make him a lawyer's clerk. He returns briefly only because he is broke and heard of his father's advertisements.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Described as a Gambusino (a restless Mexican gold-prospector), symbolizing the spirit of absolute freedom and the refusal to be anchored by home, family, or money.

Literary Profile: Josiah Carvil

  • Traits & Temperament: Loud, abusive, tyrannical, and monstrously selfish. He uses his physical helplessness to completely enslave his daughter.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A blind, retired boat-builder of evil repute who rents the second cottage from Captain Hagberd.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Described as a gross, unwieldy bulk resembling a hippopotamus , representing the suffocating, heavy weight of domestic tyranny that crushes Bessie's youth.
Detailed Plot Synopsis & Narrative Arc
1. The Delusion of Colebrook

Captain Hagberd settles in the small seaport of Colebrook after selling his home in Colchester. He builds two cheap, ugly yellow-brick cottages, occupying one and renting the other to the blind domestic tyrant Josiah Carvil and his daughter Bessie . The townspeople view Hagberd as an eccentric "character" and a miser. He wears a bizarre, stiff suit made of sailcloth and spends his days digging his front yard without planting anything, claiming he is waiting for his son Harry to return.

2. The Eternal "Tomorrow"

Over the years, Hagberd's hope morphs from a rational expectation into an absolute delusion. He stops saying his son will return "next week" or "next year" and instead insists he will return tomorrow . He secretly furnishes the second cottage with expensive furniture, keeping it locked and hidden from all eyes, intending it for Harry and his future wife—whom he has decided will be Bessie. Bessie humors his delusions out of pity, finding a strange comfort in his gentle madness, which contrasts with her father's brutal shouting.

3. The Arrival of the Stranger

One evening, a charismatic, swaggering stranger arrives at the gate, having heard of Hagberd's advertisements in the papers. He introduces himself to the Captain, but Hagberd, terrified of being mocked and completely blinded by his obsession with "tomorrow," refuses to recognize him. Because the stranger has arrived today , Hagberd rejects him as a "grinning information fellow" and throws a shovel at his head, locking himself inside his cottage.

4. The Climax and Tragic Departure

Bessie confronts the stranger and realizes he is indeed the real Harry Hagberd . She tries to explain his father's madness and the elaborate home prepared for him. However, Harry is horrified by the prospect of being trapped in a domestic "rabbit-hutch" as a clerk. He reveals he only came to ask for five pounds to pay off his debts. Realizing the hopelessness of the situation, Bessie gives him her own money. Before vanishing back into the night, Harry passionately embraces and kisses Bessie, leaving her physically and emotionally shattered. Captain Hagberd chuckles from his window, celebrating that the "intruder" has gone and declaring that Harry will surely arrive tomorrow, while old Carvil's voice roars for Bessie from the dark house.

Thematic Deep Dive
The Disease of Hope vs. Reality

Conrad explores how hope, when detached from reality, becomes a destructive force. Captain Hagberd's obsession with "tomorrow" is a psychological shield that protects him from the pain of his son's permanent abandonment. However, this shield ultimately blinds him to the actual fulfillment of his hope; when his son finally stands before him, the Captain rejects him because reality does not align with the perfect, static delusion of "tomorrow."

Entrapment and Domestic Tyranny

The narrative presents a stark contrast between different forms of imprisonment and freedom:

  • Bessie Carvil is physically and emotionally trapped by her blind father's monstrous selfishness and Hagberd's suffocating delusions. Her life is confined to the narrow space between the two cottages.
  • Harry Hagberd represents absolute, wild freedom. He refuses to be anchored by domesticity, viewing a settled life as a cage. His transient nature brings a brief spark of hope to Bessie, only to leave her more profoundly isolated when he departs.
The Sea and the Land

The sea and the land serve as opposing symbolic domains. For Captain Hagberd, the sea is an enemy that took his son, and he clings desperately to the stability of the land. For Harry, the land is a suffocating prison of "rabbit-hutches" and routine, while the vast, unpredictable sea is his true home. This elemental conflict ensures that father and son can never truly reconcile.

Literary Devices & Language Work
Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Allusion "beard of Father Neptune... resembled a deposed sea-god" Compares Captain Hagberd's weather-beaten appearance to the Roman god of the sea. Highlights his maritime past and his current fallen, earth-bound state of madness.
Metaphor "recovered already from the disease of hope" Frames hope not as a positive virtue, but as a pathological affliction that wastes away the mind. Emphasizes the tragic, consuming nature of Hagberd's obsession.
Symbolism "The spade and the quagmire" Hagberd's constant digging turns his yard into a muddy, useless mess. Represents the futility of his endless preparations for a future that never arrives.
Irony "Harry is coming tomorrow." The Captain declares this while his actual son is standing right outside his window. Underscores the absolute triumph of delusion over reality in Hagberd's mind.
Study & Comprehension Guide
Stop and Think Answers
  1. What brought Captain Hagberd to Colebrook? He was led there by a hoax letter suggesting his runaway son Harry was in the area. Though it was a dead end, his growing delusion prevented him from leaving.
  2. Why did the people of Colebrook not have a favorable opinion of him? They viewed him as an eccentric, morbid, and unreasonable outsider who lived like a miser and wore bizarre sailcloth clothing.
  3. What sort of a seaman had Captain Hagberd been? He was a coasting-skipper who hated the open sea, always staying within sight of land and never venturing out of soundings.
  4. What did Captain Hagberd constantly hint at that made Bessie blush? He constantly hinted that Bessie would marry his son Harry when he returned, and that they would live together in the furnished cottage.
  5. Who was the stranger who met Captain Hagberd? The stranger was his actual son, Harry Hagberd, who returned after sixteen years of wandering.
  6. What did young Hagberd think it meant when his father said he would return "tomorrow"? He initially assumed his father was involved in some sort of financial scheme or swindle, not realizing the depth of his mental illness.
