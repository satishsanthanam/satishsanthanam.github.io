English: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 8: Poems by Blake

Author Profile & Literary Context

Literary Profile: William Blake (1757-1827)

  • Traits & Temperament: Visionary poet, painter, and engraver who vehemently abhorred the rationalism and materialism of the Age of Enlightenment. He sought to depict the spiritual struggle of human beings beset by evil yet striving for the divine.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Working during the early Industrial Revolution, Blake witnessed the rise of mechanical industry and institutionalized oppression, which heavily influenced his critique of social structures.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Explored the contrary states of the human soul through paired volumes of poetry. He pioneered a unique self-publishing method, engraving both \text and illustrations onto copper plates and illuminating them with watercolours, making him a pioneer of multi-media art .
Poem I: The Divine Image (Songs of Innocence)

Originally published in Songs of Innocence , this poem presents an optimistic, pure, and unified vision of humanity and divinity, where God and man share the same essential virtues.

Stanza-by-Stanza Analytical Breakdown
  1. Stanza 1: Humanity in distress prays to Mercy, Pity, Peace, and Love , returning thankfulness for these virtues of delight.
  2. Stanza 2: These four virtues are identified directly with God the Father, and simultaneously with Man, His child and care, establishing a divine-human unity.
  3. Stanza 3: The virtues are given physical, human forms: Mercy has a human heart, Pity a human face, Love is the divine form, and Peace is the human dress.
  4. Stanza 4: Every person, regardless of geographical location ( every clime ), who prays in distress is praying to the "human form divine."
  5. Stanza 5: Love and respect must extend to all human beings, including those labeled as outsiders ( heathen, turk, or jew ), because God dwells wherever these virtues reside.
Key Thematic Motifs
  • Divine Immanence: God is not a distant, abstract entity but is directly present within human beings who embody love and mercy.
  • Universal Brotherhood: A radical assertion of equality that transcends religious and racial boundaries, urging love for all humanity.

"For Mercy has a human heart, Pity a human face, And Love, the human form divine, And Peace, the human dress."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This stanza serves as the core philosophical anchor of the poem. By mapping abstract spiritual virtues directly onto human anatomy (heart, face, form, dress), Blake rejects the division between the physical and the spiritual. He asserts that the human body itself is sacred and is the primary vessel through which the divine is expressed in the material world.

Poem II: The Human Abstract (Songs of Experience)

Published in Songs of Experience , this poem serves as the cynical, realistic counterpart to "The Divine Image," exposing how human virtues are corrupted and exploited within a fallen, unequal society.

Stanza-by-Stanza Analytical Breakdown
  1. Stanza 1: Pity and Mercy are exposed as parasitic virtues; they cannot exist unless someone is actively kept poor and unhappy.
  2. Stanza 2: Mutual fear temporarily creates a fragile peace, but as selfish desires grow, Cruelty actively weaves a trap ( knits a snare ) to exploit others.
  3. Stanza 3: Cruelty hypocritically performs religious piety, watering the ground with tears of false sympathy, which allows the root of Humility to grow.
  4. Stanza 4: This false humility sprouts into the thick, dark shade of Mystery (representing institutionalized religious dogma), which is fed upon by parasites like the Caterpillar and Fly.
  5. Stanza 5: The tree of Mystery bears the attractive but toxic fruit of Deceit , and the Raven (a symbol of death and bad omens) nests in its shade.
  6. Stanza 6: While nature gods searched everywhere to find this toxic tree, it does not exist in the physical world; it grows solely within the Human Brain .
Key Thematic Motifs
  • The Deconstruction of Virtue: Blake argues that institutionalized charity and pity are self-serving, requiring the systematic preservation of poverty to justify their own existence.
  • The Internal Origin of Evil: Unlike traditional religious views that place the source of sin in external devils or nature, Blake asserts that systemic cruelty, deceit, and religious mystification are constructs of the human intellect ( the Human Brain ).

"The Gods of the earth and sea Sought thro’ Nature to find this Tree; But their search was all in vain: There grows one in the Human Brain."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This concluding stanza shifts the blame of human suffering away from nature or divine will. The "Tree of Mystery" (representing systemic deceit, hierarchy, and religious dogma) is entirely a human creation. Blake critiques rationalism and institutionalized systems for inventing structures of oppression that do not exist in the natural order.

Comparative Analysis & Poetic Structure

The two poems represent Blake's dialectical vision of the human soul, contrasting the innocent ideal with the experienced reality.

Language Work & Metrical Analysis
  • Capitalisation: Blake capitalises abstract nouns like Mercy, Pity, Peace, Love, Cruelty, Humility, Mystery, and Deceit to personify them as active, living forces shaping human behavior.
  • Syllable Structure & Meter: "The Divine Image" utilizes a traditional ballad meter. The first line of each stanza typically contains eight syllables ( iambic tetrameter ) and the second line contains six syllables ( iambic trimeter ), creating a song-like, rhythmic quality that mirrors the simplicity of innocence.

Metaphor "Then Cruelty knits a snare, / And spreads his baits with care." Cruelty is compared to a hunter setting traps, representing the calculated nature of human malice in a corrupt society. To illustrate how social systems actively trap and exploit the vulnerable.
