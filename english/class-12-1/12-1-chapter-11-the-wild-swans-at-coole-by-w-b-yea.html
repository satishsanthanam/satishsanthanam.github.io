English: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 11: The Wild Swans at Coole

Author Profile & Historical Context

Literary Profile: W.B. Yeats (1865-1939)

  • Traits & Temperament: An iconic Irish poet, dramatist, and mystic. Yeats was a central figure of the Irish Literary Revival and co-founder of the Abbey Theatre . His work is characterized by deep symbolism, romantic melancholy, and a preoccupation with aging, transition, and spiritual permanence. He was awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1923.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Written in 1916 and published in 1917, the poem is set at Coole Park in County Galway, Ireland, the estate of his close friend and patron, Lady Gregory. This period was marked by personal disillusionment for Yeats, including his aging (he was 51), the political turmoil of the Easter Rising, and his repeated romantic rejections by Maud Gonne.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: The swans serve as symbols of eternal youth, passion, and companionable beauty, contrasting sharply with the poet's own sense of decay, loneliness, and mortality.
Stanza-by-Stanza Literary Analysis
Stanza 1: The Autumnal Setting

The trees are in their autumn beauty,
The woodland paths are dry,
Under the October twilight the water
Mirrors a still sky;
Upon the brimming water among the stones
Are nine-and-fifty swans.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The poem opens with a vivid description of Coole Park in autumn. The "autumn beauty" and "October twilight" establish a melancholic, reflective mood, symbolizing the twilight of the poet's own life. The "still sky" and "brimming water" suggest a state of suspended animation and quietude. The count of "nine-and-fifty swans" (an odd number, 59) subtly hints at a solitary, companionless swan, mirroring Yeats's own isolation and lack of a romantic partner.

Stanza 2: The Passage of Time and Sudden Flight

The nineteenth autumn has come upon me
Since I first made my count;
I saw, before I had well finished,
All suddenly mount
And scatter wheeling in great broken rings
Upon their clamorous wings.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The poet reflects on the passage of nineteen years since he first counted the swans at this location. This temporal marker highlights his personal aging and the relentless march of time. Before he can finish his count, the swans suddenly take flight. The dynamic, energetic movement of the swans "wheeling in great broken rings" with "clamorous wings" contrasts sharply with the static, quiet landscape of the first stanza, symbolizing the untamed, free spirit of nature.

Stanza 3: The Sore Heart and Nostalgia

I have looked upon those brilliant creatures,
And now my heart is sore.
All’s changed since I, hearing at twilight,
The first time on this shore,
The bell-beat of their wings above my head,
Trod with a lighter tread.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The poet experiences a deep emotional ache ("my heart is sore") upon observing the "brilliant creatures." He contrasts his current heavy-hearted state with his first visit nineteen years ago, when he "trod with a lighter tread"—a reference to his youthful energy, optimism, and lack of weariness. The auditory imagery of the "bell-beat of their wings" underscores the powerful, resonant impact of nature on his consciousness.

Stanza 4: The Immortality of the Swans

Unwearied still, lover by lover,
They paddle in the cold
Companionable streams or climb the air;
Their hearts have not grown old;
Passion or conquest, wander where they will,
Attend upon them still.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: Yeats contrasts human frailty with the perceived immortality of the swans. They are "unwearied still" and move "lover by lover," highlighting their companionable and loving existence. Unlike the poet, "their hearts have not grown old." They remain driven by "passion or conquest," showing that time has not diminished their vital energy, freedom, or capacity for love.

Stanza 5: The Melancholy Departure

But now they drift on the still water,
Mysterious, beautiful;
Among what rushes will they build,
By what lake’s edge or pool
Delight men’s eyes when I awake some day
To find they have flown away?

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The final stanza returns to a quiet image of the swans drifting on the water, described as "mysterious, beautiful." The poet ends with a poignant, rhetorical question, wondering where the swans will nest next. This departure serves as a metaphor for death and the transience of human experience; the swans will continue to bring joy to others long after the poet has passed away or when he "awakes" to find his own life's passions have permanently vanished.

Thematic Analysis & Core Motifs
  • Aging and Mortality: The poem is a profound meditation on the physical and emotional decline that accompanies aging. The transition from the poet's "lighter tread" of youth to his current "sore heart" illustrates the painful realization of human decay.
  • Permanence vs. Impermanence: While the poet is subject to the ravages of time and emotional weariness, the swans represent an unchanging, eternal ideal. They remain "unwearied," passionate, and companionable, transcending the temporal limitations of human life.
  • Nature as a Mirror of the Soul: The autumnal landscape of Coole Park —with its dry paths, twilight, and still waters—reflects the poet's internal state of loneliness, stagnation, and emotional drought.
  • Unrequited Love and Solitude: The odd count of "nine-and-fifty swans" suggests that one swan is left without a mate, symbolizing Yeats's own persistent loneliness and his unfulfilled love for Maud Gonne.
Literary Devices & Stylistic Features
Literary Device / Phrase Exact Textual Example Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Visual Imagery "The trees are in their autumn beauty, / The woodland paths are dry" Establishes a quiet, dry, and fading landscape. Sets a somber, reflective tone that aligns with the theme of aging and the twilight of life.
Auditory Imagery & Onomatopoeia "The bell-beat of their wings above my head" The heavy, rhythmic sound of the swans taking flight. Conveys the immense physical power, vitality, and majestic presence of the swans.
Symbolism "nine-and-fifty swans" An odd number of swans, implying one is solitary. Symbolizes the poet's own isolation, loneliness, and lack of a companion.
Juxtaposition / Contrast "Their hearts have not grown old" vs. "And now my heart is sore" Contrasts the eternal youth of the swans with the poet's emotional exhaustion. Highlights the tragic divide between the immortal beauty of nature and the transient, decaying nature of human life.
Alliteration "bell-beat", "trod with a lighter tread" Repetition of consonant sounds to create a musical rhythm. Enhances the lyrical quality of the poem, mimicking the rhythmic movement of the swans and the passage of time.
Textbook Questions & Analytical Answers
  1. How do the 'trees in their autumn beauty', 'dry woodland paths', 'October twilight', 'still sky' connect to the poet's own life?

    These natural descriptions serve as objective correlatives for the poet's internal state. Autumn and twilight represent the later stages of human life, indicating that the poet is entering his own twilight years. The "dry woodland paths" suggest a lack of emotional warmth, vitality, and fertility in his life, reflecting his loneliness and creative exhaustion. The "still sky" mirrors his passive, stagnant, and melancholic state of mind.

  2. What do 'the light tread' and 'the sore heart' refer to?

    "The light tread" refers to the poet's youth nineteen years ago, when he first visited Coole Park. At that time, he was physically agile, full of hope, energy, and romantic expectations. In contrast, "the sore heart" refers to his present state of grief, weariness, and sorrow. This soreness is caused by the passage of time, the loss of his youth, and his unfulfilled desires and romantic disappointments.

  3. What is the contrast between the liveliness of the swans and human life?

    The swans represent eternal youth, passion, and companionable unity. They are "unwearied," their hearts "have not grown old," and they are always accompanied by their mates ("lover by lover"). Human life, as represented by the poet, is characterized by decay, weariness, loneliness, and emotional stagnation. While humans grow old, lose their passions, and suffer from isolation, the swans remain eternally vibrant, free, and purposeful.

  4. What contributes to the beauty and mystery of the swans' lives?

    The beauty of the swans lies in their physical grace, their majestic flight ("wheeling in great broken rings"), and their companionable drifting on the water. Their mystery is rooted in their independence from human limitations—they are driven by untamed instincts of "passion or conquest," and they disappear to unknown destinations ("among what rushes will they build") to bring delight to others, remaining elusive and untamed by time.

Language Study & Suggested Reading

Rhyme Scheme Analysis: The poem follows a consistent, structured rhyme scheme of ABCBDE (or slant-rhyme variations like ABABCD) in each six-line stanza (sestet). This formal structure provides a calm, controlled, and meditative rhythm that contrasts with the sudden, chaotic flight of the swans, reinforcing the tension between human order and natural freedom.
