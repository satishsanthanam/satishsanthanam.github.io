English: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 12: Time and Time Again

Author Profile & Literary Context

Literary Profile: A.K. Ramanujan

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly analytical, deeply reflective, and a master craftsman of the English language. He possessed a unique ability to distill complex, lifelong thoughts and feelings into precise, evocative imagery.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in India (1929–1993), he was a world-renowned scholar, translator, and linguist. He served as a professor of linguistics at the University of Chicago and was recognized globally as a profound authority on South Indian languages, literature, and culture.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: His work is heavily influenced by folklore, anthropology, and classical Tamil poetry. He frequently explores themes of cultural hybridity, memory, the passage of time, and the subtle ironies of human existence.

Ramanujan's poetry is celebrated for its linguistic precision and its ability to bridge ancient Indian traditions with modern Western sensibilities. Time and Time Again represents a complex distillation of his thoughts on human discord, temporal structures, and cosmic intervention.

Poem Analysis & Stanzaic Breakdown
Stanza 1: The Discordant Clocks of the City
  • The Symbol of the Clocktower: Represents human attempts to organize, control, and standardize time . However, each clocktower beats "slightly off the others' time," symbolizing the inherent fragmentation and lack of absolute synchronization in human institutions.
  • The Causes of Variation: The differences in timekeeping are attributed to "accidents of alloy," a "maker's shaking hand in Switzerland," or "mutual distances commemorating a donor's whim." This highlights how human systems are flawed, subjective, and prone to historical or physical contingencies.
  • The Communal Dimension: The discordant clocks parallel the "perennial feuds and seasonal alliance of Hindu, Christian, and Muslim." Just as the clocks fail to strike in unison, these religious communities exist in a state of fragile, shifting relationships, easily disrupted by external forces.
  • The Fragility of Peace: The delicate balance of the city is easily "cut off" by a "change of wind," a "change of mind," or the sudden wail of a "siren" intervening in a "backstreet quarrel."
Stanza 2: Destruction and Cosmic Intervention
  • The Ruined Clocktower: The image of a clocktower rendered "eyeless, silent" with a "zigzag sky showing through the knocked-out clockwork" represents the complete breakdown of human-made temporal and social orders.
  • The Catalysts of Ruin: The destruction is caused by human violence ("a riot," "a peace-march time bomb") or by nature ("a precise act of nature in a night of lightnings").
  • The Irony of the 'Peace-March Time Bomb': A powerful oxymoron highlighting the hypocrisy and self-destructive nature of human political movements.
  • Nature's Precision vs. Human Chaos: While human violence is chaotic and destructive, nature's intervention is described as "precise," suggesting a supreme, cosmic order that effortlessly dismantles human constructs.
Literary Devices & Stylistic Elements

The poem employs a rich array of stylistic devices to contrast human-made chaos with natural precision.

Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Metaphor "eyeless, silent" Describes the ruined clock face, comparing it to a blind, mute human face. Emphasizes the loss of the clock's function to observe and declare time, rendering it useless.
Oxymoron "peace-march time bomb" Combines the peaceful intent of a march with the violent destruction of a bomb. Exposes the tragic irony and inherent contradictions in human political endeavors.
Imagery "zigzag sky showing through the knocked-out clockwork" Visual depiction of a shattered clocktower revealing the sky behind it. Contrasts the rigid, mechanical structure of human time with the vast, organic, and free-form nature of the sky.
Enjambment "beating their gongs round the clock, each slightly / off the others’ time..." The continuation of a sentence without a pause beyond the end of a line. Mimics the continuous, overlapping, and discordant flow of the various clock strikes.
Textual Excerpts & Critical Commentary

"Or listen to the clocktowers / of any old well-managed city / beating their gongs round the clock, each slightly / off the others’ time, deeper or lighter / in its bronze..."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This opening excerpt establishes the central motif of the clocktowers. Even in a "well-managed" city—a symbol of human order, planning, and civilization—absolute uniformity is an illusion. The slight variations in the gongs ("deeper or lighter") represent the inescapable differences inherent in human creations, foreshadowing the social and religious divisions discussed later in the poem.

"...after a riot, / a peace-march time bomb, or a precise act / Of nature in a night of lightnings."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This concluding passage juxtaposes human-induced destruction with natural phenomena. The "riot" and "peace-march time bomb" represent the chaotic, self-inflicted violence of human society. In contrast, the lightning strike is a "precise act of nature." This suggests that while human violence is messy and ideological, nature operates with an absolute, clean, and unquestionable authority that easily resets human history.

Responding to the Poem (Comprehension & Discussion)
  1. Initial Impressions: Upon reading the first few lines, the reader might assume the poem is a simple, descriptive piece about urban landscapes, municipal order, and the passage of time. However, it quickly transitions into a deeper meditation on human division and cosmic power.
  2. Import of the Title: The significance of the title Time and Time Again strikes the reader most forcefully in the second stanza, where the cyclical nature of human conflict (riots, bombs) and natural intervention is revealed, showing that history repeats itself endlessly.
  3. Differences in Timekeeping: The variations arise from physical imperfections ("accidents of alloy," "maker's shaking hand") and human subjectivity ("donor's whim"). The implicit comparison is to the diverse religious and social communities ( Hindu, Christian, and Muslim ) who, despite living in the same space, operate on different cultural and historical "\times" or wavelengths.
  4. Nature's 'Precise' Act: Nature is described as "precise" because its actions, unlike chaotic human riots or hypocritical peace-march bombs, are governed by absolute physical laws. Nature's destruction is clean, direct, and free from human bias or ideological confusion.
  5. Poet's Attitude to Communal Disharmony: The poet's attitude is best reflected as a wistful lament . He observes the "perennial feuds" with a sense of melancholy, noting how easily peace is disrupted by a mere "change of wind" or "change of mind."
  6. Representation of the Average Indian: Yes, the poet's attitude reflects a common Indian perspective of weary resignation toward recurring communal violence, coupled with a deep, fatalistic respect for the overwhelming, unpredictable power of nature's fury.
