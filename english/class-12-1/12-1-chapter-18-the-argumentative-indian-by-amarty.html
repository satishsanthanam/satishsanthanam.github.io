English: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 18: The Argumentative Indian

Introduction & Author Profile

Literary Profile: Amartya Sen

  • Traits & Temperament: Analytical, humanistic, deeply committed to welfare economics, social justice, and pluralistic dialogue.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in 1933; awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1998; Lamont Professor at Harvard; renowned for his work on famine, human development theory, and gender inequality.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Champion of public reasoning, heterodoxy, and the historical depth of non-Western democratic traditions.

This \text forms the opening sections of the first essay in Amartya Sen's book The Argumentative Indian (2005). Sen argues that India has a long, robust tradition of questioning the truth of ideas through discussion, dialogue, and public debate, which is fundamental to understanding its modern democracy and secular identity.

Prolixity and the Epic Tradition

Sen explores the historical roots of India's talkative culture, demonstrating that loquaciousness is deeply embedded in the subcontinent's literary and historical traditions.

  • Modern Loquaciousness: Sen highlights Krishna Menon 's record-breaking nine-hour non-stop speech at the United Nations in 1957 defending India's stand on Kashmir as a modern peak of Indian prolixity.
  • Ancient Sanskrit Epics: The ancient epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata , are colossally longer than their Western counterparts, the Iliad and the Odyssey . The Mahabharata alone is about seven \times as long as both Homeric epics combined.
  • Dialogic Structure: These epics do not merely tell stories; they proceed through nested narratives filled with dialogues, moral dilemmas, alternative perspectives, and incessant debates.
Dialogue and Significance: The Gita Debate

Sen analyzes the famous debate in the Bhagavad Gita (a small section of the Mahabharata) as a prime example of substantive moral and philosophical argumentation.

The Moral Tussle: Krishna vs. Arjuna
  • Arjuna's Consequentialism: On the eve of the great war, the warrior Arjuna expresses profound doubts about the slaughter and misery the war will cause, questioning whether a just cause justifies such human devastation.
  • Krishna's Deontology: Krishna , acting as Arjuna's charioteer, argues that Arjuna must perform his duty as a warrior, irrespective of the consequences. Duty must take priority over personal moral evaluations.
Faring Forward vs. Faring Well

'And do not think of the fruit of action! Fare forward'. Eliot explains: 'Not fare well/But fare forward, voyagers'.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: Sen uses T.S. Eliot's poetry to contrast Krishna's demand to 'fare forward' (perform duty blindly) with Arjuna's insistence on 'faring well' (considering the ultimate well-being and consequences of actions). Sen argues that the tragic desolation at the end of the Mahabharata vindicates Arjuna's doubts, showing that both perspectives remain essential and that the Gita's message must be supplemented by the broader, argumentative wisdom of the entire epic.

Modern Parallel: J. Robert Oppenheimer

Sen draws a parallel between the Gita's moral dilemma and J. Robert Oppenheimer 's reflections during the development of the atomic bomb. Oppenheimer quoted Krishna's words ('I am become death, the destroyer of worlds') upon witnessing the first nuclear explosion in 1945, illustrating the modern tension between technical duty ('faring forward') and the catastrophic consequences of scientific success.

Gender, Caste, and Voice

Sen addresses the critical question of whether India's argumentative tradition was confined to a privileged, male elite, demonstrating that marginalized voices have historically played a central role in intellectual dissent.

The Voice of Women

While acknowledging historical gender inequalities, Sen highlights the powerful intellectual and political contributions of women throughout Indian history:

  • Modern Politics: Women have held key leadership roles, such as Sarojini Naidu (elected President of the Indian National Congress in 1925) and Nellie Sengupta (elected in 1933), long before their Western counterparts achieved similar political milestones.
  • Gargi's Intellectual Combat: In the ancient Brihadaranyaka Upanisad , the woman scholar Gargi challenges the eminent sage Yajnavalkya with sharp, militant questioning, establishing herself as a formidable intellectual peer.
  • Maitreyi's Philosophical Inquiry: Yajnavalkya's wife, Maitreyi , raises a profound question regarding the limitations of material wealth, asking: 'What should I do with that by which I do not become immortal?' This question serves as a foundation for understanding human capability over mere economic opulence.
  • Draupadi's Instigation: In the Mahabharata and Bharavi's Kiratarjuniya , Draupadi rejects passive forbearance, mockingly urging King Yudhisthira to fight for his rightful throne, showcasing a fierce and active female agency.
Caste, Class, and Heterodoxy

The challenge to orthodox hierarchy has frequently come from socially disadvantaged groups, proving that the argumentative tradition crosses class and caste barriers:

  • Buddhism and Jainism: These rebellious religious movements gained rapid traction by undermining the priestly caste's claims to superiority and promoting human equality through rigorous debate.
  • Epic Skepticism: In the Mahabharata, Bharadvaja rejects Bhrigu 's assertion that caste divisions are linked to physical attributes like skin colour, pointing out that all humans experience the same emotions, physical needs, and life struggles.
  • The Bhakti and Sufi Movements: By the fifteenth century, medieval mystical poets from working-class backgrounds challenged social and religious barriers. Key figures include Kabir (a weaver), Dadu (a cotton-carder), Ravidas (a shoemaker), and Sena (a barber), alongside prominent women like Mira Bai , Andal , and Daya-bai .
Democracy as Public Reasoning

Sen connects India's historical dialogic tradition directly to the success and resilience of its modern democratic framework.

  • Roots of Democracy: Sen argues that democracy is fundamentally about public discussion and interactive reasoning. India's long history of tolerating intellectual heterodoxy made the adoption and preservation of a democratic constitution much easier.
  • Dispelling Misconceptions: Sen refutes two common misconceptions: (1) that democracy is purely a Western gift accepted by India upon independence, and (2) that there is something uniquely genetic in Indian history that makes it singularly suited to democracy. Instead, he asserts that democracy thrives because it draws upon a globally shared, locally nurtured tradition of public reasoning.
Literary Devices & Key Vocabulary
Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Prolixity / Loquaciousness 'Prolixity is not alien to us in India.' The quality of being long-winded, talkative, or using an excess of words. Establishes the essay's lighthearted yet analytical premise regarding India's conversational culture.
Deontology vs. Consequentialism 'Krishna's emphasis on doing one's duty... and Arjuna's focus on avoiding bad consequences' The philosophical conflict between duty-based ethics (deontology) and outcome-based ethics (consequentialism). Frames the core moral dilemma of the Bhagavad Gita as a sophisticated, timeless intellectual debate.
Heterodoxy '...to make heterodoxy the natural state of affairs in India' Holding opinions or beliefs that go against established, orthodox, or traditional doctrines. Emphasizes that pluralism, dissent, and diversity of thought are foundational to Indian identity.
Militant Imagery 'Like the ruler of Videha... who strings his unstrung bow... so do I approach you with two questions' Using combat-related metaphors to describe an intellectual debate. Highlights Gargi's fierce, uncompromising, and confident approach to challenging male intellectual authority.
