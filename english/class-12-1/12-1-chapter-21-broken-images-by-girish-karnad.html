English: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 21: Broken Images

Author & Play Overview

An introduction to the playwright Girish Karnad and the structural framework of his psychological drama Broken Images .

Literary Profile: Girish Karnad (1938–2019)

  • Traits & Temperament: Contemporary Indian playwright, actor, director, and public intellectual. Known for writing in both Kannada and English , often translating his own works.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Recipient of the Padma Shri (1974), Padma Bhushan (1992), and the Jnanpith Award (1998). A major figure in modern Indian theater who used history, myth, and contemporary technology to explore deep existential and social crises.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Explores the politics of language, the split identity of the post-colonial writer, the ethics of representation, and the psychological fragmentation of the self.
Stage Setting and Dramatic Atmosphere
  • The Television Studio: The play is set in a highly modern, minimalist television studio. A large plasma screen dominates one side of the stage, designed to project high-definition close-ups of the protagonist.
  • The Spatial Split: On one side sits a chair and a semi-circular, sturdy "telly" table. At the back of the stage, multiple television screens of varying sizes remain blank, creating an eerie, surveillance-like atmosphere.
  • The Red Light: A small red bulb glows above the table, high enough to remain out of the television camera's frame, symbolizing the boundary between the public broadcast and private reality.
Character Profiles

Detailed psychological and thematic breakdowns of the characters in the play.

Literary Profile: Manjula Nayak (Mrs. Manjula Murty)

  • Traits & Temperament: Confident, articulate, defensive, and deeply conflicted. She presents herself as a successful, modern literary phenomenon but harbors deep-seated insecurities, guilt, and sibling jealousy.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A former lecturer in English in Bangalore who wrote moderately successful short stories in Kannada. She suddenly achieves global fame and a massive financial windfall by writing an English bestseller, The River Has No Memories .
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the post-colonial Indian writer caught between regional authenticity and global commercial success. She symbolizes the betrayal of the mother tongue and the exploitation of another's suffering for personal glory.

Literary Profile: The Image

  • Traits & Temperament: Calm, analytical, relentless, and omniscient. It acts as an interrogator, refusing to let Manjula escape her own lies and contradictions.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: An electronic projection of Manjula on the plasma screen that takes on a life of its own after the studio cameras are turned off.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents Manjula's alter-ego, her repressed conscience, and the objective truth. It exposes the artificiality of her public persona and the dark secrets behind her literary success.

Literary Profile: Malini

  • Traits & Temperament: Physically challenged, highly intelligent, vivacious, and fluent in English. Despite her confinement, she "radiated life" and was the intellectual center of her family.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Manjula's younger sister who suffered from meningomyelocele , leaving her paralyzed below the waist. She lived in the family's spacious Koramangala house and later moved in with Manjula in Jayanagar before her death.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: The silent, deceased muse whose actual life, intellect, and potentially her written words were co-opted by Manjula to write the English bestseller.

Literary Profile: Pramod Murty

  • Traits & Temperament: Practical, supportive, yet emotionally distant from Manjula. He is described as a "software wizard" who works from home.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Manjula's husband. He shared a deep, intimate bond with the disabled Malini, spending hours with her while working from home, which created an unspoken domestic tension.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the bridge between Malini and Manjula, and the ultimate emotional abandonment of Manjula, as he moves to Los Angeles immediately after Malini's death.
Plot Synopsis & Scene-by-Scene Breakdown

A chronological and thematic breakdown of the play's dramatic action.

Phase 1: The Public Monologue (The Broadcast)
  1. Manjula Nayak enters the high-tech television studio of Shree-TV to deliver a pre-recorded introduction to a telefilm based on her bestselling English novel, The River Has No Memories .
  2. The TV Announcer introduces her as a "Literary Phenomenon of the Decade," highlighting her transition from a Kannada short-story writer and English lecturer to an internationally acclaimed English novelist who received a massive financial advance.
  3. Manjula begins her speech with a traditional Kannada greeting, "Namaskara," but immediately addresses the controversy surrounding her choice of language. She defends her decision to write in English, claiming the novel "burst out" of her naturally.
  4. She attacks her critics—the regional intellectuals and the President of the Sahitya Akademi—who accuse her of writing in English for money and betraying her linguistic roots. She argues that writing in English gave her the financial freedom to write full-time.
  5. She transitions to the inspiration behind her novel: her deceased, physically challenged sister, Malini . She paints a touching picture of her sisterly devotion, claiming she nursed Malini in her final months and dedicated the book to her memory.
  6. She concludes by thanking her husband, Pramod Murty , for his unwavering support during her writing process, and bids the audience goodnight.
Phase 2: The Split Reality (The Image Speaks)
  1. The studio recording ends, and the red light turns off. Manjula relaxes and speaks to the technician, Raza, expressing relief that she has successfully delivered her speech and "taken enough shit" from her critics.
  2. As she prepares to leave, her image on the plasma screen does not disappear. Instead, the Image remains seated, watching her calmly, and suddenly speaks, asking, "Where are you going?"
  3. Manjula is startled and confused, assuming there is a technical glitch or a practical joke. She tries to contact Raza through her lapel mic, but receives no response.
  4. The Image reveals that it is not a recording but a live, independent entity—Manjula's own self. It begins to interrogate her, peeling back the layers of her carefully constructed public narrative.
Phase 3: The Interrogation & Deconstruction
  1. The Image questions the emotional authenticity of Manjula's relationship with Malini. It points out that Malini was always the favorite child, receiving all the parental love, wealth, and intellectual attention, while Manjula was sent away to live with grandparents in Dharwad.
  2. The Image challenges Manjula's decision to sell the family's spacious Koramangala house and bring Malini to her cramped house in Jayanagar . Manjula defensively claims she did it for convenience, but the Image hints at a desire to control and minimize Malini's space.
  3. The linguistic divide is exposed: Malini "breathed, laughed, dreamt in English," while Manjula's domain was Kannada. The Image suggests that Manjula's sudden mastery of English was not a natural burst of creativity, but an act of linguistic appropriation of Malini's voice.
  4. The interrogation turns to Pramod. The Image reveals that Pramod and Malini shared an incredibly close, intimate bond at home, while Manjula was busy with her college lectures.
  5. The final blow comes when the Image questions the timeline of Pramod's departure to the United States. He left for Los Angeles immediately after Malini's death, refusing to attend Manjula's high-profile book launch in New York, exposing the complete collapse of Manjula's marriage and the hollow nature of her global success.
Thematic Analysis

Deep dive into the core literary and socio-political themes of Karnad's play.

1. The Politics of Language and Bilingualism

The play serves as a sharp critique of the post-colonial Indian literary landscape, where a deep hierarchy exists between regional languages ( bhasha literature ) and English.

  • The Accusation of Betrayal: Regional writers who switch to English are often branded as "traitors" or accused of "complicity in the global consumer market economy." Karnad satirizes this purist stance through the character of the Sahitya Akademi President, who criticizes English writers while speaking in English himself.
  • The Illusion of Authenticity: Manjula defends her English writing by claiming her British publishers found it "so Indian" and possessing a "genuine Indian feel." This highlights how Western publishers often exoticize and validate Indian writing, creating a manufactured standard of authenticity.
  • Language as Territory: For Manjula, Kannada was the one area that was uniquely hers, a refuge from her sister's intellectual dominance. By writing her novel in English, she attempts to conquer Malini's linguistic territory, but ultimately loses her own authentic voice.
2. The Split Self and Technological Mediation

Karnad uses the television studio and the digital image as a modern theatrical device to represent the psychological split within the contemporary individual.

  • The Screen as a Mirror of Conscience: The plasma screen does not merely reflect Manjula's physical form; it acts as an externalized conscience. In the digital age, the "image" we project to the world becomes detached from our true self, eventually confronting and interrogating us.
  • Monologue to Dialogue: The play transitions from a highly controlled, polished public monologue to an unpredictable, raw, and painful dialogue. This shift represents the collapse of public relations and the unavoidable confrontation with private truth.
3. Sibling Rivalry, Guilt, and Exploitation

At its core, the play is a domestic tragedy about jealousy, emotional neglect, and the exploitation of the vulnerable.

  • The Second-Best Syndrome: Manjula grew up in the shadow of her brilliant, disabled sister. Her parents' emotional and financial resources were entirely dedicated to Malini, leaving Manjula feeling abandoned and relegated to "second best."
  • Literary Parasitism: The play strongly \implies that The River Has No Memories was not written by Manjula at all, or was heavily plagiarized from Malini's own writings and diaries. Manjula exploited her sister's physical confinement and intellectual output to achieve the global fame she always craved.
  • The Hollow Triumph: Manjula's material success (the massive advance, the global bestseller status) is contrasted with her absolute emotional bankruptcy. Her sister is dead, her husband has abandoned her for America, and she is left alone in a sterile studio, confronted by the ghost of her own deceit.
Literary Devices & Dialogue Analysis

An analysis of the stylistic elements, key vocabulary, and critical dialogues in the play.

Linguistic and Stylistic Devices Matrix
Literary Device / Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Stylistic Purpose
Irony / Satire "He of course spoke in English. Speaking in English, as you know, gives you the authority to make oracular pronouncements on Indian literatures..." Manjula mocks the hypocrisy of regional literary gatekeepers who condemn English while using it to maintain their own power. Exposes the double standards and linguistic anxieties of the Indian intellectual elite.
Double Entendre "Meaningful: Arthapoorna. The Kannada word for Meaning is Artha—which also means money!" Manjula plays on the word 'Artha' to justify her financial success, equating intellectual meaning with material wealth. Highlights her commercial motivations and her defense mechanism against accusations of selling out.
Symbolism "A heap of broken images, where the sun beats..." (T.S. Eliot, The Waste Land) The play's title and epigraph refer to spiritual dryness, fragmentation of identity, and the loss of coherent truth in the modern world. Sets the tragic, fragmented tone of the play, foreshadowing Manjula's psychological breakdown.
Metaphor "The River Has No Memories" The title of Manjula's novel suggests a desire to wash away the past, to erase the memory of her sister and her own guilt. Underlines the theme of repression and the futile attempt to escape one's history.
Critical Textual Excerpts & Interpretations

"IMAGE: You are standing up. If the camera were on, I would be standing up too. I’m not.
MANJULA: Is this some kind of a trick?"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This moment marks the precise transition of the play from a realistic television broadcast to a surreal, psychological drama. The physical divergence between Manjula and her projected Image establishes the Image as an independent, objective entity. It shatters Manjula's control over her environment and initiates her descent into self-confrontation.

"MANJULA: Actually, I have never said it publicly, but if you argue that a novel written in English cannot express truth about India because we do not express ourselves in English—... I could not have written about my sister in Kannada. She breathed, laughed, dreamt in English."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This excerpt highlights the complex intersection of language, class, and personal identity. Manjula uses her sister's English-medium life to justify her own choice of language, arguing that English is an authentic medium for a certain class of urban Indians. However, it also hints at her appropriation of Malini's identity, as she admits that having Malini in her house "helped improve my English."

Study Questions & Discussion Points

Comprehension and analytical questions based on the NCERT curriculum for "Broken Images".

Thinking about the Play
  1. How genuine is the love that Manjula expresses for her sister Malini during her public monologue? Contrast this with the revelations made during her interrogation by the Image.
  2. Although Malini never physically appears on stage, she is the central force of the play. What physical, intellectual, and emotional portrait of Malini is constructed through the dialogue?
  3. Analyze the significance of the Image's statement: "Her illness was unfortunate. But because of it, she got the best of everything." How does Manjula's defensive response reveal her childhood trauma and sibling jealousy?
  4. What specific aspects of celebrity culture, television media, and the global publishing industry does Girish Karnad satirize in the play?
Talking about the Play
  1. Discuss how "Broken Images" addresses the post-1947 debate surrounding the politics of language in Indian literary culture, specifically the tension between regional languages and English.
  2. Can a writer be a truly bilingual practitioner, or does writing in an "other tongue" inevitably amount to a betrayal of one's mother tongue and cultural reality? Debate this in the context of Manjula's literary journey.
  3. Explore the play as a psychological study of guilt. Is Manjula's ultimate breakdown a result of public exposure or her own internal inability to live with the lie of her success?
Appreciation & Dramatic Technique
  • The Dialogic Monologue: Why does the playwright choose to turn a monologue into a dialogue? How does this technique enhance the dramatic tension of a single-actor play?
  • The Role of Technology: Analyze how Karnad uses the television screen, the lapel microphone, and the studio lights as active dramatic devices rather than mere stage props.
  • The Contrast of Postures: Compare Manjula's physical and verbal posture when the camera is "on" (during the broadcast) versus when it is "off" (during her interaction with Raza and the Image).
