English: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 2: Lost Spring: Stories of Stolen Childhood

Author Profile & Thematic Introduction

Literary Profile: Anees Jung

  • Traits & Temperament: Empathetic observer, social commentator, and advocate for marginalized children; writes with a poignant, reflective, and deeply humanistic tone.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in Rourkela (1944), raised in Hyderabad and educated in the United States; comes from a family of writers; former editor and columnist for major national and international publications.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Focuses on the systemic cycle of grinding poverty, the loss of childhood innocence, the burden of traditional caste expectations, and the institutional apathy that perpetuates exploitation.

The chapter Lost Spring is an excerpt from Anees Jung's book of the same name. It presents two distinct narratives that expose the harsh realities of child labor in India, depicting how socio-economic forces steal the "spring" (the joy, freedom, and growth) of childhood from millions of young lives.

Part I: "Sometimes I find a Rupee in the garbage" (Saheb's Story)

Literary Profile: Saheb-e-Alam

  • Traits & Temperament: Optimistic despite extreme deprivation, resilient, carefree, and easily excited by small discoveries; later becomes subdued and burdened by labor.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A young ragpicker living in the slums of Seemapuri; his family migrated from Dhaka, Bangladesh, in 1971 after storms destroyed their fields and homes.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: His full name, Saheb-e-Alam , ironically translates to "Lord of the Universe," contrasting sharply with his impoverished reality; symbolizes the loss of freedom and the crushing weight of survival.
Setting: Seemapuri (The Periphery of Delhi)
  • Geographical Contrast: Located on the periphery of Delhi, yet metaphorically miles away from the capital's progress, wealth, and development.
  • Living Conditions: A wilderness inhabited by over 10,000 ragpickers living in structures of mud with roofs of tin and tarpaulin; completely devoid of sewage, drainage, or running water.
  • Survival vs. Identity: Residents live without legal permits or formal identity, but possess ration cards that enable them to buy grain and vote. Food is prioritized over identity for daily survival.
Thematic Analysis: The Dual Meaning of Garbage

"For the children it is wrapped in wonder, for the elders it is a means of survival."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This quote highlights the psychological divide between generations. For adults, garbage is a harsh, daily economic necessity—their "daily bread" and "a roof over their heads." For children like Saheb, garbage is an adventure, a treasure hunt where finding a coin or a ten-rupee note sparks hope, wonder, and dreams of discovery.

The Transition: Loss of Freedom at the Tea Stall
  1. The New Job: Saheb is employed at a local tea stall down the road, earning 800 rupees per month and receiving all his meals.
  2. The Loss of Autonomy: The author observes that Saheb's face has lost its carefree look. The steel canister he carries feels heavier than the plastic bag he used to carry so lightly over his shoulder.
  3. Symbolic Shift: The plastic bag belonged to Saheb, but the steel canister belongs to the tea shop owner. Saheb is no longer his own master.
Part II: "I want to drive a car" (Mukesh's Story)

Literary Profile: Mukesh

  • Traits & Temperament: Determined, ambitious, and defiant of traditional expectations; possesses a rare spark of "daring" that sets him apart from his family.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born into a family of impoverished glass bangle makers in Firozabad; lives in a crumbling, half-built shack in a stinking, garbage-choked lane.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: His dream of becoming a motor mechanic represents a desire to break free from the hereditary cycle of poverty and caste-bound labor.
Setting: Firozabad (The Glass-Blowing Hub)
  • Industrial Center: The center of India's glass-blowing industry, where generations of families have spent their lives working around high-temperature furnaces.
  • Illegal Child Labor: Over 20,000 children work illegally in hot, dingy glass furnaces without proper air, light, or ventilation.
  • Physical Hazards: Prolonged exposure to extreme heat, toxic fumes, and the dust from polishing glass bangles leads to chronic respiratory issues and causes many workers to lose their eyesight before reaching adulthood.
The Vicious Circle: Social & Political Forces of Exploitation

The author identifies two distinct, oppressive worlds that trap the bangle makers of Firozabad in a spiral of poverty:

  1. The Family and Caste Web: Caught in a web of poverty and burdened by the stigma of the caste into which they are born, leading to a fatalistic acceptance of their "karam" (destiny).
  2. The Vicious Circle of Oppressors: A powerful network consisting of sahukars (moneylenders), middlemen, policemen, keepers of the law, bureaucrats, and politicians who collectively impose an inescapable burden on the children.
  3. The Absence of Leadership: The workers are unable to organize themselves into cooperatives due to fear of police brutality, systemic harassment, and a lack of leadership to guide them.
Thematic Contrast: Resignation vs. Daring

"Can a god-given lineage ever be broken?"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: Spoken by Mukesh's grandmother, this rhetorical question reflects the deep-seated fatalism of the older generation. They view their suffering and their trade as an inescapable destiny. In contrast, Mukesh's insistence on learning to drive a car and becoming a motor mechanic represents a courageous act of defiance against this generational resignation.

Literary Devices & Language Analysis

The author uses poetic prose and vivid figures of speech to transform factual accounts of misery into a compelling literary experience.

Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Hyperbole "Garbage to them is gold." Garbage is the source of their daily bread, survival, and occasional unexpected wealth. Emphasizes the extreme poverty where waste material is valued as precious metal.
Simile "...an army of barefoot boys who appear like the morning birds and disappear at noon." The boys roam the streets in groups, appearing early and vanishing quickly. Highlights their transient, free, yet fragile and vulnerable nature.
Metaphor "Drowned in an air of desolation." The temple and town are completely enveloped in neglect, ruin, and sadness. Evokes a strong visual and emotional sense of decay and abandonment.
Simile "As her hands move mechanically like the tongs of a machine..." Savita solders glass with repetitive, unthinking speed. Illustrates how child labor dehumanizes children, turning them into mere tools.
Metaphor "Web of poverty" / "Vicious circle" The inescapable trap of economic deprivation and systemic exploitation. Conveys the complex, interlocking forces that prevent social mobility.
Irony "Saheb-e-Alam... lord of the universe" A boy named "Lord of the Universe" spends his days scrounging through garbage. Exposes the tragic gap between human potential/dignity and social reality.
Textbook Questions & Analytical Solutions
Think as you read (Page 16)
  1. What is Saheb looking for in the garbage dumps? Where is he and where has he come from?
    Saheb is looking for "gold"—which represents items of value, coins, or usable scrap—in the garbage dumps of the author's neighborhood. He is currently living in Seemapuri, on the outskirts of Delhi, having migrated from Dhaka, Bangladesh, in 1971 after storms destroyed his family's home and fields.
  2. What explanations does the author offer for the children not wearing footwear?
    The author notes that staying barefoot is explained by some as a cultural tradition rather than a simple lack of money. However, she suspects this explanation is merely an excuse used to rationalize and hide a perpetual state of poverty.
  3. Is Saheb happy working at the tea-stall? Explain.
    No, Saheb is not happy. Although he earns 800 rupees and gets all his meals, he has lost his independence. His face has lost its carefree look, and the steel canister he must carry feels heavier than the plastic bag he once owned and carried on his own terms.
Think as you read (Page 19)
  1. What makes the city of Firozabad famous?
    Firozabad is famous for its glass bangles. It is the center of India's glass-blowing industry, where generations of families work around high-temperature furnaces to make bangles for women across the country.
  2. Mention the hazards of working in the glass bangles industry.
    Workers, including children, toil in glass furnaces with extremely high temperatures, in dark, dingy cells without proper air or light. The constant exposure to heat, toxic fumes, and the fine dust from polishing glass bangles leads to respiratory illnesses and often causes workers to lose their eyesight before they reach adulthood.
  3. How is Mukesh’s attitude to his situation different from that of his family?
    While Mukesh's family fatalistically accepts their poverty and traditional trade as their "karam" (destiny), Mukesh dares to dream of a different future. He wants to break the cycle by learning to drive and becoming a motor mechanic.
