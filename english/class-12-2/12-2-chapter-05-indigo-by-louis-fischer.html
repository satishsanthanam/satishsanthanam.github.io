English: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 5: Indigo

Author Profile & Historical Context

Literary Profile: Louis Fischer

  • Traits & Temperament: Observant, analytical, and deeply empathetic to the cause of human rights and national sovereignty.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: Born in Philadelphia (1896-1970); served as a volunteer in the British Army; worked as a prominent journalist for publications like The New York Times and The Saturday Review .
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Renowned for capturing the humanistic essence of political movements, particularly through his acclaimed biography The Life of Mahatma Gandhi .

The chapter is an excerpt from Fischer's biographical work, detailing his first-hand interactions with Mahatma Gandhi in 1942 at his ashram in Sevagram , and reconstructing the pivotal events of the 1917 Champaran campaign.

The Catalyst & The Journey to Champaran
The Resolute Sharecropper

Literary Profile: Rajkumar Shukla

  • Traits & Temperament: Illiterate but highly resolute, tenacious, and single-minded in his pursuit of justice.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A poor, emaciated sharecropper from the foothills of the Himalayas in Champaran , Bihar.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the collective suffering, quiet determination, and latent power of the Indian peasantry.

Shukla traveled to the December 1916 annual convention of the Indian National Congress in Lucknow to complain about the landlord system in Bihar. Despite Gandhi's busy schedule and pre-arranged commitments across India, Shukla followed him persistently for weeks until Gandhi agreed to meet him in Calcutta and travel to Bihar.

The Journey and Early Obstacles
  • Patna: Shukla led Gandhi to the house of lawyer Rajendra Prasad . The servants, recognizing Shukla as a poor peasant, forced them to stay on the grounds and barred Gandhi from drawing water from the well to prevent perceived ritual pollution.
  • Muzzafarpur: Gandhi traveled here first to gather comprehensive facts. He stayed with Professor Malkani , a government school teacher, which Gandhi noted was an extraordinary act of courage given that government employees feared harboring advocates of home-rule.
  • Chiding the Lawyers: Gandhi criticized local lawyers for charging high fees to the impoverished sharecroppers, arguing that legal action was useless while the peasants remained paralyzed by fear.
The Indigo Sharecropping System & Conflict

Under a long-term contract, Champaran peasants were forced to plant fifteen percent of their land with indigo and surrender the entire harvest as rent to British landlords. When Germany developed synthetic indigo, natural indigo became unprofitable. The landlords deceitfully obtained agreements from the peasants to pay compensation in exchange for being released from the fifteen percent arrangement. While some signed willingly, others resisted, hiring lawyers while landlords employed thugs. When the peasants discovered the truth about synthetic indigo, they demanded their money back.

The Triumph of Civil Disobedience
Official Opposition and Peasant Mobilization

Upon arriving in Champaran, Gandhi was met with systemic resistance from British authorities:

  • The secretary of the British landlord's association refused to give information to Gandhi, calling him an "outsider."
  • The British official commissioner of the Tirhut division bullied Gandhi and ordered him to leave.
  • Gandhi disobeyed the order and proceeded to Motihari , where he was served an official notice to quit Champaran immediately. He signed the receipt and wrote that he would disobey the order, resulting in a court summons.
The Courtroom Confrontation

"He was involved, he told the court, in a 'conflict of duties'— on the one hand, not to set a bad example as a lawbreaker; on the other hand, to render the 'humanitarian and national service' for which he had come."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This statement defines the moral core of Gandhi's philosophy of Satyagraha. He did not break the law out of a lack of respect for authority, but in obedience to a higher moral law—the voice of conscience. The spontaneous gathering of thousands of peasants around the courthouse in Motihari marked the beginning of their liberation from the fear of the British.

Baffled by the massive, peaceful crowd, the prosecutor requested a postponement of the trial. Gandhi protested the delay, pleaded guilty, and was ultimately released without bail. The case was eventually dropped by the Lieutenant-Governor, marking the first victory of civil disobedience in modern India.

The Settlement & Socio-Economic Reforms
The 25% Refund Agreement

An official commission of inquiry was appointed, consisting of landlords, government officials, and Gandhi. Faced with overwhelming evidence of extortion, the landlords agreed to make refunds. Gandhi broke the deadlock by accepting a twenty-five percent refund instead of the fifty percent he originally demanded.

"Gandhi explained that the amount of the refund was less important than the fact that the landlords had been obliged to surrender part of the money and, with it, part of their prestige."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This pragmatic compromise shifted the power dynamic. By forcing the landlords to yield, Gandhi dismantled their aura of absolute authority, showing the peasants that they had rights, defenders, and the capacity for courage. Within a few years, the British planters abandoned their estates, and indigo sharecropping disappeared entirely.

Social and Cultural Upliftment

Gandhi recognized that political and economic victories were insufficient without addressing social and cultural backwardness. He initiated community reforms in Champaran:

  • Education: Primary schools were opened in six villages; teachers, including Gandhi's disciples and his youngest son Devadas , volunteered.
  • Health & Sanitation: Kasturbai taught personal cleanliness and community sanitation. A volunteer doctor administered castor oil, quinine, and sulphur ointment to treat prevalent ailments.
  • Dignity: Kasturbai addressed the filthy state of women's clothes, highlighting the extreme poverty where many women owned only a single sari.
The Philosophy of Self-Reliance

During the campaign, Gandhi's lawyer friends wanted the English pacifist Charles Freer Andrews to stay in Champaran and help them. Gandhi strongly opposed this, arguing that relying on an Englishman to win their battle revealed a weakness of heart. He insisted that the cause was just, and they must rely on themselves.

"Self-reliance, Indian independence and help to sharecroppers were all bound together."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This conclusion synthesizes the political, economic, and psychological dimensions of the Indian freedom struggle. True independence could not be achieved without liberating the individual mind from dependency and fear.
