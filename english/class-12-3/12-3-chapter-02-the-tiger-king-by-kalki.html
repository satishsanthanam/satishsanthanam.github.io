English: Class 12 (Book 3) — Chapter 2: The Tiger King

Introduction & The Miraculous Prophecy (Part I)

The story opens with an introduction to the protagonist, the Maharaja of Pratibandapuram , also known as the Tiger King . The narrator uses highly exaggerated, satirical titles to introduce him, highlighting the vanity of princely rulers during the British Raj.

Literary Profile: The Tiger King (Maharaja Sir Jilani Jung Jung Bahadur)

  • Traits & Temperament: Stubborn, eccentric, highly superstitious, autocratic, and driven by an obsessive desire to conquer fate. He exhibits absolute disregard for ecological balance and the welfare of his subjects.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: The ruler of a wealthy princely state under British suzerainty, pampered with an excessively Anglicised upbringing, wielding absolute power over his subjects.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Represents the hubris of ruling classes, the futility of human attempts to control destiny, and the callous exploitation of nature for personal vanity.
The Astrological Prediction & The Ten-Day-Old Infant's Miracle

At the birth of the crown prince, royal astrologers predict that the child will grow up to be a great warrior, hero, and champion, but is destined to die. A miracle occurs when the ten-day-old infant, Jilani Jung Jung Bahadur , speaks clearly, challenging the astrologers' prediction.

“All those who are born will one day have to die. We don’t need your predictions to know that. There would be some sense in it if you could tell us the manner of that death,” the royal infant uttered these words in his little squeaky voice.

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This miraculous event introduces the element of magic realism and sharp satire. The infant's logical reasoning exposes the redundancy of standard astrological predictions, forcing the chief astrologer to specify that because the prince was born in the hour of the Bull , and the Bull and the Tiger are enemies, his death will come from a tiger.

The Vow and the Royal Upbringing (Part II)

The narrative fast-forwards to the prince's youth, illustrating the highly Anglicised lifestyle of Indian royalty under British influence, followed by his ascension to the throne and the initiation of his obsessive campaign.

A Highly Anglicised Upbringing
  • The prince drank the milk of an English cow .
  • He was brought up by an English nanny .
  • He was tutored in English by an Englishman .
  • He watched nothing but English films , mirroring the standard upbringing of crown princes of other Indian states.
  • At the age of twenty, the state, previously managed by the Court of Wards , was handed over to him.
The First Kill and the Astrologer's Challenge

Upon hearing rumors of the prophecy, the Maharaja decides to hunt tigers to safeguard himself, citing the old saying, "You may kill even a cow in self-defence." He kills his first tiger and triumphantly summons the state astrologer.

“Your majesty may kill ninety-nine tigers in exactly the same manner. But... you must be very careful with the hundredth tiger.”

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: The astrologer's warning sets up the central conflict of the narrative. The astrologer vows that if the King kills the hundredth tiger, he will tear up his astrology books, burn them, cut off his hair tuft, and become an insurance agent . This high-stakes challenge seals the Maharaja's obsession.

The Campaign and Geopolitical Hurdles (Part III)

The Maharaja prioritises his tiger-hunting campaign above all state duties, leading to absolute authoritarian measures and a major diplomatic crisis with the British authorities.

The State Ban on Tiger Hunting
  • The state bans tiger hunting by anyone except the Maharaja.
  • A proclamation is issued: if anyone dares to fling even a stone at a tiger, all their wealth and property will be confiscated.
  • The Maharaja vows to attend to all other state matters only after completing his tally of one hundred tigers .
The British Officer Crisis & The Diamond Ring Appeasement

A high-ranking British officer (durai) visits Pratibandapuram, wishing to hunt tigers. The Maharaja firmly refuses, even rejecting the compromise of letting the officer pose for a photograph with a carcass killed by the King.

To prevent losing his kingdom due to the displeased British officer, the Maharaja and his dewan devise a plan:

  1. They order fifty expensive diamond rings of different designs from a British jeweler in Calcutta.
  2. They send the entire lot to the British officer's wife ( duraisani ), expecting her to choose one or two and return the rest.
  3. The greedy duraisani keeps all fifty rings and sends a thank-you note.
  4. The Maharaja receives a bill of three lakh rupees but is relieved to have saved his throne.
Strategic Marriage & The Extinction Crisis (Part IV)

As the Maharaja's hunt progresses, ecological limits force him to adopt strategic personal and political measures to continue his campaign.

The Depletion of Tigers in Pratibandapuram

Within ten years, the Maharaja successfully kills seventy tigers . However, the tiger population becomes extinct in the forests of Pratibandapuram, leading the narrator to humorously speculate whether the tigers practiced birth control or committed harakiri .

Matrimonial Alliance for Tiger Hunting

The Maharaja summons his dewan and declares his intention to marry. He instructs the dewan to find a princess from a royal family of a state with a large tiger population.

  • The dewan successfully finds a suitable bride.
  • Each time the Maharaja visits his father-in-law, he kills five or six tigers.
  • In this manner, ninety-nine tiger skins come to adorn the walls of the reception hall in the Pratibandapuram palace.
The Hunt for the Hundredth Tiger & The Climax (Part V)

The final phase of the Maharaja's quest is marked by extreme desperation, administrative tyranny, deception, and ultimate situational irony.

The Desperation and the Dewan's Secret Plot

With only one tiger left to reach his target of one hundred, the Maharaja's anxiety reaches a fever pitch. Tigers cannot be found anywhere, even in his father-in-law's kingdom. In his rage, the Maharaja doubles the land tax and threatens to dismiss the dewan.

To save his job, the dewan secretly arranges for an old, docile tiger from the People's Park in Madras . At midnight, the dewan and his wife drag the tiger to their car, drive to the forest, and push it out near the Maharaja's camp.

The Illusion of the Hundredth Kill

The next day, the old tiger wanders into the Maharaja's presence. The Maharaja takes careful aim, shoots, and the tiger falls in a heap. Overjoyed, the King orders the tiger to be brought to the capital in a grand procession and leaves.

However, after his departure, the hunters discover that the bullet had missed; the tiger had merely fainted from the shock of the bullet whizzing past. Fearing the King's wrath and the loss of their jobs, the hunters shoot the tiger themselves at point-blank range.

The Wooden Toy Tiger and the Irony of Fate

Believing his vow is fulfilled, the Maharaja turns his attention to his son's third birthday. He buys a cheap, poorly crafted wooden toy tiger from a local shop for three hundred rupees (marked up from two annas and a quarter).

  • While playing with his son, a tiny, rough sliver of wood from the toy tiger pierces the Maharaja's right hand.
  • He pulls it out, but the next day, a severe infection flares up.
  • Within four days, it develops into a suppurating sore spreading all over his arm.
  • Three famous surgeons from Madras are brought in to operate.

The three surgeons who performed it came out of the theatre and announced, “The operation was successful. The Maharaja is dead.”

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This classic example of dramatic irony concludes the story. The surgeons declare the operation "successful" because they successfully contained the infection/fulfilled their medical procedure, yet the patient died. Thus, the hundredth tiger —a harmless wooden toy—takes its final, inescapable revenge on the Tiger King, proving the absolute futility of his tyrannical campaign against fate.

Literary Analysis & Thematic Exploration

Kalki's "The Tiger King" is a masterful political satire and allegory that critiques feudal power, colonial sycophancy, and human hubris.

Key Themes
  • The Futility of Defying Fate: Despite killing ninety-nine living tigers and taking extreme precautions, the Maharaja is killed by a tiny, inanimate wooden sliver from a toy tiger. Destiny remains supreme.
  • Satire on Power and Bureaucracy: The story exposes the whims, vanity, and absolute irresponsibility of rulers. The Maharaja is willing to double taxes, fire loyal officers, and spend state wealth on bribes just to satisfy his personal ego.
  • Ecological Callousness: The rapid extinction of tigers in two kingdoms highlights the devastating impact of human greed and vanity on wildlife and ecological balance.
  • Colonial Sycophancy: The interaction with the British officer and the bribe of diamond rings mock the fragile, superficial relationships between Indian princely states and British colonisers.
Literary Device & Expression Matrix
Literary Device / Colloquial Phrase Exact Textual Example / Quote Contextual Meaning & Subtext Pedagogical / Stylistic Purpose
Dramatic Irony "The operation was successful. The Maharaja is dead." The literal success of the surgical procedure is contrasted with the death of the patient. Highlights the ultimate triumph of fate over human arrogance and the futility of the King's efforts.
Satire & Absurdity "We are ready for a mosquito hunt. But tiger hunt! That’s impossible!" The Maharaja offers ridiculous alternatives to avoid letting a British officer hunt a tiger. Mocks the triviality and cowardice of feudal rulers who compromise on everything except their personal obsessions.
Hyperbole "It seemed easier to find tiger’s milk than a live tiger." An extreme exaggeration used to describe the absolute depletion of the tiger population. Emphasizes the ecological devastation caused by the King's relentless hunting campaign.
Understatement "The child born under this star will one day have to meet its death." The astrologers state an obvious universal truth as if it were a profound, tragic secret. Creates comic relief and sets up the infant's logical, miraculous intervention.
