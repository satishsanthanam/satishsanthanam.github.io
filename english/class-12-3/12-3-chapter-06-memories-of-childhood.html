English: Class 12 (Book 3) — Chapter 6: Memories of Childhood

Introduction & Biographical Profiles

This chapter presents two autobiographical accounts from women belonging to marginalized communities. Both narratives explore childhood experiences of discrimination, cultural oppression, and the subsequent awakening of resistance and self-assertion.

Literary Profile: Zitkala-Sa (Gertrude Simmons Bonnin)

  • Traits & Temperament: Highly sensitive, fiercely independent, rebellious, and deeply attached to her native cultural traditions.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A Native American woman born in 1876, sent to the Carlisle Indian School , where systematic efforts were made to strip indigenous children of their cultural identity.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: Long hair as a symbol of bravery and cultural pride; moccasins and blankets representing freedom and native identity; the cold scissors representing forced assimilation and cultural violence.

Literary Profile: Bama (Faustina Mary Fatima Rani)

  • Traits & Temperament: Observant, joyful, innocent in early childhood, but deeply reflective, passionate, and determined when confronted with social injustice.
  • Socio-Economic & Contextual Background: A Tamil Dalit woman from a Roman Catholic family in southern India. Her autobiography Karukku (1992) highlights the pervasive nature of caste discrimination.
  • Thematic & Symbolic Motifs: The vadai packet carried by its string representing the absurdity and cruelty of untouchability ; education as a tool of liberation and social mobility.
Part I: The Cutting of My Long Hair (Zitkala-Sa)
Sensory Overload and Cultural Shock

Zitkala-Sa's first day at the missionary school is characterized by a harsh, alien environment that assaults her senses and strips her of her dignity.

  • The land of apples is bitter-cold, covered in snow, symbolizing a hostile and sterile environment.
  • The loud, metallic breakfast bell and the clatter of hard shoes on bare floors create a chaotic bedlam that replaces the peaceful sounds of her native home.
  • Her warm, native blanket is stripped from her shoulders, leaving her feeling exposed and immodestly dressed in the tight-fitting clothes of the school.
  • She wears soft moccasins , which allow her to walk noiselessly, contrasting with the loud, rigid shoes of the assimilated girls.
The Dining Room Ordeal (Eating by Formula)

The dining hall experience highlights the rigid, institutionalized control used to civilize the native children.

  1. First Bell: The pupils pull out their chairs. Zitkala-Sa sits down immediately, only to realize she is the only one seated while everyone else remains standing.
  2. Second Bell: As she attempts to rise in embarrassment, the second bell rings, and everyone else sits down, forcing her to crawl back into her chair.
  3. Third Bell: A man mutters a prayer, and the third bell signals the start of eating with knives and forks. Overwhelmed and terrified of making another mistake, Zitkala-Sa begins to cry.
The Struggle Against Shingling

The central conflict arises when Zitkala-Sa learns from her friend Judewin that the authorities plan to cut their long hair.

"Our mothers had taught us that only unskilled warriors who were captured had their hair shingled by the enemy. Among our people, short hair was worn by mourners, and shingled hair by cowards!"

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: For Zitkala-Sa, hair is not merely an aesthetic feature but a sacred symbol of cultural identity, bravery, and heritage. Cutting it represents absolute defeat, humiliation, and the systematic erasure of her indigenous identity by the colonizing force.

  • While Judewin advises submission because the authorities are strong, Zitkala-Sa rebels, declaring: "No, I will not submit! I will struggle first!"
  • She hides in a dim, empty room under a bed in the farthest corner, holding her breath as a search party hunts for her.
  • Upon discovery, she is dragged out resisting, kicking, and scratching, and is tied securely to a chair.
  • As her thick braids are cut, she experiences a profound loss of spirit, feeling like "only one of many little animals driven by a herder."
Part II: We Too are Human Beings (Bama)
The Innocence of Childhood and Street Attractions

Bama's narrative begins with a lighthearted description of her walk home from school, which normally takes ten minutes but is stretched to an hour due to her fascination with the vibrant street life.

  • Entertaining Novelties: The performing monkey, the snake charmer's snake, the cyclist pedaling for three consecutive days, and the changing colors of the street lights.
  • Cultural and Religious Sights: The Maariyaata temple with its huge bell, the cooking of pongal offerings, and political rallies with stages and mikes.
  • Seasonal Delicacies: Mangoes, cucumbers, sweet potatoes, palm-shoots, payasam, halva, and iced lollies that tempt her along the way.
The Incident of the Elder and the Vadai Packet

Bama's childhood innocence is shattered when she witnesses an elder from her community carrying a packet of snacks to the landlord.

  • The elder, a tall and respected man, carries a small packet of vadais by its string, holding it far from his body to avoid touching the packet itself.
  • He bows low and extends the packet reverently to the landlord, who sits on a stone ledge at the threshing floor.
  • Initially, Bama finds this sight hilarious and narrates it to her elder brother, Annan , expecting him to laugh.
The Awakening to Untouchability

Annan explains the grim reality behind the elder's behavior, transforming Bama's amusement into deep anger and sadness.

"Annan told me the man wasn't being funny when he carried the package like that. He said everybody believed that they were upper caste and therefore must not touch us. If they did, they would be polluted."

Literary Interpretation & Contextual Significance: This moment marks Bama's transition from childhood innocence to social consciousness. The physical distance maintained by the elder represents the deep-seated psychological and structural barriers of the caste system, which treats human beings as sources of pollution.

  • Bama is infuriated by the injustice: "But we too are human beings." She questions why her community must perform menial tasks for people who refuse to treat them with basic dignity.
  • Annan shares his own experience of being asked which street he lived on by a landlord's man, a subtle way of determining his caste.
Education as the Path to Resistance

Annan provides Bama with a constructive way to channel her anger, emphasizing education as the ultimate tool to overcome social inequality.

  • Annan advises her: "But if we study and make progress, we can throw away these indignities. So study with care, learn all you can."
  • Deeply inspired, Bama studies with immense dedication, stands first in her class, and earns the respect and friendship of her peers, proving that intellectual achievement can dismantle social barriers.
Thematic Synthesis & Literary Devices
Comparative Analysis of the Two Accounts
  • Common Theme: Both narratives depict the painful realization of marginalization during childhood and the subsequent rebellion against oppressive social structures.
  • Nature of Oppression: Zitkala-Sa faces cultural assimilation and institutional racism in a colonial school, whereas Bama faces systemic caste discrimination and untouchability within her own society.
  • Modes of Resistance: Zitkala-Sa's resistance is immediate, physical, and confrontational (hiding, kicking, scratching), while Bama's resistance is intellectual, long-term, and constructive (academic excellence and literary expression).
Literary Devices and Vocabulary Matrix
Literary Device / Phrase Textual Example Contextual Meaning & Subtext Stylistic Purpose
Metaphor "only one of many little animals driven by a herder" Compares the students to domesticated animals stripped of their free will. Highlights the dehumanizing impact of institutional assimilation.
Onomatopoeia "clatter of shoes", "clash of harsh noises" The harsh, metallic, and unfamiliar sounds of the school. Emphasizes the sensory assault and cultural shock experienced by Zitkala-Sa.
Symbolism The vadai packet carried by its string Represents the absurd and dehumanizing practices of untouchability. Exposes the physical and social distance maintained by caste prejudice.
Pun / Double Meaning "Karukku" Means both "Palmyra leaves" (with serrated, sword-like edges) and "freshness/embryo". Reflects both the pain of discrimination and the hope of new beginnings.
