Fine Arts: Class 11 — Chapter 2: Arts of the Indus Valley

Introduction to Indus Valley Art & Civic Planning

The arts of the Indus Valley Civilisation emerged during the second half of the third millennium BCE (c. 2500–1900 BCE). The artistic corpus includes highly realistic stone sculptures, bronze castings, terracotta figurines, incised and painted pottery, gold and semi-precious jewellery, and thousands of intricately carved seals. The anatomical accuracy and realistic delineation of both human and animal forms indicate highly advanced artistic sensibilities and sophisticated technical workflows.

Geographical Distribution & Civic Infrastructure

  • Major Urban Centers: Harappa in the north (on the Indus river system) and Mohenjodaro in the south showcase the earliest global examples of systematic civic planning.
  • Excavated Indian Sites: Key sites include Lothal and Dholavira in Gujarat, Rakhigarhi in Haryana, Ropar in Punjab, and Kalibangan in Rajasthan.
  • Civic Markers: Grid-pattern street layouts, multi-story brick houses, dedicated marketplace zones, large-scale storage facilities, administrative offices, public baths (e.g., the Great Bath of Mohenjodaro), and a highly advanced, covered drainage system.
Material-Based Artistic Traditions

1. Stone Statuary

Though stone sculptures are not abundant, they exhibit exceptional mastery in handling three-dimensional volumes. The two most celebrated examples are the Bearded Priest and the red sandstone Male Torso.

  • Bust of a Bearded Priest: Carved from soapstone (steatite). Features a shawl draped under the right arm and covering the left shoulder, decorated with distinct trefoil patterns. The eyes are elongated and half-closed in meditative concentration. The hair is parted in the middle, secured by a plain woven fillet. The ears resemble double shells with central perforations.
  • Male Torso: Carved from red sandstone, featuring socket holes in the neck and shoulders for attaching a separate head and arms. The abdomen is slightly prominent, showcasing a naturalistic, fleshy rendering.

2. Bronze Casting & The Lost-Wax Technique

Bronze casting was practiced on a wide scale across all major Harappan centers, utilizing the sophisticated lost-wax (cire perdue) process.

The Lost-Wax Process: First, a highly detailed figure is modeled in wax. This wax model is coated with a thick layer of clay and allowed to dry. The clay-covered model is then heated, causing the wax to melt and drain out through a tiny hole made at the base. Molten bronze is poured into the resulting hollow clay mould. Once the metal cools and solidifies, the outer clay shell is carefully chipped away to reveal the solid bronze statue.

Analysis / Historical Value: This metallurgical technique represents a continuous technological tradition in India, still practiced today in various regions for casting sacred and secular metalware.

  • Key Bronze Artifacts: The iconic Dancing Girl from Mohenjodaro, a bronze bull from Kalibangan, and a copper dog and bird from Lothal.
  • Late Harappan Metalwork: The late Harappan and Chalcolithic site of Daimabad in Maharashtra yielded highly advanced, large-scale metal-cast animal and human figures.

3. Terracotta Figurines

Compared to the refined stone and bronze sculptures, Harappan terracotta representations of the human form are generally cruder and more stylized, though highly realistic examples have been excavated at Gujarat sites and Kalibangan.

  • Mother Goddess: The most prominent terracotta form, characterized by heavy ornamentation, fan-shaped headdresses with cup-like lateral projections, pellet eyes, and a slit mouth.
  • Bearded Males: Figurines with coiled hair, rigidly upright postures, legs slightly apart, and arms parallel to the body, widely interpreted as representations of male deities.
  • Secular & Toy Objects: Terracotta masks of horned deities, toy carts with functional wheels, whistles, rattles, birds, animals, gamesmen, and discs.

4. Seals & Copper Tablets

Thousands of seals, primarily square plaques measuring 2×2 inches, have been discovered. They were carved mostly from soft steatite, but occasionally from agate, chert, copper, faience, terracotta, and ivory.

  • Iconography: Seals feature realistic representations of animals (such as the unicorn bull, humped bull, rhinoceros, tiger, elephant, bison, goat, and buffalo) alongside an undeciphered pictographic script.
  • Function: Primarily commercial, used to stamp clay sealings on trade goods. They also functioned as personal amulets or identity markers.
  • Pashupati Seal: Depicts a three-faced male figure seated cross-legged on a low throne, surrounded by wild animals: an elephant and a tiger to his right, a rhinoceros and a buffalo to his left, and two antelopes beneath his seat.
  • Copper Tablets: Square or rectangular copper plates featuring an animal or human figure on one side and an inscription on the other, carefully engraved with a burin. These are believed to have served as protective amulets.

5. Pottery, Beads, and Personal Ornaments

The Indus Valley people possessed a highly developed ceramic industry and a sophisticated personal adornment culture.

  • Pottery Typologies: Primarily wheel-made. Plain red clay pottery with or without a fine red or grey slip is most common. Black painted ware features a fine red slip decorated with geometric and stylized animal designs in glossy black paint. Rare types include polychrome pottery (vases with red, black, green, and yellow patterns), incised ware, and perforated pottery (likely used for straining beverages).
  • Bead Industry: Highly developed factories excavated at Chanhudaro and Lothal. Beads were crafted from carnelian, amethyst, jasper, crystal, quartz, steatite, turquoise, lapis lazuli, copper, bronze, gold, shell, and faience. Shapes include disc, cylindrical, spherical, barrel-shaped, and segmented forms.
  • Personal Attire & Cosmetics: Men and women wore two-piece garments resembling a modern dhoti and shawl (draped over the left shoulder). Archaeological finds of cinnabar, lipstick, and collyrium (eyeliner) indicate a highly fashion-conscious society.
