Fine Arts: Class 11 — Chapter 3: Arts of the Mauryan Period

Historical and Socio-Religious Context (6th Century BCE)

The sixth century BCE marked a major turning point in the Gangetic valley, characterized by the rise of new religious and social movements that challenged orthodox Vedic systems.

The Shraman Tradition and Rise of Heterodox Sects

  • Buddhism and Jainism emerged as key components of the shraman tradition, gaining rapid popularity by opposing the rigid varna and jati systems of the Hindu religion.
  • The kingdom of Magadha consolidated power over other regions, establishing a powerful empire that culminated in Mauryan hegemony by the fourth century BCE.
  • By the third century BCE, Emperor Ashoka patronized the Buddhist shraman tradition, facilitating its spread across a large part of India.

Popular Folk Cults and Assimilation

  • Religious practices were diverse and not confined to single modes of worship; folk cults centered on the worship of Yakshas (male nature spirits) and Yakshinis (female nature spirits) or mother-goddesses were highly prevalent.
  • Yaksha worship existed extensively before and after the advent of Buddhism and Jainism, and was eventually assimilated into their monumental iconographies.
Mauryan Pillars, Sculptures, and Rock-Cut Architecture

The Mauryan period witnessed the transition from wood and clay to monumental stone carving, producing pillars, rock-cut caves, and monumental sculptures.

Mauryan vs. Achamenian Pillars

  • Mauryan Pillars: Monolithic, rock-cut shafts displaying the carver's exceptional skill; erected by Ashoka across northern India with imperial inscriptions engraved on them.
  • Achamenian Pillars: Constructed in multiple stone pieces by masons rather than being carved from a single rock.
  • Capital Figures: The top portion of Mauryan pillars featured vigorous animal capitals (bull, lion, elephant) standing on a square or circular abacus decorated with stylised lotuses.
  • Key Pillar Sites: Basarah-Bakhira, Lauriya-Nandangarh, and Rampurva in Bihar; Sankisa and Sarnath in Uttar Pradesh.

Detailed Masterwork Analysis

The Sarnath Lion Capital

  • Discovery & Context: Discovered over a century ago at Sarnath; built by Ashoka to commemorate the first sermon (Dhammachakrapravartana).
  • Five Component Parts: (i) Shaft (now broken), (ii) inverted lotus bell base, (iii) circular drum with four animals proceeding clockwise, (iv) four majestic addorsed lions, and (v) crowning element Dharamchakra (large wheel, now broken and kept in museum).
  • Stylistic Features: Strong facial musculature, inversed lines of lips showing naturalistic projection, sharp lines of the curly mane with protruding volume, and heavily polished surface typical of the Mauryan period.
  • Abacus Details: Features a chakra (wheel) with 24 spokes in all four directions, with a bull, horse, elephant, and lion finely carved between each wheel, displaying dynamic movement in a limited space.

The Didarganj Yakshini

  • Location & Material: Kept in Patna Museum; tall, well-proportioned, free-standing sculpture in round made of sandstone with a highly polished surface.
  • Anatomical Sophistication: Round fleshy cheeks, small neck, sharp eyes, nose, and lips. Folds of muscles and heavy breasts depict a robust, muscular body.
  • Drapery & Ornaments: Necklace beads rendered in full round hanging to the belly; tight lower garment creates a bulging belly effect; folds of the garment on the legs shown by protruding lines clinging to the limbs, creating a transparent effect; thick bell-ornaments adorn the feet.
Monastic Buddhist Architecture: Stupas, Viharas, and Chaityas

With the popularity of Buddhism and Jainism, monastic complexes consisting of stupas, viharas, and chaityas were constructed on a large scale.

Stupa Evolution and Relic Distribution

  • Stupas were originally constructed over the relics of the Buddha.
  • Original Relic Sites: Rajagraha, Vaishali, Vethadipa, and Pava in Bihar; Kapilavastu, Allakappa, and Ramagrama in Nepal; Kushinagar and Pippalvina in Uttar Pradesh.
  • Structural Components: A stupa consists of a cylindrical drum, a circular anda (dome), a harmika (square railing on top), and a chhatra (umbrella). Later additions included the pradakshina patha (circumambulatory path) enclosed with railings and monumental toranas (gateways).
  • Sanchi Stupa: Built with bricks during the time of Ashoka, later covered with stone and expanded with new additions.

Patronage Patterns and Artisans

  • Patronage was highly collective rather than purely royal. Patrons ranged from lay devotees and kings to gahapatis (householders) and merchant guilds.
  • Inscriptions record donations along with the donors' places of origin and professions.
  • Very few inscriptions mention individual artisans, with rare exceptions like Kanha at Pitalkhora and his disciple Balaka at Kondane caves in Maharashtra.
  • Artisans were categorized into specialized guilds: stone carvers, goldsmiths, stone-polishers, and carpenters.

Iconographic Transition: Symbolic to Narrative Art

  • Early Phase (Aniconic): Buddha was depicted symbolically through footprints, stupas, lotus thrones, and the chakra (wheel).
  • Narrative Phase: Events from the life of the Buddha and Jataka stories (stories of Buddha's previous births) were carved on the railings and toranas of stupas.
  • Narrative Techniques: Synoptic narrative, continuous narrative, and episodic narrative.
  • Key Life Events Depicted: Birth, renunciation, enlightenment, dhammachakrapravartana, and mahaparinibbana.
  • Frequently Depicted Jatakas: Chhadanta Jataka, Vidurpundita Jataka, Ruru Jataka, Sibi Jataka, Vessantara Jataka, and Shama Jataka.
