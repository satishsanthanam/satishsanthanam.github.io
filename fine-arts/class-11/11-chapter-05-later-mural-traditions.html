Fine Arts: Class 11 — Chapter 5: Later Mural Traditions

Introduction and Badami Cave Murals

Post-Ajanta mural traditions show a continuation of cave excavations where sculpting and painting occurred simultaneously, with sculptures also being plastered and painted.

The Chalukyan Murals of Badami

  • Badami: Capital of the early Chalukya Dynasty (ruled 543–598 CE) in Karnataka, succeeding Vakataka power in the Deccan.
  • Cave No. 4 (Vishnu Cave): Patronised by Chalukya king Mangalesha (son of Pulakesi I, brother of Kirtivarman I); contains an inscription dated 578–579 CE recording Vaishnava dedication.
  • Visual Style: Sinuously drawn lines, fluid forms, compact compositions representing an extension of Ajanta style to South India; features large eye-sockets, half-closed eyes, and protruding lips.
  • Narrative Scenes: Palace scenes depicting Kirtivarman I with his wife and feudatories watching a dance performance, with Indra and his retinue in the corner.
Murals under Pallava, Pandya, and Chola Kings

Pallava Dynasty Patronage

  • Mahendravarman I (7th century CE): Built temples at Panamalai, Mandagapattu, and Kanchipuram.
  • Mandagapattu Inscription: Honors Mahendravarman I with titles like Vichitrachitta (curious-minded), Chitrakarapuli (tiger among artists), and Chaityakari (temple builder).
  • Panamalai Devi: Gracefully drawn female divinity with rhythmic lines and subtle ornamentation.
  • Kanchipuram Temple: Patronised by Rajsimha; features Somaskanda paintings with round, large faces and elongated torsos.

"Vichitrachitta, Chitrakarapuli, Chaityakari"

Analysis / Historical Value: Inscriptional titles of Pallava King Mahendravarman I at Mandagapattu, highlighting his multifaceted patronage of innovative temple architecture and painting traditions.

Pandya Dynasty and Sittanvasal

  • Sittanvasal Jaina Caves (9th century CE): Features paintings on ceilings, verandas, and brackets.
  • Celestial Nymphs: Dancing figures on veranda pillars with vermilion red contours on light backgrounds, yellow bodies, supple limbs, and slightly elongated, protruding eyes.

Chola Dynasty Masterpieces

  • Brihadeswara Temple (Thanjavur): Built by Rajaraja Chola in the 11th century CE; features paintings on narrow passage walls surrounding the sanctum.
  • Double-Layered Murals: Chola-period paintings were discovered underneath a 16th-century Nayak period layer.
  • Iconography: Narrations of Lord Shiva (Shiva in Kailash, Shiva as Tripurantaka, Shiva as Nataraja), and portraits of Rajaraja Chola with his mentor Kuruvar.
Vijayanagara and Nayaka Murals

Vijayanagara Dynasty Murals (14th–16th Centuries)

  • Hampi Virupaksha Temple: Paintings on the ceiling of the mandapa depicting dynastic history, Ramayana, Mahabharata, and the spiritual teacher Vidyaranya in a palanquin procession.
  • Lepakshi Veerabhadra Temple: Wall paintings of Shiva temple showing two-dimensional figures, profile faces, large frontal eyes, and narrow waists.

Nayaka Dynasty Murals (17th–18th Centuries)

  • Locations: Thiruparakunram, Sreerangam, Tiruvarur, Chidambaram, and Chengam.
  • Stylistic Features: Extension of Vijayanagara style with flat backgrounds, profile figures, slim-waisted males, and dynamic movement (e.g., Nataraja at Tiruvalanjuli).
Kerala Murals and Traditional Folk Wall Paintings

Kerala Mural Tradition (16th–18th Centuries)

  • Stylistic Synthesis: Evolved a distinct pictorial language taking cues from Kathakali and kalam ezhuthu; features vibrant, luminous colors and three-dimensional human figures.
  • Key Sites: Dutch Palace (Kochi), Krishnapuram Palace (Kayamkulam), Padmanabhapuram Palace, and Pundareekapuram Krishna Temple.

Traditional Folk Mural Forms

  • Pithoro: Traditional murals in parts of Rajasthan and Gujarat.
  • Mithila Painting: Folk art from northern Bihar's Mithila region.
  • Warli Paintings: Tribal wall paintings of Maharashtra.
