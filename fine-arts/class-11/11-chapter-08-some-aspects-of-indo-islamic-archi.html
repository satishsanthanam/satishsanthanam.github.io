Fine Arts: Class 11 — Chapter 8: Some Aspects of Indo-Islamic Architecture

Introduction and Architectural Evolution

In the seventh and eighth centuries CE, Islam spread towards Spain and India, arriving primarily via Muslim merchants, traders, holy men, and conquerors over six hundred years. While early construction began in Sind and Gujarat by the eighth century, large-scale architectural activity commenced in the early thirteenth century under the Delhi Sultanate following the Turkish conquest of northern India.

Structural Transition: Trabeate to Arcuate

  • Trabeate Style: Traditional Indian construction utilizing brackets, pillars, and lintels to support flat roofs or shallow domes. Arches were occasionally shaped in wood or stone but could not bear heavy top structures.
  • Arcuate Style: Introduced gradually, employing arches capable of supporting the weight of massive domes. These arches were constructed using voussoirs (interlocking blocks) fitted with keystones.
  • Domes: Supported by pendentives and squinches, enabling the spanning of vast spaces while keeping interiors free of pillars.

Cultural and Artistic Synthesis

The migration and conquests led to a syncretic architectural style known as Indo-Saracenic or Indo-Islamic architecture, combining local traditions with Islamic practices.

  • Iconography: Unlike Hindu traditions that adorned surfaces with sculptures of living forms, Islamic religious art avoided replicating living forms, focusing instead on arabesque, geometrical patterns, and calligraphy on plaster and stone.
Typologies, Styles, and Decorative Forms

Typologies of Structures

Architectural edifices were constructed by wealthy patrons (rulers, nobles, merchants, and guilds) to serve religious and secular needs:

  • Mosques and Jama Masjids for daily and congregational prayers.
  • Tombs and dargahs (shrines of Sufi saints).
  • Minars (towers) and hammams (bathhouses).
  • Formally laid out gardens, madrasas (educational institutions), sarais (caravanserais/lodges), and Kos minars (distance markers).

Categories of Styles

  • Imperial Style: Associated with the Delhi Sultanate.
  • Provincial Style: Developed in regional centers like Mandu, Gujarat, Bengal, and Jaunpur. Gujarat borrowed heavily from temple traditions, using toranas, lintels in mihrabs, and bell-and-chain motifs.
  • Mughal Style: Prominent in Delhi, Agra, and Lahore.
  • Deccani Style: Developed in Bijapur and Golconda.

Decorative Forms and Techniques

  • Plaster and Stucco: Incised or carved plaster, left plain or painted.
  • Motifs: Flowers (local and Persian varieties), cypress, chinar trees, and flower vases. Inner curves of arches featured lotus bud fringes.
  • Tiles: Used on walls and domes from the 14th to 16th centuries in blue, turquoise, green, and yellow.
  • Tessellation and Pietra dura: Inlay of semi-precious stones (like lapis lazuli, yellow marble, jade, and jasper) into stone surfaces.
  • Jalis: Perforated lattice screens providing light and ventilation.
  • Roof Elements: Central domes topped with inverted lotus motifs and metal/stone pinnacles, surrounded by chatris (cupolas) and tiny minarets.
Materials and Fortifications

Materials for Construction

  • Walls were extremely thick, built of easily available rubble masonry, and cased with chunam (limestone plaster) or dressed stone (quartzite, sandstone, buff, marble).
  • Polychrome tiles finished the walls, and from the 17th century, bricks were introduced for greater structural flexibility.

Forts and Military Architecture

Forts symbolized royal power and sovereignty, built on commanding heights for security, strategic advantage, and residential/official complexes.

  • Daulatabad Fort: Featured staggered entrances to prevent elephant attacks, twin concentric forts, and a complex labyrinth pathway to trap enemies.
  • Gwalior Fort: Renowned for its steep, invincible height and palace complexes.
  • Chittorgarh: The largest fort in Asia, containing victory towers (stambhas) and numerous water bodies.

"Babur, who did not find much merit in many things he saw in Hindustan, was said to have been overawed at the sight of the Gwalior Fort."

Analysis / Historical Value: Highlights the strategic and aesthetic dominance of the Gwalior Fort, which impressed even the highly critical Mughal founder Babur due to its steep, invincible height and majestic palace complexes.

Sarais, Common Structures, and Congregational Spaces

Sarais

Sarais were simple square or rectangular structures built to provide temporary accommodation for travelers, pilgrims, and merchants. They acted as public domains that fostered cross-cultural interactions and syncretic cultural tendencies.

Structures for Common People

Non-royal architecture included domestic buildings, temples, mosques, khanqahs (Sufi hermitages), dargahs, commemorative gateways, and bazaars, showcasing a blend of local and imperial styles.

Jama Masjid Layout

Large congregational mosques served as the commercial, cultural, and religious focus of medieval cities. Standard features included:

  • An open courtyard (Sahn) surrounded on three sides by cloisters.
  • The Qibla Liwan on the west, housing the mihrab (prayer niche indicating the direction of Mecca) and the mimbar (pulpit).
  • Minarets for the call to prayer and a central tank for ablutions.
