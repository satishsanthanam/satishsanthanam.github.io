Fine Arts: Class 12 — Chapter 1: The Manuscript Painting Tradition

Introduction to Indian Manuscript Painting & Canons of Art

The Chitrasutra and Canons of Painting

The Chitrasutra, a chapter in the third Khanda of the Vishnudharmottara Purana (5th century CE), serves as the foundational source book for Indian art and painting. It outlines the canons of image-making known as pratima lakshana.

"Different limbs of painting, such as roopbheda (looks/appearance), pramana (measurements/proportion), bhava (expressions), lavanya yojana (aesthetic composition), sadrishya (resemblance), and varnikabhanga (use of brush/colours) have been explained at length..."

Analysis / Historical Value: These six limbs (Shadanga) established a systematic, highly sophisticated aesthetic framework that guided Indian artists across centuries, ensuring continuity of style and spiritual depth in both religious and secular art forms.

  • Roopbheda: Aesthetic differentiation of forms, looks, and physical appearance.
  • Pramana: Structural laws of proportion, measurement, and spatial scale.
  • Bhava: Depiction of inner emotions, states of mind, and psychological expressions.
  • Lavanya Yojana: Infusion of grace, artistic beauty, and aesthetic composition.
  • Sadrishya: Verisimilitude, portraiture likeness, or analogical resemblance to nature.
  • Varnikabhanga: Masterful execution of brushwork, pigment application, and color theory.

The Miniature Format and Manuscript Folios

Medieval Indian paintings are widely classified as miniature paintings due to their small, intimate scale. Unlike large-scale mural paintings designed for palace walls, miniatures were portable, hand-held objects meant to be viewed from a close distance.

  • Manuscript Illustrations: Pictorial translations of poetic verses from epics, canonical texts, or musical treatises. Verses were handwritten in clearly demarcated box-like spaces at the top or on the reverse of the folio.
  • Thematic Sets: Conceived as loose folios belonging to a unified narrative set (e.g., Ramayana, Bhagavata Purana, Gita Govinda, Ragamala), wrapped in protective cloth bundles and stored in royal libraries.
  • Colophon Page: The most critical folio of a set, providing historical records of the patron's name, the scribe/artist, the date, and the geographic location of completion.
Western Indian and Jain Schools of Painting

Socio-Economic Context and Patronage

Thriving primarily in Gujarat, southern Rajasthan, and western Central India, this school was heavily patronized by the wealthy Jain merchant class and local chieftains enriched by maritime trade routes.

  • Shaastradaan: The pious act of donating illustrated manuscripts to monastic libraries (bhandars), which stimulated massive artistic production as a gesture of charity and religious devotion.
  • Material Evolution: Transitioned from fragile palm-leaf manuscripts (etched with calligraphic styluses and bound with central cords between wooden patlis) to paper in the 14th century, enabling larger compositions and richer pigments.

Key Canonical Texts and Iconography

  • Kalpasutra: Recites the biographical narratives of the 24 Tirthankaras. Focuses on five key life events: conception, birth, renunciation, enlightenment, and salvation. Features Trishala's fourteen dreams (symbolizing the birth of a great sovereign or saint).
  • Kalakacharyakatha: Narrative of Acharya Kalaka's heroic quest to rescue his abducted sister from an evil king, featuring magical elements like a spellbound donkey.
  • Sangrahini Sutra: A 12th-century cosmological text detailing the structure of the universe, planetary distances, and spatial mapping.
  • Uttaradhyana Sutra: Prescribes the ethical code of conduct and teachings of Lord Mahavir for Jain monks.

Stylistic and Visual Characteristics

  • Linearity: Dominated by thin, wiry, highly energetic outlines.
  • Facial Features: Highly stylized profiles featuring the characteristic protruding "further eye" to suggest three-dimensionality.
  • Color Palette: Lavish use of bright primary colors, later dominated by gold and lapis lazuli during the peak creative phase (1350–1450 CE).
  • Architectural Motifs: Inclusion of Sultanate-style domes and pointed arches, reflecting contemporary political and cultural synthesis in Gujarat, Mandu, and Jaunpur.
Pre-Mughal Indigenous Styles and the Sultanate School

The Indigenous Style (Pre-Mughal / Pre-Rajasthani)

During the late 15th and 16th centuries, a parallel painting tradition flourished under feudal lords and wealthy citizens, depicting secular, religious, and literary themes before the formalization of Mughal court styles.

  • Key Texts: Chaurpanchashika, Mahapurana, Aranyaka Parvan of the Mahabharata, Bhagvata Purana, and Gita Govinda.
  • Stylistic Innovations: Distinctive figure types with transparent fabrics (odhnis ballooned over the head with stiff, standing edges), suggestive architecture, stylized water bodies depicted with hatchings, and formalized representations of flora and fauna.

The Sultanate School of Painting

Formed by the intermingling of indigenous Indian styles with Persian, Turkic, and Afghan influences brought by the ruling Sultans of Malwa, Gujarat, and Jaunpur.

  • Visual Synthesis: Combined indigenous Indian figures and compositions with Persian color palettes, refined physiognomy, and highly decorative, simplified landscapes.
  • Nimatnama (Book of Delicacies): Painted at Mandu (c. 1500–1510 CE) under Nasir Shah Khalji. A royal recipe book containing sections on hunting, medicine, cosmetics, and perfumes.
  • Laurchanda: Illustrated romantic manuscripts reflecting Sufi mystical ideas.
The Pala School of Painting

Monastic Buddhist Art of Eastern India

Flourishing under the Pala Dynasty (750 CE to mid-12th century) in Bihar and Bengal, this school represents the last great phase of Buddhist classical art in India.

  • Centers of Production: Great monastic universities like Nalanda and Vikramsila, which housed scriptoriums and bronze-casting workshops.
  • Global Dispersal: Pilgrims and international students carried these portable palm-leaf manuscripts and small bronzes across Southeast Asia, directly influencing the art of Nepal, Tibet, Burma, Sri Lanka, and Java.
  • Stylistic Features: Characterized by flowing, sinuous lines, subdued color tones, and plastic, sculptural modeling reminiscent of the classical murals of Ajanta.
  • Key Masterwork: Astasahasrika Prajnaparamita (Perfection of Wisdom in Eight Thousand Lines), painted at Nalanda during the 15th regnal year of King Ramapala (late 11th century).
  • Decline: The school abruptly ended in the first half of the 13th century following the destruction of the great Buddhist monasteries by Muslim invaders.
