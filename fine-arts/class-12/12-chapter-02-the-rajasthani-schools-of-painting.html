Fine Arts: Class 12 — Chapter 2: The Rajasthani Schools of Painting

Introduction & Historical Context

The term Rajasthani Schools of Painting refers to the distinct manuscript and miniature painting traditions that flourished in the princely states and thikanas of Rajasthan and parts of Madhya Pradesh from the 16th to the early 19th centuries.

  • Geographical Spread: Centered in Mewar, Bundi, Kota, Jaipur, Bikaner, Kishangarh, Jodhpur (Marwar), Malwa, Sirohi, and neighboring principalities.
  • Nomenclature Evolution: In 1916, scholar Anand Coomaraswamy coined the term Rajput Paintings to differentiate these indigenous, Hindu-patroned works from the Islamic Mughal School of Painting. This initial categorization included the Pahari Schools of the Himalayas and the Malwa School of Central India. Modern scholarship has retired the term 'Rajput Schools' as obsolete, replacing it with specific regional categories: Rajasthani and Pahari.
  • Stylistic Diversity: Despite geographical proximity, schools varied widely in execution (bold vs. fine), color palettes (brilliant vs. gentle), compositional elements, and narrative modes.
Materials, Techniques & Workshop Organization

The production of Rajasthani miniatures was a highly structured, collaborative process utilizing specialized local materials.

  1. Support Preparation: Paintings were executed on waslis, which were layered sheets of thin handmade paper pasted together with starch glue to achieve a sturdy, card-like thickness.
  2. Underdrawing: The initial composition outline was sketched on the wasli using black or brown ink, followed by brief color notations or sample patches.
  3. Pigment Application: Colors were derived from natural minerals, organic sources, and precious metals like gold and silver, mixed with gum arabic or glue as a binding medium. Brushes were crafted from fine camel or squirrel hair.
  4. Burnishing: Once completed, the painting was placed face down on a smooth surface and burnished from the back with a polished agate stone to achieve a uniform, lustrous sheen.
  5. Atelier Teamwork: Workshops operated under a master artist who designed the composition and executed the preliminary drawings. Specialized pupils or assistants painted specific elements like architecture, landscape, animals, or base coloring. The master artist returned to apply the final details and outlines, while a professional scribe wrote accompanying poetic verses in designated borders or panels.
Themes of Rajasthani Paintings

The thematic repertoire of Rajasthani painting was deeply intertwined with the contemporary Bhakti Movement, classical literature, and courtly life.

  • Vaishnavism: The cults of Rama and Krishna swept northern and western India, positioning Krishna not only as a supreme deity but also as the archetypal lover. The mystical union of the human soul with God was symbolized through the love between Radha and Krishna.
  • Gita Govinda: Composed by the 12th-century court poet Jayadeva of Bengal, this lyrical Sanskrit poem ('Song of the Cowherd') evokes shringara rasa (erotic love) to portray the divine romance of Radha and Krishna.
  • Rasamanjari: Written by 14th-century Maithil Brahmin Bhanu Datta, this Sanskrit treatise classifies heroes (nayakas) and heroines (nayikas) based on age (baal, taruna, praudha), physical traits (padmini, chitrini, shankhini, hastini), and emotional states (khandita, vasaksajja, abhisarika, utka).
  • Rasikapriya: Composed in Brajbhasha by Keshav Das (court poet of Orchha) in 1591, this text explores complex emotional states of love, jealousy, and separation through the characters of Radha and Krishna.
  • Kavipriya: Another work by Keshav Das written for the courtesan Rai Parbin. Its tenth chapter, the Baramasa, describes the seasonal life, festivals, and emotions of lovers across the twelve months of the year.
  • Bihari Satsai: Authored by Bihari Lal in 1662 at the Jaipur court, consisting of 700 moralizing and romantic aphoristic verses.
  • Ragamala: Pictorial translations of classical musical modes (ragas and raginis) envisioned in human or divine forms. Organized into families headed by six male ragas (Bhairava, Malkos, Hindol, Dipak, Megha, and Shri), each associated with six female consorts (raginis), specific seasons, and times of day.
  • Secular & Courtly Themes: Included royal portraits, darbar assemblies, hunting expeditions, historical battles, festivals, wedding processions, and romantic bardic legends like Dhola-Maru, Sohni-Mahiwal, Mrigavat, Chaurpanchashika, and Laurchanda.
Regional Schools of Painting

Malwa School of Painting

  • Chronology & Style: Flourished between 1600 and 1700 CE. Characterized by a flat, two-dimensional, simplistic visual language that evolved directly from early Jain manuscript traditions and the Chaurpanchashika style.
  • Geographical Anomaly: Defies a single state center; instead, it was spread across Central Indian territories like Mandu, Nusratgarh, Narsyang Sahar, and Bundelkhand.
  • Key Artworks: Illustrated sets of the Amaru Shataka (1652 CE) and a Ragamala painted by Madho Das (1680 CE).
  • Patronage: Notable for a complete absence of portraits or mentions of patron kings, suggesting paintings were purchased by local rulers (like the Datia rulers) from traveling artist guilds.

Mewar School of Painting

  • Significance: Conjectured to be the earliest and most continuous stylistic center of painting in Rajasthan. Early bold, indigenous styles transitioned into a refined manner after contact with the Mughal court during the reign of Rana Karan Singh.
  • Chawand Ragamala (1605): Painted by artist Nisardin at the temporary capital Chawand. Features a crucial colophon page, simple compositions, direct narrative approach, and vibrant colors.
  • Jagat Singh I Era (1628–1652): The golden age of Mewar painting. Virtuoso artists Sahibdin and Manohar reformulated the school's aesthetics. Sahibdin painted the Ragamala (1628), Rasikapriya, Bhagavata Purana (1648), and the Yuddha Kanda of the Ramayana (1652). Manohar executed the Bal Kanda of the Ramayana (1649).
  • Later Developments: Artist Jagannath painted the Bihari Satsai in 1719. By the 18th century, Mewar painting shifted from literary texts to secular courtly activities, hunting scenes, and royal pastimes.
  • Nathdwara Sub-school: Developed in the late 17th century near Udaipur as a major Vaishnava center. Famous for pichhwais—large, elaborate paintings on cloth hung behind the deity Shrinathji.

Bundi School of Painting

  • Aesthetics: Renowned for an exquisite, unblemished color sense, rich formal designs, and a deep interest in depicting lush, detailed vegetation, water bodies, and dramatic landscapes.
  • Chunar Ragamala (1591): The earliest formative phase of Bundi painting, executed at Chunar under Rao Bhoj Singh. Inscribed in Persian, it names artists Shaykh Hasan, Shaykh Ali, and Shaykh Hatim, who identified as pupils of Mughal masters Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd-us-Samad.
  • Patronage: Blossomed under Rao Chattar Sal (1631–1659) and Rao Bhao Singh (1659–1682). Reached its peak of refinement under Umed Singh (1749–1771), where paintings acquired minute detailing and incorporated Deccani color sensibilities.
  • Feminine Ideal: Petite women with round faces, receding foreheads, sharp noses, full cheeks, sharply penciled eyebrows, and extremely narrow ('pinched') waists.
  • Chitrashala: The palace walls of Bundi, decorated under Ram Singh (1821–1889), feature magnificent murals of royal processions, hunts, and Krishna legends.

Kota School of Painting

  • Origin: Kota separated from Bundi in 1625 when Emperor Jahangir divided the territory. The painting school commenced around the 1660s during the reign of Jagat Singh.
  • Stylistic Evolution: Early Kota paintings are nearly indistinguishable from Bundi works. By the reign of Ram Singh I (1686–1708), Kota artists developed a highly individual style, becoming the first in India to render wild, untamed landscapes as the primary subject of compositions.
  • Hunting Obsession: Under Umed Singh (1770–1819), the school became obsessed with the depiction of the royal chase (shikar). These paintings served as detailed records of hunting expeditions in dense jungles, in which even court women participated.
  • Visual Traits: Spontaneous, calligraphic brushwork, marked shading, double-lidded eyes, and unparalleled skill in rendering animals in combat.

Bikaner School of Painting

  • Mughal Influence: Due to close political ties with the Mughal court, Bikaner developed a highly refined, delicate style characterized by a subdued, elegant color palette.
  • Key Masters: Karan Singh employed Delhi master painter Ustad Ali Raza around 1650. Under Anup Singh (1669–1698), master artist Ruknuddin amalgamated indigenous Rajasthani idioms with Deccani and Mughal conventions. Other prominent atelier artists included Ibrahim, Nathu, Sahibdin, and Isa.
  • Mandi System: Bikaner paintings were produced in professional studios called Mandis, where groups of artists worked under a master. The court archivist recorded the master's name and date on the back of the finished work, a practice called gudarayi (meaning to 'lift' or finish a pupil's work).
  • Documentation: Bikaner is the best-documented school of Rajasthani painting due to detailed inscriptions in Marwari and Persian, and the survival of royal daily ledgers (Bahis). Artists, known as Ustas or Ustads, frequently painted self-portraits.

Kishangarh School of Painting

  • Aesthetics: The most highly stylized of all Rajasthani schools. Distinguished by an exquisite sophistication and a unique facial type: arched eyebrows, long lotus-petal eyes tinged with pink, drooping heavy eyelids, sharp slender noses, and thin lips.
  • Patronage & Themes: Founded by Kishan Singh in 1609. Under Raj Singh (1706–1748), the rulers' initiation into the Pushtimargiya Vaishnava cult made Krishna Lila the dominant theme.
  • Sawant Singh & Nihal Chand: Prince Sawant Singh (who wrote devotional poetry under the pen name Nagari Das) fell in love with a gifted singer and poetess whom he titled Bani Thani ('the elegant lady of fashion'). His court artist, Nihal Chand (active 1735–1757), visualized Sawant Singh's poetry, portraying the divine lovers Radha and Krishna with the distinct facial features of Bani Thani and Sawant Singh, set within vast, panoramic, green landscapes.

Jodhpur School of Painting

  • Stylistic Tension: Maintained a continuous tension between imported Mughal courtly aesthetics (seen in formal portraits and darbar scenes) and a robust, indigenous, folkish style that resisted foreign influence.
  • Early Phase: The earliest dated work is a Ragamala set painted at Pali by artist Virji in 1623.
  • Patronage: Maharaja Jaswant Singh (1638–1678) initiated a trend of documentary court paintings and Krishna-themed works. Ajit Singh's (1679–1724) reign popularized heroic equestrian portraits of the legendary warrior Veer Durgadas Rathore.
  • Man Singh Era (1803–1843): Marked by highly innovative, large-scale manuscript sets of the Ramayana (1804), Dhola-Maru, Panchatantra, and Shiva Purana. As a follower of the Nath Sampradaya, Man Singh also commissioned extensive paintings of himself with Nath gurus and the manuscript Nath Charita (1824).

Jaipur School of Painting

  • Mughal Proximity: Based in the old capital Amer, Jaipur had the closest political and matrimonial ties with the Mughals, strongly influencing its early courtly aesthetics.
  • Sawai Jai Singh (1699–1743): Founded the planned capital Jaipur in 1727. He brought Mughal painters from Delhi, reorganized the royal painting atelier and storehouse (Suratkhana), and commissioned extensive sets of the Rasikapriya, Gita Govinda, and Ragamala where the hero's face resembled the king.
  • Sawai Ishwari Singh (1743–1750): Patronized court portraiture and leisure scenes (elephant fights, hunts). His atelier included the master portraitist Sahibram and artist Muhammad Shah.
  • Sawai Pratap Singh (1779–1803): Under his rule, direct Mughal influence receded, replaced by a vibrant, indigenous Jaipur style. A poet and devout follower of Krishna, Pratap Singh employed over 50 artists, producing large-format, life-size portraits and lavishly using gold leaf.
