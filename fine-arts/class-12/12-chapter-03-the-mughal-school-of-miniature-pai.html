Fine Arts: Class 12 — Chapter 3: The Mughal School of Miniature Painting

Introduction & Historical Context of Mughal Painting

The Mughal School of Miniature Painting represents a highly sophisticated style of art that flourished in the northern Indian subcontinent from the sixteenth century until the mid-nineteenth century. It is celebrated for its synthesis of indigenous Indian, Persian, and later European artistic traditions, creating a unique visual language that redefined the artistic paradigm of the region.

Key Characteristics & Influences
  • Amalgamation of Styles: A harmonious blend of Islamic, Hindu, and European visual cultures and aesthetics.
  • The Royal Atelier: A highly formalised workshop consisting of calligraphers, painters, gilders, and binders. Artworks were primarily produced for manuscripts and imperial albums (Muraqqas) meant for royal viewing.
  • Aesthetic Shift: Transitioned from the flat perspective, bold lines, and vivid palettes of pre-Mughal indigenous styles to the subtlety, three-dimensional figures, and optical realism of the Mughal style.
Chronological Development & Imperial Patronage

The evolution of Mughal painting is closely tied to the personal tastes, political fortunes, and cultural agendas of successive Mughal emperors.

Babur (1526–1530)

The founder of the dynasty, Babur brought Persian and Central Asian aesthetic sensibilities to India. A man of letters, his autobiography, the Baburnama, records his keen interest in nature, portraiture, and artists like Bihzad and Shah Muzaffar.

"Bihzad’s work was dainty but he did not draw the faces well; he used to greatly lengthen the double chin (ghab-ghab); and drew bearded faces admirably."

Analysis / Historical Value: This excerpt from the Baburnama demonstrates Babur's critical eye for portraiture and his appreciation for the Herat school of Persian painting, setting a precedent for detailed artistic observation.

Humayun (1530–1556)

During his exile in the Safavid court of Shah Tahmasp, Humayun witnessed the magnificent Persian miniature tradition. Upon regaining power, he invited master artists Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd us Samad to establish the imperial studio (Nigaar Khana) in Kabul and later Delhi, initiating the project of illustrating the Hamza Nama.

Akbar (1556–1605)

Under Akbar, the royal atelier grew to employ over a hundred Persian and indigenous Indian artists, leading to a vibrant Indo-Persian style. Believed to have suffered from dyslexia, Akbar heavily prioritized visual narratives and manuscript illustrations.

  • Hamza Nama Project: Spanning 1567–1582, consisting of 14 volumes and 1400 illustrations executed on cloth-backed paper in gouache.
  • Cultural Integration: Commissioned Persian translations and illustrations of sacred Hindu texts, including the Mahabharata (known as the Razm Nama, completed in 1589 under Daswant) and the Ramayana.
  • European Contact: Introduced Christian themes and Western naturalism, as seen in early works like Madonna and Child (1580).

Jahangir (1605–1627)

Jahangir shifted the atelier's focus from mass-produced manuscript illustrations to high-quality individual paintings by master artists. His reign marked the peak of naturalism, scientific accuracy in flora and fauna, and complex allegorical portraiture.

  • Key Artists: Employed Aqa Riza, his son Abul Hasan (titled Nadir al Zaman), and the legendary nature painter Ustad Mansur (titled Nadir ul Asr).
  • Muraqqas: Individual paintings mounted in albums with highly illuminated margins embellished with gold, flora, and fauna.

Shah Jahan (1628–1658)

Shah Jahan favored idealisation, great stylisation, and jewel-like refinement over raw naturalism. Artworks under his reign focused on imperial majesty, courtly pomp, and architectural grandeur, epitomized by the grand manuscript Padshahnama.

His liberal son, Dara Shikoh, was a major patron of mystical and philosophical themes, immortalized in works depicting him with Sufi sages.

Later Mughal Painting (1658–1857)

While Aurangzeb did not actively patronize the arts, the royal atelier continued to function. However, the gradual decline of imperial patronage led highly skilled artists to migrate to provincial courts (Oudh, Bengal, Punjab Hills), giving rise to Provincial Mughal styles. The last flickers of the style occurred under Muhammad Shah Rangila and the final emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, before converging into the Company School.

The Process, Colours, and Techniques

Mughal miniatures were highly collaborative works of art, combining the specialized skills of multiple craftsmen within the workshop.

The Painting Process
  1. Preparation and cutting of handmade paper to fit the manuscript layout.
  2. Ruling of pages and writing of the text by calligraphers, leaving designated spaces for illustrations.
  3. Tarh: The master artist sketches the initial composition.
  4. Chiharanama: Detailed drawing of faces and portraits.
  5. Rangamizi: The application of colours in layers.
  6. Burnishing the completed painting face-down on a smooth surface with an agate gemstone to set the pigments and create a radiant sheen.

Pigments and Materials
  • Brushes: Crafted using the fine hair of squirrels or kittens.
  • Vermilion: Sourced from cinnabar.
  • Ultramarine: Ground from precious Lapis Lazuli.
  • Yellow: Derived from orpiment.
  • White: Made from finely ground sea shells.
  • Black: Sourced from charcoal lampblack.
  • Metals: Gold and silver powders were mixed directly with pigments or sprinkled to add imperial luxury.
