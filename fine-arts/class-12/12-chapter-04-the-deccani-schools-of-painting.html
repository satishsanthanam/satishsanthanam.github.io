Fine Arts: Class 12 — Chapter 4: The Deccani Schools of Painting

Overview of Deccani Painting
  • Timeline & Evolution: Nurtured from the late 16th century until the 1680s (Mughal conquest of the Deccan). Continued through the 19th century under the Asafiya dynasty and in provincial courts of Rajas and Nawabs in Hyderabad under the Nizam.
  • Aesthetic Identity: Long misclassified as Indo-Persian, Safavid, Turkish, or Mughal. Recognized by modern historians as a distinct, full-fledged school sustained by Deccani rulers with unique political and cultural visions.
  • Key Stylistic Traits: Intense colors, rich sensuality, dense compositions, high circular horizons, and an overall aura of romance expressed through natural and vivid idioms.
Ahmadnagar School of Painting

Key Manuscripts & Stylistic Features

  • Tarif-i-Hussain Shahi: Earliest examples (1565–1569) celebrating the reign of Hussain Nizam Shah I (1553–1565). Contains 12 miniatures; battle scenes are of low artistic interest, but marriage/queen scenes feature gorgeous colors and sensuous lines.
  • Female Costumes & Features: Women wear modified northern costumes with choli (bodice) and long braided pigtails ending in tassels. Features a long southern-style scarf passing below the hips, similar to Lepakshi frescoes. Hair is rolled in a bun at the nape of the neck.
  • Landscape & Persian Influence: High circular horizons and gold skies show strong Persian influence. Landscapes feature neutral-colored grounds patterned with small stylised plants or symmetrical architectural domes.
  • Male Costumes: Decisively northern jama with pointed tails (early Akbari style) and small pagri.
  • Bukhara Influence: Original paintings in the Gulistan of 1567 are attributed to Bukhara artists who also worked in the Deccan. Supported by a manuscript in the Bankipore Library (Patna) signed by scribe Yusuf and dedicated to Ibrahim Adil (1569) / Ibrahim Qutb Shah of Golconda (1550-1580) in pure Bukhara idiom.
Bijapur School of Painting

Key Manuscripts & Royal Patrons

  • Nujum al-Ulum (1570): A richly illustrated encyclopedia containing 876 miniatures depicting weapons, utensils, and astronomical constellations. Influenced by Ottoman Turkish manuscripts.
  • Royal Patrons: Nurtured under Ali Adil Shah I (1558–1580) and Ibrahim Adil Shah II (1580–1627). Ibrahim II was an expert in Indian music, author of Nauras-nama, and patron of the Ragamala series (1590s).
  • The Throne of Prosperity: A symbolic diagram from Nujum al-Ulum featuring a seven-stage auspicious throne supported by elephants, tigers, palm trees, peacocks, and tribes. Incorporates Gujarati wood-carved doorway structures and Deccani foliage against a deep blue sky.
  • Yogini Painting: Depicts a female practitioner of yoga leading a disciplined spiritual life. Features a vertical composition, standing figure holding a myna bird, long swirling scarves, and exquisite flora.
Golconda School of Painting

Socio-Economic Context & Artistic Style

  • Economic Prosperity: Independent state in 1512; wealthiest Deccani kingdom by the late 16th century due to trade in iron, cotton, and diamonds.
  • Global Reach: Dutch merchants exported portraits of sultans to Europe. Large-scale paintings (up to 8 feet high) were produced as wall hangings (1635–1650).
  • Diwan of Hafiz (1463/1590): Earliest miniatures showing court scenes of a young ruler holding a long straight Deccani sword, wearing a white coat with embroidered vertical bands. Features lavish gold, deep azure skies, and dancing girls with no Mughal influence. Notable for purple hues and blue foxes.
  • Muhammad Qutb Shah Portrait: Depicts the ruler (1611–1626) on a diwan wearing typical Golconda dress and a tight-fitting cap, showing high composition symmetry and sophistication.
  • Sufi Poem Manuscript: Richly illustrated with over 20 miniatures. Features gold and blue skies in separate bands, Deccani-type trees with tinted edges, and plants silhouetted against dark foliage.
