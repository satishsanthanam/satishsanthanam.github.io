Fine Arts: Class 12 — Chapter 5: The Pahari Schools of Painting

Introduction and Stylistic Classification of Pahari Painting

The term Pahari denotes 'hilly or mountainous' in origin, representing schools of painting that flourished in the western Himalayas from the seventeenth to the nineteenth century. These schools emerged across various hill states, including Basohli, Guler, Kangra, Kullu, Chamba, Mankot, Nurpur, Mandi, Bilaspur, and Jammu.

The Family of Artists Theory

  • B. N. Goswamy, a pioneering art historian, shifted the classification of Pahari paintings from fluid geographical/political regions to the lineage of artist families.
  • His central argument attributes the evolution of Pahari painting to the family of Pandit Seu (Shiv), whose sons Manak (Manaku) and Nainsukh revolutionized the style.
  • This framework explains how multiple stylistic strands could coexist within the same region, as artists traveled and worked under different patrons while retaining their family style.
  • The style transitioned from the bold, intense simplicity of the Basohli idiom in the early eighteenth century, through a transitional pre-Kangra or Guler-Kangra phase, to the highly refined and naturalistic Kangra style by the late eighteenth century.
The Basohli School

The earliest and most dramatic school of Pahari painting, Basohli, developed a magnificent and highly individualistic style under the patronage of the enlightened prince Raja Kirpal Pal (reigned 1678–1695).

Key Stylistic Features

  • Vibrant Palette: Strong use of primary colors, particularly warm yellows, filling the background and horizon.
  • Beetle-Wing Casings: The unique application of small, shiny green particles of beetle wings to delineate jewelry and simulate the lustrous effect of emeralds.
  • Textural Details: Use of raised white paint to imitate the texture of pearls in ornaments.
  • Aesthetic Affinities: Shares the bold, expressive aesthetics of the Western Indian Chaurpanchashika group of paintings.

Major Themes and Manuscripts

  • Rasamanjari: Composed by Bhanu Datta, this was the most popular theme. In 1694–95, the carpenter-painter (tarkhan) Devida executed a magnificent series for Kirpal Pal.
  • Shangri Ramayana: A famous set painted in the Kullu Valley (last quarter of the seventeenth century), named after 'Shangri', the residence of a branch of the Kullu royal family. It shows varying degrees of influence from Basohli and Bilaspur styles.
  • Other popular themes included the Bhagvata Purana, Ragamala, and portraits of local kings, courtiers, and mendicants.
The Guler School

The first quarter of the eighteenth century witnessed a transformation of the bold Basohli style into the refined Guler-Kangra phase, initiated under the patronage of Raja Govardhan Chand (1744–1773) of Guler.

The Legacy of Pandit Seu's Sons

  • Manak (Manaku) painted a celebrated set of the Gita Govinda in 1730 at Guler, which retained some Basohli elements like beetle-wing casings but introduced greater refinement.
  • Nainsukh left Guler to work for Mian Zoravar Singh and his successor Raja Balwant Singh of Jasrota. His genius lay in intimate, highly individualized portraiture, capturing his patron in daily activities (performing puja, sitting in a camp, smoking a hukka).
  • The Guler style is characterized by delicate pastel shades, daring expanses of white or grey, and a soft, poetic naturalism.
The Kangra School

The Kangra School represents the zenith of Pahari painting, blossoming under the long patronage of Raja Sansar Chand (1775–1823).

Stylistic Characteristics

  • Lyrical Lineage: Marked by extreme delicacy of line, brilliance of color, and minute decorative details.
  • Idealized Female Form: Delineation of the female face with a straight nose in line with the forehead, which became highly fashionable from the 1790s onwards.
  • Naturalism: Exquisite rendering of landscape, vegetation, and atmospheric effects, reflecting a deep observation of the Himalayan environment.

Themes and Master Painters

  • Popular Texts: Bhagvata Purana, Gita Govinda, Nala Damayanti, Bihari Satsai, Ragamala, and Baramasa (illustrating the twelve months of the year based on Keshav Das's Kavipriya).
  • Court Records: Numerous paintings document Sansar Chand's court life, showing him listening to music, watching dancers, or practicing archery.
  • Key Artists: Fattu, Purkhu, and Khushala.

Detailed Narrative Analyses

Awaiting Krishna and the Hesitant Radha

This painting belongs to the celebrated Gita Govinda series by Manaku, representing the transitional Guler-Kangra phase. It depicts Radha feeling shy and hesitant as she approaches the forested bower where Krishna eagerly awaits her.

“Radha! Sakhis have come to know the secret that your soul is intent on the warfare of love. Now, abandon your shyness, let your girdle tinkle merrily and go ahead to meet your beloved...”

Analysis / Historical Value: This work beautifully visualizes Jayadeva's poetry, translating spiritual devotion into human love. The composition contrasts the dark, dense forest (representing the mystery of union) with the bright, expectant figure of Krishna, capturing the emotional tension of the narrative.

Balwant Singh Looking at a Painting with Nainsukh

A rare and intimate painting depicting Raja Balwant Singh of Jasrota closely examining a miniature painting of Krishna, while the artist Nainsukh stands respectfully behind him, bowing slightly.

  • Composition: Set in a quiet, open palace terrace overlooking a green landscape, evoking a sense of peace and tranquility.
  • Details: The king is shown smoking a hukka, relaxed during a break from his duties. Musicians are placed softly at the outer edge of the frame, suggesting quiet, non-intrusive music.
  • Significance: This is one of the few historical paintings where an artist depicts himself alongside his royal patron, highlighting the close, respectful relationship between Nainsukh and Balwant Singh.

Nanda, Yashoda and Krishna

This masterpiece from the Bhagvata Purana series illustrates Nanda and his family traveling from Gokul to Vrindavan to escape constant demonic attacks.

  • Naturalism: Features a highly detailed, naturalistic landscape with a flush-cut composition that resembles a modern photograph.
  • Human Expressions: Captures subtle human emotions—the fatigue of carrying heavy loads, the tilt of heads during conversation, and the protective gestures of the mothers, Yashoda and Rohini, seated in the bullock cart with Krishna and Balaram.
