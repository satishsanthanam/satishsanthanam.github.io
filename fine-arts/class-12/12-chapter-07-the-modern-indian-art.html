Fine Arts: Class 12 — Chapter 7: The Modern Indian Art

Introduction to Modernism in India

The development of modern art in India emerged as a complex negotiation between colonial academic training, nationalist reclamation, and international avant-garde movements.

Colonial Art Education and Academic Realism

  • Colonial Bias: British administrators viewed fine arts as an exclusively European domain, asserting that Indians lacked the training and aesthetic sensibility to create or appreciate fine art.
  • Establishment of Art Schools: By the mid-to-late 19th century, colonial art schools were established in major urban centers including Lahore, Calcutta (Kolkata), Bombay (Mumbai), and Madras (Chennai).
  • Victorian Taste: These institutions promoted academic naturalism, traditional Indian crafts modified for European markets, and Victorian aesthetic standards.

The Nationalist Response and the Bengal School

  • Bengal School of Art: Led by Abanindranath Tagore and E. B. Havell, this movement rejected colonial academic realism in favor of indigenous techniques, drawing inspiration from Mughal miniatures, Pahari paintings, and Ajanta frescoes.
  • Kala Bhavana: Founded in 1919 as part of Visva-Bharati University in Shantiniketan by poet Rabindranath Tagore, it became India's first nationalist art school, fostering an art style deeply connected to Indian life and nature.

Early Pioneers of Modernism

  • Gaganendranath Tagore: Adapted the language of Cubism to create a highly personal style, using vertical, horizontal, and diagonal lines to construct mysterious, light-filled interiors (e.g., A Cubist City, 1925), distinct from Picasso's geometric facets.
  • Rabindranath Tagore: Turned to visual art late in life, developing a unique calligraphic style from crossed-out words and doodles (e.g., Doodle, 1920) with a limited palette of black, yellow ochre, reds, and browns.
  • Jamini Roy: Rejected his academic training from the Government School of Art, Calcutta, to embrace the bold forms and pure colors of Bengal's rural folk art (e.g., Black Horse, 1940), making his own pigments from local minerals and vegetables.
  • Amrita Sher-Gil: Half-Hungarian and half-Indian, trained in Paris, she assimilated European modernism (Impressionism and Post-Impressionism) with Indian miniature and mural traditions, focusing on rural Indian subjects (e.g., Haldi Grinder, 1940).
Modern Ideologies and Political Art in India

The mid-20th century, marked by World War II, the Bengal Famine, and the struggle for Independence, catalyzed politically charged and socially conscious art movements.

The Calcutta Group (1943)

  • Foundation: Formed under the leadership of sculptor Prodosh Das Gupta, alongside artists like Nirode Mazumdar, Paritosh Sen, Gopal Ghose, and Rathin Moitra.
  • Ideology: Rejected the sentimentality of the Bengal School, seeking a universal art that spoke directly to contemporary social realities and crises.
  • Socialist Influence: Many artists turned to Marxism to address class differences and poverty. Chittoprasad and Somnath Hore utilized printmaking (etchings, linocuts, lithographs) for mass reproduction to depict the horrors of the 1943 Bengal Famine (e.g., Chittoprasad's Hungry Bengal).

The Progressive Artists' Group of Bombay (1946)

  • Foundation: Established in Bombay by young artists seeking absolute creative freedom. Led by the outspoken Francis Newton Souza, the group included M. F. Husain, K. H. Ara, S. A. Bakre, H. A. Gade, and S. H. Raza.
  • F. N. Souza: Challenged traditional notions of beauty and morality, often painting exaggerated, expressive nudes.
  • M. F. Husain: Combined Western expressionist brushwork with vibrant Indian colors, drawing from Indian mythology, folk toys, and miniature traditions (e.g., Mother Teresa series).
  • S. H. Raza: Transitioned from landscapes to pure abstraction, incorporating traditional Indian cosmological symbols like the mandala, yantra, and bindu (e.g., Ma, 1972).
Post-Independence Trends: Abstraction and Indigenous Roots

During the 1960s and 1970s, Indian artists grappled with the tension between Western internationalism and indigenous artistic traditions.

The Rise of Abstraction

  • Pioneers: Artists like Gaitonde, K. K. Hebbar, S. Chavda, Akbar Padamsee, Tyeb Mehta, and Krishen Khanna moved fluidly between figurative and abstract modes.
  • K. C. S. Paniker: Established Cholamandalam, an artists' village near Madras. He pioneered an abstract style incorporating Tamil and Sanskrit scripts, traditional diagrams, and rural crafts (e.g., The Dog, 1973).

Neo-Tantric Art

  • Concept: An Indianized form of abstraction utilizing geometric designs from traditional meditation diagrams (yantras).
  • Key Artists: Biren De experimented with vibrant colors and patterns, while G. R. Santosh created symmetrical compositions representing the cosmic union of male and female energies (purusha and prakriti).

Group 1890 (1963)

  • Foundation: Formed under the leadership of J. Swaminathan, including artists like Gulam Mohammed Sheikh, Jyoti Bhatt, Ambadas, Jeram Patel, Raghav Kaneria, and Himmat Shah.
  • Manifesto: Rejected established ideologies, advocating for a fresh engagement with materials, textures, and surfaces.
The New Figurative Art and Contemporary Media (1980s–1990s)

The late 20th century witnessed a return to narrative figuration, followed by a dramatic shift toward new media, installation, and digital art post-liberalization.

The Baroda School and Narrative Figuration

  • Baroda Art School: Established in the late 1950s, it became a hub for narrative painting in the 1980s, combining autobiography, fantasy, and art history.
  • Gulam Mohammed Sheikh: Painted complex, multi-layered urban scenes combining local realities with historical European and Persian styles (e.g., City for Sale, 1984).
  • K. G. Subramanyan: Scholar and artist who worked across murals, sand casting, and painting on acrylic sheets (e.g., Fairy Tales from Purvapalli, 1986), bridging the gap between high art and artisanal craft.
  • Bhupen Khakhar: Painted everyday urban characters (barbers, watch repairers) and openly explored queer themes and middle-class morality (e.g., Janata Watch Repairing, 1972).

New Media and Installation Art (from 1990s)

  • Impact of Globalisation: Economic liberalization in the 1990s led artists to move away from traditional easel painting toward immersive, technology-driven mediums.
  • Installation Art: Combined painting, sculpture, photography, and video in a single space. Key pioneers include Nalini Malani (Mumbai) and Vivan Sundaram (Delhi).
  • Photorealism: Artists like Atul Dodiya, T. V. Santosh, and Shibu Natesan used oil or acrylic to paint in the style of photographs or television screens to comment on contemporary social and political issues.
  • Documentary Photography: Sheba Chachi, Ravi Agarwal, and Atul Bhalla used photography to document marginalized communities and ecological crises.
