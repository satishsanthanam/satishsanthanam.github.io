Fine Arts: Class 12 — Chapter 8: The Living Art Traditions of India

Introduction to Living Art Traditions

India possesses a timeless, indigenous tradition of art forms practiced by communities living far from urban centers in forests, deserts, mountains, and villages. These living traditions, passed down through generations, represent a repository of organic knowledge, aesthetic instinct, and functional utility.

  • Terminology: Variously classified by scholars as minor arts, utility art, folk art, tribal art, people's art, ritual art, and crafts.
  • Historical Continuity: Traced back to prehistoric cave paintings, Indus Valley pottery, terracotta, bronze, and ivory works.
  • Socio-Economic Role: Created to fulfill daily utilitarian needs, decorate domestic spaces, perform rituals, and supply local markets.
  • Aesthetic Character: Defined by an instinctive sense of design, symbolic motifs, local materials, and organic color-making methods.
Painting Traditions of India

Regional painting traditions across India serve as visual narratives of community cosmographies, religious beliefs, and domestic rituals. These are executed on walls, floors, paper, palm leaves, and textiles.

Mithila Painting (Madhubani)

  • Origin & Region: Based in the Mithila region (ancient Videha, birthplace of Sita) in Bihar. Traditionally practiced by women on mud walls of houses for ceremonial occasions, particularly weddings.
  • Domestic Sacred Spaces: Painted in three primary areas:
    • Outer/Central Courtyards: Features armed deities, animals, and women performing daily chores.
    • Inner Verandah (Devasthana/Gosain Ghar): Houses the family shrine where griha devatas (household gods) and kula devatas (clan deities) are painted.
    • Kohbar Ghar (Inner Room): Features the kohbar motif—a highly symbolic lotus in full bloom with a stalk, carrying tantric and metaphoric connotations of fertility and union.
  • Stylistic Features: Characterized by vibrant colors and a complete absence of empty space. Backgrounds are filled with birds, flowers, animals, fish, snakes, and celestial bodies.
  • Materials & Tools: Painted using bamboo twigs wrapped with cotton swabs, rice straw, or fiber. Pigments are derived from organic and mineral sources like phalsa, kusum flowers, bilwa leaves, kajal (lampblack), and turmeric.

Warli Painting

  • Community & Geography: Practiced by the Warli tribe inhabiting the north Sahyadri range in the Thane district of Maharashtra.
  • The Chowk Ritual: Married women paint the sacred Chowk to mark marriages, harvests, and sowing seasons. The central figure is the mother goddess Palaghat (goddess of fertility/corn goddess Kansari).
  • Iconography:
    • Hariyali Deva: Represented by pointed chevrons along the outer frame, symbolizing the God of Plants.
    • Panch Sirya Devata: A headless warrior riding a horse or standing with five shoots of corn springing from his neck, acting as the guardian of fields (Khetrapal).
    • Secular Scenes: Surrounding motifs depict hunting, fishing, farming, dancing, and modern elements like buses and urban life in Mumbai.
  • Technique: Painted with rice flour paste on earth-colored mud walls using a chewed bamboo stick as a brush.

Gond and Pithoro Painting

  • Gond Painting: Practiced by the Gonds of Mandla (Madhya Pradesh). Features geometric and organic drawings on hut walls depicting nature, animals, and Hindu themes like Krishna with cows surrounded by gopis.
  • Pithoro Painting: Large wall paintings created by the Rathva Bhils of Panchmahal (Gujarat) and Jhabua (Madhya Pradesh) for thanksgiving ceremonies. Represents the cosmography of the Rathvas, divided by an ornate wavy line into the world of gods (upper section with horse riders) and the world of earth (lower section with wedding processions, farmers, and animals).

Pata Painting (Bengal and Puri)

  • Bengal Patas: Scroll paintings on cloth (pata) used as visual aids by itinerant performers called patuas or chitrakars (from Midnapore, Birbhum, and Bankura). They travel villages, unrolling scrolls while singing narrative stories.
  • Puri Patachitras: Centered around the temple city of Puri, Odisha. Painted on cotton cloth prepared with soft white stone powder and tamarind seed glue. Finished with a protective layer of lacquer over charcoal fire.
  • Puri Iconographic Themes:
    • Veshas: Daily and festival attires of Jagannath, Balabhadra, and Subhadra (e.g., Bada Shringar Vesha, Raghunath Vesha).
    • Ansara Patti: Temporary paintings that substitute the main temple icons when they are removed for cleaning after Snanayatra.
    • Jatri Patti: Souvenir paintings purchased by pilgrims for home shrines.
  • Palm Leaf Manuscripts: Illustrated on Khar-taad palm leaves by incising lines with a steel stylus and filling them with ink or paint.

Phads of Rajasthan

  • Context: Long, horizontal cloth scrolls painted to honor the deified cattle heroes (bhomia) of pastoral communities around Bhilwara, Rajasthan.
  • Deities: Celebrates heroic figures like Gogaji, Jejaji, Dev Narayan, Ramdevji, and Pabhuji who died protecting community livestock.
  • Performance (Phad Banchan): Carried by bhopas (itinerant bards) who sing and narrate the tales at night, accompanied by instruments like the ravanahattha and veena in the Khyal style.
  • Artists: Painted by the traditional Joshis caste, who historically served as court miniature painters in Rajasthan.

Sculptural Traditions of India

Folk sculptural traditions utilize locally available materials like clay, bronze, and stone to create votive offerings, ritual icons, and utilitarian objects.

Dhokra Casting (Lost Wax Technique)

  • Geography: Prominent in Bastar (Chhattisgarh), Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and Midnapore (West Bengal).
  • The Ghadwas: The metal craftsmen of Bastar, whose name ghadwa means "to shape and create."
  • The Lost Wax (Cire Perdue) Process:
    1. A core mould is prepared using riverbank black soil mixed with rice husk and water.
    2. The dried clay core is covered with a layer of cow dung and clay.
    3. Natural resin from the saal tree is melted with mustard oil, strained, and stretched into fine threads or coils.
    4. The clay core is wrapped with these resin threads to build up the decorative details (eyes, nose, ornaments).
    5. The model is covered with layers of fine clay, cow dung, and ant-hill clay mixed with rice husk.
    6. A clay receptacle containing scrap metal pieces is attached to the bottom and sealed.
    7. The assembly is fired in a furnace using saal wood. The metal melts while the resin evaporates, leaving a cavity.
    8. The molten metal is poured into the cavity. Once cooled, the outer clay shell is hammered away to reveal the bronze sculpture.

Terracotta Traditions

  • Ubiquity: The most widespread sculptural medium in India, practiced by local potters across regions like Manipur, Assam, Kuchchha (Gujarat), Tamil Nadu, and the Gangetic plains.
  • Production: Made from local riverbank clay, shaped by hand, mould, or potter's wheel, and baked in kilns for durability.
  • Functions: Used as votive offerings to local deities, ritual objects during festivals, and toys. Common forms include horses, elephants, and deities like Ganesh and Durga.
