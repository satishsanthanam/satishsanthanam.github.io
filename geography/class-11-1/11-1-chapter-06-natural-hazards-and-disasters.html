Geography: Class 11 (Part 1) — Chapter 6: Natural Hazards and Disasters

Introduction to Hazards and Disasters

Change is a continuous, universal law of nature, varying in magnitude, intensity, and scale. While natural changes are value-neutral, human perspectives classify them as desirable or undesirable. Undesirable, rapid changes outside human control are classified as hazards or disasters.

Defining Hazards vs. Disasters

Though often used interchangeably, Natural Hazards and Natural Disasters are distinct concepts:

  • Natural Hazards: Elements of circumstances in the natural environment that have the potential to cause harm to people or property (e.g., steep slopes, ocean currents, extreme climates).
  • Natural Disasters: Relatively sudden, large-scale events causing widespread death, loss of property, and social disruption over which people have little or no control. An event becomes a disaster only when the magnitude of destruction is very high.

Earthquakes

Earthquakes are highly destructive and unpredictable tectonic disasters resulting from a sudden release of energy in the Earth's crust. In India, tectonic earthquakes are highly devastating due to the constant northward movement of the Indian plate at a rate of $1\text{ cm/year}$, colliding with the Eurasian plate.

Earthquake Vulnerability Zones in India

The National Institute of Disaster Management and other bodies classify India into five earthquake zones:

  1. Very High Damage Risk Zone: Includes North-east states, areas north of Darbhanga and Araria (Bihar), Uttarakhand, Western Himachal Pradesh, Kashmir Valley, and Kuchchh (Gujarat).
  2. High Damage Risk Zone: Remaining parts of Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, Northern Punjab, Eastern Haryana, Delhi, Western Uttar Pradesh, and Northern Bihar.
  3. Moderate to Low Damage Risk Zones: Most remaining parts of the country, with the stable Deccan Plateau being the safest.

Mitigation Strategies

  • Establishing seismological monitoring centres for real-time data dissemination using GPS.
  • Preparing vulnerability maps and educating communities.
  • Modifying house designs and discouraging high-rise constructions in high-risk zones.
  • Enforcing earthquake-resistant building codes.
Tsunamis

A Tsunami (harbour wave) is a series of extremely long-wavelength ocean waves generated by the rapid displacement of water, usually caused by submarine earthquakes or volcanic eruptions.

Wave Dynamics and Impact

In deep water, tsunamis have long wavelengths and low wave heights, making them difficult to detect. As they enter shallow coastal waters, their speed decreases, causing the wave energy to compress and the wave height to rise dramatically (up to $15\text{ m}$ or more), resulting in catastrophic coastal destruction.

Mitigation

Due to the scale of damage, individual state mitigation is difficult. India has joined the International Tsunami Warning System following the devastating Indian Ocean Tsunami of December 26, 2004.

Tropical Cyclones

Tropical Cyclones are intense low-pressure areas confined to latitudes between $30^\circ\text{ N}$ and $30^\circ\text{ S}$. They act as thermal engines powered by the release of latent heat of condensation.

Conditions for Formation

  • Large and continuous supply of warm, moist air (sea surface temperature $> 27^\circ\text{ C}$).
  • Strong Coriolis Force to create a rotary system (absent near the equator, $0^\circ\text{ to }5^\circ$).
  • Unstable conditions in the troposphere.
  • Absence of strong vertical wind shear.

Spatio-temporal Distribution and Consequences

In India, cyclones originate in both the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea. Bay of Bengal cyclones are more frequent and intense, particularly during October and November. The primary hazard is the Storm Surge—an abnormal rise in sea level that inundates low-lying coastal settlements and agricultural fields.

Floods

Floods occur when water in a channel exceeds its carrying capacity, overflowing into adjacent floodplains. Unlike other disasters, floods are relatively slow to rise and occur in well-identified regions.

Causes and Distribution

Floods are caused by heavy rainfall, storm surges, melting snow, or human activities like deforestation and unscientific land use. The Rashtriya Barh Ayog has identified $40\text{ million hectares}$ of land as flood-prone in India, with Assam, Bihar, and West Bengal being the worst affected.

Consequences and Control

Floods destroy crops, damage infrastructure, and spread waterborne diseases like cholera and hepatitis. However, they also deposit fertile silt. Control measures include constructing embankments, dams, afforestation, and removing human encroachments from river channels.

Droughts

A Drought is an extended period of water shortage caused by inadequate precipitation, high evaporation, and over-utilization of water resources.

Types of Droughts

  • Meteorological Drought: Prolonged period of inadequate and poorly distributed rainfall.
  • Agricultural Drought: Low soil moisture that fails to support crop growth, leading to crop failure.
  • Hydrological Drought: Depletion of water levels in storage reservoirs, aquifers, and lakes below replenishment levels.
  • Ecological Drought: Productivity collapse of a natural ecosystem due to water scarcity.

Drought Prone Areas in India

  • Extreme Drought Affected Areas: Marusthali (Rajasthan) and Kuchchh (Gujarat), receiving less than $90\text{ mm}$ annual rainfall.
  • Severe Drought Prone Areas: Eastern Rajasthan, parts of Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, interior Karnataka, southern Jharkhand, and interior Odisha.
  • Moderate Drought Prone Areas: Northern Rajasthan, Haryana, southern Uttar Pradesh, and remaining parts of Gujarat and Maharashtra.

Mitigation

Mitigation involves distributing safe drinking water and fodder, inter-linking rivers, constructing reservoirs, rainwater harvesting, and promoting drought-resistant crops.

Landslides

A Landslide is the rapid downward movement of a large mass of rock, debris, or earth under the influence of gravity. They are highly localized and influenced by geology, slope, and human activities.

Vulnerability Zones

  • Very High Vulnerability Zone: Unstable, young mountainous areas of the Himalayas, Andaman and Nicobar, steep slopes of the Western Ghats, and Nilgiris.
  • High Vulnerability Zone: All Himalayan states and the North-eastern region (excluding the plains of Assam).
  • Moderate to Low Vulnerability Zone: Trans-Himalayan areas (Ladakh, Spiti), Aravalis, and rain-shadow areas of the Deccan Plateau.

Mitigation

Mitigation includes restricting construction on fragile slopes, promoting terrace farming, and implementing large-scale afforestation programmes.

Disaster Management

Disaster management involves structured planning across three distinct stages:

  1. Pre-Disaster Management: Generating data, preparing vulnerability maps, planning, and spreading community awareness.
  2. During Disaster Management: Rescue and relief operations, evacuation, setting up relief camps, and providing medical aid.
  3. Post-Disaster Management: Rehabilitation, recovery, and capacity building for future resilience.

The Government of India formalized these steps through the Disaster Management Bill, 2005 and the establishment of the National Institute of Disaster Management.
