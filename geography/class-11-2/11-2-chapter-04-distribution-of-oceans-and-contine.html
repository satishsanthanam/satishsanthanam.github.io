Class 11 (Part 2) — Chapter 4: Distribution of Oceans and Continents

Continental Drift Theory

Wegener's Hypothesis

The Continental Drift Theory was comprehensively put forward by German meteorologist Alfred Wegener in 1912. He proposed that all continents once existed as a single supercontinent called Pangaea (meaning "all earth"), surrounded by a massive mega-ocean called Panthalassa (meaning "all water").

About 200 million years ago, Pangaea began to split into two large landmasses:

  • Laurasia: The northern continental mass.
  • Gondwanaland: The southern continental mass.

These two masses subsequently broke down into the smaller, distinct continents we recognize today.

Evidence Supporting Continental Drift

Several lines of scientific evidence support the historical connection of the continents:

  • The Jig-Saw-Fit of Continents: The shorelines of Africa and South America facing each other show a remarkable, unmistakable match. In 1964, Bullard used a computer program to map this fit at the 1,000-fathom line, proving a near-perfect match.
  • Rocks of Same Age Across Oceans: Radiometric dating correlates ancient rock belts. For example, a 2,000-million-year-old belt of ancient rocks from the Brazilian coast matches rocks found in Western Africa.
  • Tillite: Glacial sedimentary rocks known as Tillite found in the Gondwana system of India have matching counterparts in Africa, the Falkland Islands, Madagascar, Antarctica, and Australia, indicating a shared paleoclimate and glacial history.
  • Placer Deposits: Rich gold placer deposits occur along the Ghana coast without any local source rock. The source veins lie directly across the ocean in Brazil, proving the landmasses were once joined.
  • Distribution of Fossils: Identical species of plants and animals are found on widely separated landmasses. For example, fossils of the small freshwater reptile Mesosaurus are found only in Southern Africa and Eastern Brazil, which are currently separated by $4,800\text{ km}$ of ocean.

Forces Proposed for Drift

Wegener suggested two primary forces to explain the movement of continents, though both were later deemed inadequate by contemporary physicists:

  1. Pole-fleeing force: Related to the rotation of the Earth and its equatorial bulge.
  2. Tidal force: Driven by the gravitational attraction of the Sun and Moon, creating tidal friction over millions of years.
Post-Drift Studies & Ocean Floor Configuration

Convectional Current Theory

In the 1930s, Arthur Holmes proposed the Convectional Current Theory. He argued that thermal differences generated by radioactive decay within the Earth's mantle create convective cells. This system of currents provided the physical mechanism for crustal movement that Wegener's theory lacked.

Ocean Floor Configuration

Post-WWII ocean mapping revealed that the ocean floor is not a flat plain but contains complex relief features. It is divided into three major provinces:

  • Continental Margins: The transition zone between continental shores and deep-sea basins, including the continental shelf, slope, rise, and deep-oceanic trenches.
  • Abyssal Plains: Extensive, flat plains where continental sediments settling beyond the margins are deposited.
  • Mid-Oceanic Ridges: An interconnected chain of volcanic mountain systems running through the world's oceans, characterized by a central rift system at the crest and intense volcanic activity.

Distribution of Earthquakes and Volcanoes

Seismic and volcanic activities are highly concentrated along specific zones. Earthquakes along the mid-oceanic ridges occur at shallow depths, whereas those along the Alpine-Himalayan belt and the rim of the Pacific Ocean are deep-seated. The rim of the Pacific Ocean is known as the Ring of Fire due to its high concentration of active volcanoes.

Sea Floor Spreading

The Hypothesis of Harry Hess

In 1961, Harry Hess proposed the concept of Sea Floor Spreading. He argued that constant volcanic eruptions along the crests of mid-oceanic ridges release lava that cools to form new oceanic crust, pushing the older crust symmetrically away on either side.

Key evidence supporting this hypothesis includes:

  • Rocks equidistant from the ridge crest show identical age, chemical composition, and magnetic polarity.
  • Oceanic crust rocks are significantly younger than continental rocks; none are older than 200 million years, whereas continental rocks can be up to 3,200 million years old.
  • Ocean floor sediments are unexpectedly thin, indicating the ocean basins are geologically young.
  • Oceanic crust pushed away from the ridges eventually sinks and is consumed at deep-oceanic trenches (subduction zones).
Plate Tectonics

Theory of Lithospheric Plates

In 1967, McKenzie, Parker, and Morgan formulated the theory of Plate Tectonics. A tectonic plate is a massive, rigid slab of solid rock composing both continental and oceanic lithosphere. The lithosphere varies in thickness from $5\text{ to }100\text{ km}$ in oceanic areas to about $200\text{ km}$ in continental regions.

Types of Plate Boundaries

Tectonic plates interact along three distinct types of boundaries:

  • Divergent Boundaries: Where new crust is generated as plates pull apart (e.g., the Mid-Atlantic Ridge).
  • Convergent Boundaries: Where crust is destroyed as one plate subducts beneath another (e.g., subduction zones). This can occur between oceanic-continental, oceanic-oceanic, or continental-continental plates.
  • Transform Boundaries: Where crust is neither created nor destroyed as plates slide horizontally past each other along transform faults.

Rates and Forces of Plate Movement

Magnetic striping parallel to mid-oceanic ridges allows scientists to calculate plate velocities:

  • Slowest Rate: The Arctic Ridge moves at less than $2.5\text{ cm/yr}$.
  • Fastest Rate: The East Pacific Rise near Easter Island moves at more than $15\text{ cm/yr}$.

The primary driving force behind this movement is the slow, circular convective flow of hot, softened mantle material beneath the rigid lithospheric plates.

Movement of the Indian Plate

Tectonic Journey and Himalayan Orogeny

The Indian plate includes both Peninsular India and the Australian continental landmass. Its northern boundary is defined by the continent-continent convergence zone along the Himalayas.

The historical movement of the Indian plate is characterized by several key phases:

  1. Separation: Around 200 million years ago, when Pangaea broke, India began its northward journey from the southern hemisphere, separated from Asia by the ancient Tethys Sea.
  2. Deccan Traps Formation: About 60 million years ago, as India passed over a volcanic hotspot, massive outpourings of lava occurred, forming the Deccan Traps.
  3. Collision: Around 40 to 50 million years ago, India collided with the Eurasian plate, causing the rapid uplift of the Himalayas. This tectonic collision and mountain-building process continue to this day.
