Class 11 (Part 3) — Chapter 1: Introduction to Maps

Introduction & Core Concepts

A map is a simplified, selective, and generalised representation of the whole or a part of the earth's surface on a plane surface at a reduced scale. Unlike a three-dimensional globe which best represents the earth's geoid shape, a map is a two-dimensional representation constructed using map projections.

Glossary of Cartographic Terms

TermDefinition
Cadastral MapA large-scale map drawn at a scale of $1 : 500$ to $1 : 4000$ to show property boundaries and land parcels.
CartographyThe art, science, and technology of making and studying maps, charts, plans, and other graphical expressions.
Map GeneralisationA simplified representation of features on a map appropriate to its scale or purpose, without affecting their visual form.
Map ProjectionThe system of transforming the spherical or geoidal surface of the earth onto a plane surface.
ScaleThe ratio between the distance of two points on the map and the actual distance between the same two points on the ground.
Sketch MapA simplified map drawn freehand that fails to preserve true scale or orientation.
Essentials of Map Making

Cartography involves several essential processes common to all maps:

  • Scale: Determines the level of detail and reduction. All maps are reductions, and the choice of scale sets limits on information content.
  • Map Projection: The system used to transform the curved geoidal surface into a flat plane, which inevitably introduces minor distortions in direction, distance, area, or shape.
  • Map Generalisation: Selecting and simplifying relevant data to serve the map's specific objective (e.g., general purpose vs. thematic maps).
  • Map Design: Planning graphic characteristics, including symbols, size, form, lettering style, line width, colours, shades, and legend layout.
  • Map Construction and Production: The physical or digital drawing and reproduction of maps, heavily revolutionised by computer-assisted mapping and photo-printing.
History of Map Making

Global Evolution

The history of map-making is as old as mankind itself:

  • The oldest known map was found in Mesopotamia, drawn on a clay tablet dating back to 2,500 B.C.
  • Greek and Arab geographers laid the foundations of modern cartography, establishing coordinate systems and measuring the Earth's circumference. Ptolemy's Map of the World is a landmark historical work.
  • The 19th and 20th centuries introduced aerial photography, which supplemented ground surveys and advanced modern mapping.

Map Making in India

Indian cartographic traditions date back to the Vedic period:

  • Astronomical and cosmological principles were recorded as Sidhantas in classical treatises by scholars like Arya Bhatta, Varahamihira, and Bhaskara.
  • Ancient Indian scholars divided the known world into seven dwipas, and the Mahabharata conceptualised a round world surrounded by water.
  • During the medieval period, land surveying was pioneered by Todarmal and enriched by Sher Shah Suri's revenue maps.
  • The Survey of India was established in 1767, culminating in the publication of the Map of Hindustan in 1785.
Classification of Maps

Based on Scale

Maps are classified into large-scale and small-scale categories:

CategoryScale Range / ExamplesCharacteristics
Large-scale Maps$1 : 500$ to $1 : 250,000$ (e.g., Cadastral maps, Topographical maps)Show small areas in great detail; represent property boundaries, local relief, and infrastructure.
Small-scale MapsVery small scales (e.g., Wall maps, Atlas maps)Show large areas (countries, continents) with highly generalised physical or cultural features.

Based on Function

Maps are divided into physical and cultural categories based on their primary function:

  • Physical Maps: Show natural features. Includes:
    • Relief Maps: General topography, mountains, valleys, plains, and drainage.
    • Geological Maps: Geological structures and rock types.
    • Climatic Maps: Climatic regions, temperature, rainfall, and wind distribution.
    • Soil Maps: Distribution and properties of different soil types.
  • Cultural Maps: Show man-made features. Includes:
    • Political Maps: Administrative divisions (countries, states, districts).
    • Population Maps: Distribution, density, growth, and demographic composition.
    • Economic Maps: Crop production, mineral distribution, industries, markets, and trade routes.
    • Transportation Maps: Roads, railways, airports, and shipping routes.
Uses of Maps & Spatial Measurements

Maps are essential tools for geographers and planners to measure distance, direction, and area.

Measurement of Distance

Linear features are measured based on their geometry:

  • Straight Lines: Measured directly using a pair of dividers or a scale.
  • Erratic/Zigzag Lines (e.g., rivers, coastlines): Measured using a thread aligned along the feature and then stretched against a scale, or by using a mechanical instrument called a Rotameter.

Measurement of Direction

Direction is defined as an imaginary straight line showing the angular position relative to a common base direction (usually North, which is $0^ ext{o}$):

  • The four primary directions are Cardinal Points: North (N), South (S), East (E), and West (W).
  • Intermediate directions (e.g., NE, NW, SE, SW) provide more precise orientation.

Measurement of Area

Area of administrative or geographic units can be calculated using different methods:

  • Grid Square Method: A simple, manual method where a grid of squares is placed over the area. The area is calculated using the formula:
    $$ ext{Area} = ext{Sum of whole squares} + rac{ ext{Sum of partial squares}}{2} imes ext{Map Scale}$$
  • Polar Planimeter Method: A highly precise mechanical method using an instrument called a Polar Planimeter to trace the perimeter of an area.
