Geography: Class 11 (Part 3) — Chapter 3: Latitude, Longitude and Time

Introduction to the Earth's Grid

The Earth is nearly a sphere, but because its equatorial radius and polar radius are not the same, its actual shape resembles an oblate spheroid. The rotation of the earth over its axis produces a bulging at the equator. To position surface features and locate places, a network of imaginary lines is drawn on a globe or map, forming a geographical grid.

The spinning of the earth on its axis from west to east provides two natural points of reference: the North Pole and the South Pole. The grid consists of two sets of horizontal and vertical lines, called parallels of latitudes and meridians of longitudes.

Parallels of Latitudes

The latitude of a place is its angular distance north or south of the equator, measured from the centre of the earth. Lines joining places with the same latitudes are called parallels.

  • The equator represents $0^\circ$ latitude and is the largest circle, also called a great circle.
  • The poles are at $90^\circ\text{N}$ and $90^\circ\text{S}$.
  • If drawn at $1^\circ$ intervals, there are 89 parallels in each hemisphere, making a total of 179 parallels (including the equator).
  • On a perfect sphere, $1^\circ$ of latitude would equal a constant 111 km. However, due to the oblate shape, it varies slightly: 110.6 km at the equator and 111.7 km at the poles.

Drawing the Parallels of Latitudes

  1. Draw a circle and divide it into two equal halves with a horizontal line representing the equator.
  2. Place a protractor on this circle so that the $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ line coincides with the equator.
  3. To draw $20^\circ\text{S}$, mark two points at an angle of $20^\circ$ from the equator, east and west in the lower half of the circle.
  4. Join these two points with a line parallel to the equator.
Meridians of Longitude

Unlike parallels, meridians of longitude are semi-circles that converge at the poles. Opposite meridians together form a complete circle. They intersect the equator at right angles and are all equal in length.

  • The meridian passing through the Greenwich observatory near London is adopted as the Prime Meridian ($0^\circ$).
  • Longitude is the angular distance east or west of the Prime Meridian, varying from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$.
  • The Eastern Hemisphere lies east of the Prime Meridian, and the Western Hemisphere lies to its west.

Drawing the Meridians of Longitude

  1. Draw a circle whose centre represents the North Pole and circumference represents the equator.
  2. Draw a vertical line through the centre to represent the $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ meridians.
  3. To draw $45^\circ\text{E}$ and $45^\circ\text{W}$, place a protractor along the vertical line and measure $45^\circ$ on both sides.
Comparison between Latitudes and Longitudes

The following table summarizes the key differences between parallels of latitudes and meridians of longitudes:

2.All latitudes are parallel to the equator.All meridians converge at the poles.

Longitude and Time

The earth rotates from west to east, completing a $360^\circ$ rotation in 24 hours. This relationship allows us to calculate time based on longitude:

$$\text{Rate of Rotation} = \frac{360^\circ}{24 \text{ hours}} = 15^\circ \text{ per hour}$$
$$\text{Time for } 1^\circ \text{ Longitude} = \frac{60 \text{ minutes}}{15^\circ} = 4 \text{ minutes}$$

Time increases (gains) as we move east of the Prime Meridian and decreases (loses) as we move west.

Standard Time and Time Zones

To maintain uniformity of time within a country, the local time of a central meridian is adopted as the Standard Time. The selected meridian is called the Standard Meridian, which is ideally divisible by $15^\circ$ or $7^\circ30'$.

  • Indian Standard Time (IST) is calculated from the $82^\circ30'\text{E}$ meridian passing through Mirzapur, making it 5 hours and 30 minutes ahead of GMT.
  • The world is divided into 24 major time zones. Countries with vast east-west spans, like Russia, Canada, and the USA, have multiple time zones.

International Date Line

The International Date Line roughly follows the $180^\circ$ meridian. Crossing this line changes the calendar day:

  • A traveler crossing the line from east to west (e.g., towards Asia) gains a day (e.g., Tuesday becomes Wednesday).
  • A traveler crossing from west to east (e.g., towards America) loses a day (e.g., Tuesday becomes Monday).
