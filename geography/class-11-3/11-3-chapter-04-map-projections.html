Geography: Class 11 (Part 3) — Chapter 4: Map Projections

Introduction to Map Projections

A map projection is the systematic method of transferring the spherical network of latitude and longitude (the graticule) from the Earth's surface onto a flat, plane surface. Because the Earth is a geoid (nearly spherical), its three-dimensional surface cannot be flattened without stretching, tearing, or compressing. While a globe is the most accurate representation of the Earth, it has significant limitations: it is expensive, difficult to carry, and cannot show minor local details at large scales.

The Need for Map Projection

The primary need for map projections arises from the practical necessity of studying specific regions in detail on a flat sheet of paper. Since a sphere is a non-developable surface (it cannot be flattened without distortion), any attempt to project it onto a flat sheet introduces distortions in shape, area, distance, or direction. Map projections use mathematical and geometric methods to minimize and control these inevitable distortions based on the map's intended purpose.

Elements of Map Projection

To construct any map projection, several fundamental elements must be defined and calculated:

  • Reduced Earth: A simplified, scaled-down model of the Earth from which the graticule is projected. It is represented by a sphere of radius $R$, calculated using the map scale:
    $$R = \frac{\text{Actual Radius of the Earth}}{\text{Denominator of the Scale}}$$
    Where the actual radius of the Earth is approximately $640,000,000\text{ cm}$ (or $250,000,000\text{ inches}$).
  • Parallels of Latitude: Concentric circles running parallel to the Equator, ranging from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$ North and South.
  • Meridians of Longitude: Semi-circles extending from pole to pole, intersecting parallels at right angles. The Prime Meridian ($0^\circ$) passing through Greenwich serves as the standard reference.
  • Global Properties: The four basic properties of the globe that cartographers attempt to preserve are:
    1. Distance between points
    2. Shape of regions
    3. Size or area accuracy
    4. Direction/bearing between points
Classification of Map Projections

Map projections are classified based on construction techniques, the projection surface used, and the properties they preserve:

1. By Drawing Techniques
  • Perspective Projections: Developed geometrically by projecting the graticule onto a surface using a light source.
  • Non-Perspective Projections: Developed mathematically without using a physical light source.
  • Mathematical/Conventional Projections: Derived entirely through mathematical formulas to serve specific analytical needs.

2. By Developable Surface

A developable surface is a geometric shape (like a cylinder, cone, or plane) that can be flattened without distortion:

  • Cylindrical Projection: Obtained by wrapping a cylinder around the globe. Parallels and meridians appear as straight lines intersecting at right angles.
  • Conical Projection: Obtained by placing a cone over the globe, touching along a chosen parallel called the standard parallel.
  • Zenithal (Azimuthal) Projection: Obtained by projecting the graticule directly onto a flat plane touching the globe at a single point (usually one of the poles).

3. By Global Properties
  • Equal-Area (Homolographic) Projection: Areas of different regions are preserved accurately, though shapes may be distorted.
  • Orthomorphic (True-Shape) Projection: Shapes of small areas are preserved accurately. Parallels and meridians always intersect at $90^\circ$.
  • Azimuthal (True-Bearing) Projection: Directions from the center point to all other points are represented accurately.
  • Equi-distant (True-Scale) Projection: Distances are maintained accurately along specific lines (e.g., along meridians or selected parallels).

4. By Source of Light
  • Gnomonic Projection: Light source is placed at the center of the globe.
  • Stereographic Projection: Light source is placed at the point diametrically opposite to the tangent plane.
  • Orthographic Projection: Light source is placed at infinity, projecting parallel rays.
Construction of Selected Projections

1. Conical Projection with One Standard Parallel

Constructed by projecting the graticule onto a cone touching the globe along a single parallel of latitude, known as the standard parallel.

Properties:

  • Parallels are concentric circular arcs; meridians are straight lines radiating from the pole.
  • Scale is true only along the standard parallel.
  • It is neither equal-area nor orthomorphic.

Uses: Ideal for mapping mid-latitude regions with limited latitudinal but large longitudinal extents (e.g., transcontinental railways like the Canadian Pacific Railway).

2. Cylindrical Equal-Area Projection (Lambert's Projection)

Constructed by projecting the globe onto a cylinder touching the equator, using parallel rays of light.

Properties:

  • All parallels and meridians are straight lines intersecting at right angles.
  • The scale is true only along the equator.
  • Distortion increases rapidly towards the poles, flattening high-latitude landmasses.

Uses: Most suitable for showing the distribution of tropical crops (e.g., rice, tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane) in equatorial regions.

3. Mercator's Projection

A mathematical, orthomorphic cylindrical projection designed by Gerardus Mercator in 1569. The spacing between parallels increases towards the poles to match the stretching of the meridians.

Properties:

  • Parallels and meridians are straight lines intersecting at right angles.
  • Any straight line drawn on this projection represents a constant bearing, known as a Loxodrome or Rhumb Line.
  • Area is highly exaggerated near the poles (e.g., Greenland appears equal in size to South America, though it is much smaller).

Uses: Essential for marine and air navigation due to its true-bearing property.

Glossary of Key Terms
  • Lexodrome / Rhumb Line: A straight line on Mercator's projection that crosses all meridians at the same angle, representing a path of constant compass bearing.
  • Great Circle: The shortest distance between any two points on a spherical surface.
  • Homolographic Projection: An equal-area projection where the area of any region on the map is proportional to its actual area on the globe.
  • Orthomorphic Projection: A projection that preserves correct local shapes and angles.
