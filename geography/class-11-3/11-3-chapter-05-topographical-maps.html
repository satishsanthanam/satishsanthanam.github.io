Geography: Class 11 (Part 3) — Chapter 5: Topographical Maps

Introduction to Topographical Maps

Topographical maps, also known as general purpose maps, are drawn at relatively large scales to show both natural and cultural features of the Earth's surface. These include relief, vegetation, water bodies, cultivated land, settlements, and transportation networks. They serve as crucial base maps for geographers to construct other thematic maps.

National Mapping Series

Topographical maps are prepared and published by national mapping organizations. In India, the Survey of India is responsible for mapping the entire country. Indian topographical maps have historically been prepared under two main series:

  • India and Adjacent Countries Series: Prepared by the Survey of India until 1937, after which mapping of adjoining countries was abandoned.
  • The International Map Series of the World: Designed to produce standardized maps globally on specific scales.

Methods of Relief Representation

The Earth's surface features various elevations and depressions, known as relief features. Several methods have been developed to represent these features on two-dimensional maps:

  • Hachures: Small, straight lines drawn along the direction of maximum slope.
  • Hill Shading: Simulates light and shadow to give a three-dimensional appearance.
  • Layer Tints: Uses color gradients to represent different elevation zones.
  • Benchmarks and Spot Heights: Indicate precise elevations at specific points.
  • Contours: The most widely used and precise method for depicting relief.

Contours and Their Characteristics

Contours are imaginary lines joining places that share the same elevation above mean sea level. Key concepts include:

  • Contour Interval (VI): The constant vertical distance between two successive contour lines on a map.
  • Horizontal Equivalent (HE): The horizontal distance between two contour lines, which varies depending on the steepness of the slope.

Basic Features of Contour Lines:

  1. They connect points of equal height.
  2. Their spacing indicates the gradient: closely spaced contours represent steep slopes, while widely spaced contours represent gentle slopes.
  3. When contours merge, they indicate vertical features like cliffs or waterfalls.
  4. Contours of different elevations do not cross each other.
Types of Slopes and Landforms

Different landforms and slope profiles exhibit distinct contour spacing patterns:

Types of Slopes

  • Gentle Slope: Contours are spaced far apart due to a low angle of slope.
  • Steep Slope: Contours are closely spaced due to a high angle of slope.
  • Concave Slope: Contours are widely spaced at the bottom (gentle gradient) and closely spaced at the top (steep gradient).
  • Convex Slope: Contours are closely spaced at the bottom (steep gradient) and widely spaced at the top (gentle gradient).

Types of Landforms

  • Conical Hill: Represented by concentric contours spaced at regular intervals.
  • Plateau: A flat-topped highland represented by contours closely spaced at the margins and a wide gap in the center.
  • V-shaped Valley: Formed by river erosion; contours form a 'V' shape pointing towards higher ground, with values increasing outwards.
  • U-shaped Valley: Formed by glacial erosion; contours show a flat bottom with a wide gap between sides.
  • Gorge: A deep, narrow valley with very steep sides, represented by extremely close contours.
  • Spur: A projection of land into lower ground, represented by 'V' shaped contours pointing towards lower ground.
  • Cliff: A near-vertical rock face where contours run extremely close and merge into one.
  • Waterfall: A sudden descent of water where contours merge as they cross a river stream.
Cultural Features and Map Interpretation

Topographical sheets use conventional signs, symbols, and colors to represent human-made and cultural features.

Distribution of Settlements

Settlements are analyzed based on their site, pattern, and density:

  • Rural Settlements: Can be classified as Compact, Scattered, Linear (along roads, rivers, or coastlines), or Circular.
  • Urban Settlements: Classified by function and pattern, such as cross-road towns, nodal points, market centers, port towns, or hill stations.

Transport and Communication

The density and layout of transport networks (highways, railways, cart tracks, footpaths) are heavily influenced by relief and population density, depicted using standardized international symbols.

Steps for Map Interpretation

A systematic approach to interpreting topographical sheets involves analyzing:

  1. Marginal Information: Sheet number, scale, grid references, and coordinates.
  2. Relief and Drainage: Identifying landforms, slopes, and drainage patterns (e.g., dendritic, radial, trellis).
  3. Land Use and Vegetation: Forest cover (reserved, protected), agricultural land, and water bodies.
  4. Settlement and Occupation: Analyzing settlement patterns to infer primary occupations (e.g., agriculture in plains, fishing in coastal areas).
