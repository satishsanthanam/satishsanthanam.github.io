Class 112 — Chapter 8: Solar Radiation, Heat Balance and Temperature

Solar Radiation and Insolation

The earth receives almost all of its energy from the sun. This energy is radiated back to space, ensuring the earth neither warms up nor cools down over time. However, the distribution of this heat varies across the globe, causing pressure differences and atmospheric circulation.

Insolation and Orbital Variations

The incoming solar radiation received by the earth is known as insolation. The earth receives an average of 1.94 calories per square centimetre per minute at the top of its atmosphere. The solar output received varies slightly throughout the year due to the earth's elliptical orbit:

  • Aphelion: On 4th July, the earth is farthest from the sun (152 million km).
  • Perihelion: On 3rd January, the earth is nearest to the sun (147 million km).

Consequently, the insolation received on 3rd January is slightly higher than on 4th July, though its effects are masked by atmospheric circulation and land-sea distribution.

Factors Controlling Insolation

The variability of insolation at the earth's surface depends on several factors:

  1. The rotation of the earth on its axis.
  2. The angle of inclination of the sun's rays (determined by latitude).
  3. The length of the day.
  4. The transparency of the atmosphere.
  5. The configuration of land in terms of its aspect.

The earth's axis makes an angle of 66½° with the plane of its orbit, which heavily influences the insolation received at different latitudes. Slanted rays cover a larger area, reducing the net energy received per unit area, and must pass through a greater depth of the atmosphere, leading to more absorption and scattering.

Atmospheric Passage and Spatial Distribution

Passage of Solar Radiation

The atmosphere is largely transparent to short-wave solar radiation. Within the troposphere, gases like water vapour and ozone absorb much of the near-infrared radiation. Small suspended particles scatter visible light, which gives the sky its blue colour and causes the red hues of sunrise and sunset.

Spatial Distribution of Insolation

Insolation varies from about 320 Watt/m² in the tropics to about 70 Watt/m² at the poles. The maximum insolation is received over subtropical deserts where cloudiness is least. The equator receives less insolation than the tropics due to cloud cover. At any given latitude, continents receive more insolation than oceans.

Heating and Cooling of the Atmosphere

Mechanisms of Heat Transfer

The atmosphere is heated and cooled through three primary processes:

  • Conduction: The transfer of heat through direct contact between two bodies of unequal temperature. It is important in heating the lower layers of the atmosphere in contact with the earth's surface.
  • Convection: The vertical transmission of heat through convective currents. This process is confined to the troposphere.
  • Advection: The horizontal movement of air. In middle latitudes, advection causes most diurnal weather variations. In northern India, the local summer wind called loo is a product of advection.

Terrestrial Radiation

The earth's surface, heated by short-wave insolation, becomes a radiating body and emits energy in the form of long waves. This terrestrial radiation heats the atmosphere from below. Greenhouse gases, particularly carbon dioxide, absorb this long-wave radiation, indirectly heating the atmosphere.

Heat Budget of the Planet Earth

The earth maintains a constant temperature because the amount of heat received as insolation equals the amount lost through terrestrial radiation.

Latitudinal Heat Balance

There are latitudinal variations in the net radiation balance. A surplus of net radiation exists between 40° N and 40° S, while the polar regions experience a deficit. Heat is redistributed from the surplus tropical regions to the deficit polar regions by winds and ocean currents, preventing the tropics from overheating and the poles from freezing completely.

Temperature and its Distribution

Temperature is the measurement of the hotness or coldness of a place, resulting from the interaction of insolation with the atmosphere and the earth's surface.

Factors Controlling Temperature

  1. Latitude: Temperature decreases from the equator to the poles due to varying angles of insolation.
  2. Altitude: The atmosphere is heated from below, so temperature decreases with height. The rate of decrease is the normal lapse rate:
    $$\text{Normal Lapse Rate} = 6.5^\circ\text{C} \text{ per } 1,000\text{ m}$$
  3. Distance from the Sea: Land heats and cools faster than water. Coastal areas experience moderating sea and land breezes.
  4. Air-mass and Ocean Currents: Warm ocean currents and warm air masses raise temperatures, while cold currents and cold air masses lower them.

Global Distribution of Temperature

Temperature distribution is mapped using isotherms (lines joining places of equal temperature). In January, isotherms bend northwards over oceans and southwards over continents in the Northern Hemisphere due to land-sea contrasts. In the Southern Hemisphere, isotherms run more parallel to latitudes due to the dominance of oceans.

Inversion of Temperature

Under certain conditions, the normal lapse rate is reversed, and temperature increases with altitude. This is known as inversion of temperature.

Conditions and Effects

A long winter night with clear skies and still air is ideal for temperature inversion. The ground radiates heat rapidly, becoming cooler than the air above. This promotes stability in the lower atmosphere, trapping smoke and dust particles to form dense morning fogs.

Air Drainage Inversion

In hills and mountains, cold air produced at night flows downslope under gravity. This heavy, dense cold air piles up in valley bottoms, leaving warmer air above. This process, called air drainage, protects valley plants from frost damage.
