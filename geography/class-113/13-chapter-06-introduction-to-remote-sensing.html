Class 113 — Chapter 6: Introduction To Remote Sensing

Introduction to Remote Sensing

The term Remote Sensing was first used in the early 1960s. It is defined as the total processes used to acquire and measure information of some property of objects and phenomena by a recording device (sensor) that is not in physical contact with the objects and phenomena under study. It primarily involves an object surface, a recording device, and information-carrying energy waves.

Glossary of Key Terms

  • Absorptance: The ratio of the radiant energy absorbed by a substance to the energy it receives.
  • Band: The specific wavelength interval in the electromagnetic spectrum.
  • Digital Image: An array of digital numbers (DN) arranged in rows and columns, having the property of an intensity value and their locations.
  • Digital Number (DN): An intensity value of a pixel in a digital image.
  • Digital Image Processing: The numerical manipulation of DN values for extracting information about the phenomena of the surface they represent.
  • Electromagnetic Radiation (EMR): The energy propagated through space or a medium at the speed of light.
  • Electromagnetic Spectrum: The continuum of EMR that ranges from short-wave high-frequency cosmic radiations to long-wavelength low-frequency radio waves.
  • False Colour Composite (FCC): An artificially generated colour image in which blue, green, and red colours are assigned to wavelength regions to which they do not belong in nature.
  • Gray Scale: A medium to calibrate the variations in the brightness of an image that ranges from black to white with intermediate grey values.
  • Image: The permanent record of a scene comprising natural and man-made features, produced by photographic and non-photographic means.
  • Scene: The ground area covered by an image or a photograph.
  • Sensor: Any imaging or non-imaging device that receives EMR and converts it into a signal that can be recorded and displayed as a photographic or digital image.
  • Reflectance: The ratio of the radiant energy reflected by a substance to the energy it receives.
  • Spectral Band: The range of wavelengths in the continuous spectrum.
Stages in Remote Sensing

Remote sensing data acquisition involves several sequential processes that help collect information about the properties of the Earth's surface:

  1. Source of Energy (sun/self-emission)
  2. Transmission of energy from the source to the surface of the Earth
  3. Interaction of energy with the Earth's surface
  4. Propagation of reflected/emitted energy through the atmosphere
  5. Detection of the reflected/emitted energy by the sensor
  6. Conversion of energy received into photographic/digital form of data
  7. Extraction of information contents from the data products
  8. Conversion of information into Map/Tabular forms

a. Source of Energy

The Sun is the most important source of energy used in remote sensing. Energy may also be artificially generated, such as flashguns or energy beams used in RADAR (Radio Detection and Ranging).

b. Transmission of Energy from Source to Earth

Energy propagates in the form of waves at the speed of light: $$c hickapprox 300,000 ext{ km/s}$$ This propagation is called Electromagnetic Radiation (EMR). The plotting of these variations is known as the Electromagnetic Spectrum, which is grouped into Gamma rays, X-rays, Ultraviolet rays, Visible rays, Infrared rays, Microwaves, and Radio waves. Remote sensing primarily utilizes the visible, infrared, and microwave regions.

c. Interaction of Energy with the Earth's Surface

Propagating energy interacts with Earth features, leading to absorption, transmission, reflection, or emission. Different objects respond uniquely to different regions of the spectrum. For example, a fresh water body absorbs more energy in the red and infrared regions (appearing dark/black), while a turbid water body reflects more in the blue and green regions (appearing light-toned).

d. Propagation of Reflected/Emitted Energy through Atmosphere

Atmospheric constituents like gases, water molecules, and dust particles modify the reflected energy. Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), Hydrogen ($H$), and water molecules absorb energy in the middle infrared region, while dust particles scatter blue light, preventing some energy from reaching the sensor.

e. Detection of Reflected/Emitted Energy by the Sensor

Sensors are placed on satellite platforms operating in specific orbits:

  • Sun-Synchronous Orbit: Near-polar orbits at altitudes of 700–900 km (e.g., Indian Remote Sensing - IRS series).
  • Geostationary Orbit: Orbits that synchronize with the Earth's rotation, placed at an altitude of nearly 36,000 km (e.g., INSAT series).

f, g, h. Data Conversion, Extraction, and Presentation

The received radiations are electronically converted into a digital image composed of digital numbers arranged in rows and columns. This data is transmitted to Earth Receiving Stations (e.g., Shadnagar near Hyderabad in India). Once corrected for errors, information is extracted using digital image processing or visual interpretation, and finally converted into thematic maps or tabular data.

Sensors and Scanners

Sensors are classified into photographic (analogue) and non-photographic (digital) sensors. Non-photographic sensors are known as scanners.

Multispectral Scanners (MSS)

MSS are designed to obtain images of objects by sweeping across the field of view. The oscillation of a scanning mirror directs received energy to detectors, converting it into electrical signals and then into Digital Numbers (DN). Scanners are divided into two main types:

  1. Whiskbroom Scanners: Use a rotating mirror and a single detector. The mirror sweeps across the field of view between 90° and 120° to obtain images in narrow spectral bands. It utilizes the Instantaneous Field of View (IFOV) and Total Field of View (TFOV).
  2. Pushbroom Scanners: Consist of a linear array of numerous detectors. The number of detectors is equivalent to: $$ ext{Number of Detectors} = rac{ ext{Swath}}{ ext{Spatial Resolution}}$$ For example, the SPOT satellite's HRVR-1 sensor has a swath of 60 km and a spatial resolution of 20 metres, deploying 3,000 detectors.
Sensor Resolutions

Remote sensors are characterized by three primary types of resolutions:

Types of Resolutions

  • Spatial Resolution: The capability of the sensor to distinguish two closely spaced object surfaces as distinct entities.
  • Spectral Resolution: The sensing and recording power of the sensor in different bands of the electromagnetic spectrum.
  • Radiometric Resolution: The capability of the sensor to discriminate subtle differences in electromagnetic radiance. Higher resolution means smaller radiance differences can be detected.

Data Products & Visual Interpretation

Remotely sensed data products are classified into Photographic Images (recorded on light-sensitive film, typically 0.3 – 0.9 µm) and Digital Images (composed of discrete picture elements called pixels, each with a specific DN value).

Elements of Visual Interpretation

Visual interpretation is a manual exercise that uses several image and terrain characteristics to identify objects:

  1. Tone or Colour: The relative brightness or colour of elements in an image. Smooth, dry surfaces reflect more energy (lighter tone) than rough, moist surfaces (darker tone).
  2. Texture: The frequency of change and arrangement of tones in an image, varying from smooth (fine) to coarse.
  3. Size: Helps in identifying features based on their relative or absolute scale (e.g., distinguishing industrial complexes from residential dwellings).
  4. Shape: The general form, structure, or outline of individual objects (e.g., the linear shape of a railway line vs. sharp-bending roads).
  5. Shadow: Provides clues about the height of objects, though it can sometimes obscure features in city centres.
  6. Pattern: The spatial arrangement of objects in a repeating or characteristic layout (e.g., planned residential areas, orchards).
  7. Association: The relationship between a feature and its surrounding geographical context (e.g., a school associated with a playground).
