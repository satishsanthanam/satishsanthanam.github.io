Class 12 (Part 1) — Chapter 5: Secondary Activities

Introduction to Secondary Activities

All economic activities—primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary—revolve around obtaining and utilising resources necessary for survival. Secondary activities add value to natural resources by transforming raw materials into valuable products. For example, cotton in the boll has limited use, but once transformed into yarn, it becomes more valuable and can be used for making clothes. Similarly, iron ore cannot be used directly from mines, but after conversion into steel, it gains value and is used for making machines and tools. Therefore, secondary activities are concerned with manufacturing, processing, and construction (infrastructure) industries.

Manufacturing

Manufacturing involves a full array of production, from handicrafts to moulding iron and steel, stamping out plastic toys, and assembling delicate computer components or space vehicles. The common characteristics of manufacturing processes include the application of power, mass production of identical products, and specialised labour in factory settings for the production of standardised commodities.

Characteristics of Modern Large Scale Manufacturing

  • Specialisation of Skills/Methods of Production: Under the craft method, factories produce only a few made-to-order pieces, resulting in high costs. In contrast, mass production involves producing large quantities of standardised parts by each worker performing a single, repetitive task.
  • Mechanisation: This refers to using gadgets to accomplish tasks. Automation is the advanced stage of mechanisation where manufacturing occurs without human thinking, utilizing feedback and closed-loop computer control systems.
  • Technological Innovation: Achieved through research and development (R&D) strategies, it is crucial for quality control, eliminating waste and inefficiency, and combating pollution.
  • Organisational Structure and Stratification: Characterised by complex machine technology, extreme specialisation, division of labour, vast capital requirements, large organisations, and executive bureaucracy.
  • Uneven Geographic Distribution: Major concentrations of modern manufacturing cover less than 10% of the world's land area. These regions serve as centres of economic and political power.
Factors Influencing Industrial Location

Industries maximise profits by reducing costs. They are located at points where production costs are minimised. The key factors influencing industrial location include:

  • Access to Market: The existence of a market (people with demand and purchasing power) is the most important factor. Developed regions like Europe, North America, Japan, and Australia offer large global markets, while densely populated regions of South and South-east Asia also provide massive markets.
  • Access to Raw Material: Industries using cheap, bulky, and weight-losing materials (like steel, sugar, and cement) are located close to raw material sources. Perishability is also vital, leading agro-processing and dairy industries to locate near farms or milk supplies.
  • Access to Labour Supply: Despite increasing mechanisation and automation, some manufacturing types still require skilled labour.
  • Access to Sources of Energy: Energy-intensive industries, such as aluminium manufacturing, are located close to power sources. While coal was historically dominant, hydroelectricity and petroleum are now major energy sources.
  • Access to Transportation and Communication Facilities: Speedy and efficient transport is essential for carrying raw materials to factories and finished goods to markets. Highly developed transport systems in Western Europe and eastern North America have induced industrial concentration.
  • Government Policy: Governments adopt regional policies to promote balanced economic development by setting up industries in backward areas.
  • Access to Agglomeration Economies: Many industries benefit from being near a leader-industry and other related industries, deriving savings from mutual linkages.

Foot Loose Industries

Foot loose industries can be located in a wide variety of places. They are not dependent on any specific raw material (weight-losing or otherwise) and largely depend on component parts that can be obtained anywhere. They produce in small quantities, employ a small labour force, are generally non-polluting, and rely heavily on accessibility by road networks.

Classification of Manufacturing Industries

Manufacturing industries are classified based on their size, inputs/raw materials, output/products, and ownership.

Industries Based on Size

  • Household Industries or Cottage Manufacturing: The smallest manufacturing unit. Artisans use local raw materials and simple tools to produce everyday goods (foodstuffs, fabrics, mats, pottery) in their homes with family labour. Capital and transportation have low influence, and products are sold locally or bartered.
  • Small Scale Manufacturing: Distinguished by production techniques and workshops outside the home. It uses local raw materials, simple power-driven machines, and semi-skilled labour, providing significant employment in countries like India, China, Indonesia, and Brazil.
  • Large Scale Manufacturing: Involves large markets, diverse raw materials, enormous energy, specialised workers, advanced technology, assembly-line mass production, and large capital. It originated in the UK, north-eastern USA, and Europe, and has now diffused globally.

Industries Based on Inputs/Raw Materials

  • Agro-based Industries: Involves processing raw materials from fields and farms into finished products (e.g., food processing, sugar, fruit juices, beverages, spices, textiles). Agri-business refers to commercial farming on an industrial scale, often run by large corporations (e.g., tea plantations).
  • Mineral-based Industries: Use minerals as raw materials. Divided into metallic (ferrous like iron/steel, non-ferrous like copper/aluminium) and non-metallic (cement, pottery).
  • Chemical-based Industries: Use natural chemical minerals (petroleum, salts, sulphur, potash) or raw materials from wood and coal to produce synthetic fibres, plastics, and fertilisers.
  • Forest-based Raw Material Industries: Use forest products like timber, wood, bamboo, and grass for furniture, paper, and lac industries.
  • Animal-based Industries: Rely on animal products, such as leather for the leather industry and wool for woollen textiles.
Concept of High Technology Industry

High technology (high-tech) is the latest generation of manufacturing activities, characterized by intensive research and development (R&D) leading to advanced scientific and engineering products. Professional (white-collar) workers form a large share of the workforce, outnumbering blue-collar production workers. Examples include robotics, computer-aided design (CAD), electronic controls, and pharmaceuticals.

High-tech industrial landscapes feature neatly spaced, low, modern, dispersed office-plant-lab buildings rather than massive assembly structures. High-tech industries that are regionally concentrated, self-sustained, and highly specialised are called technopolies (e.g., Silicon Valley near San Francisco and Silicon Forest near Seattle).
