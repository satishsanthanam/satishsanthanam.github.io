Geography: Class 12 (Part 2) — Chapter 1: Population: Distribution, Density, Growth and Composition

Introduction & Sources of Population Data

The people are the most important component of a country. India is the second most populous country in the world after China, with a total population of 1,210 million (2011 Census). Such a large population puts immense pressure on limited resources and lies at the center of many socio-economic challenges in the country.

Sources of Population Data

Population data in India are collected through the Census operations, which are conducted every 10 years.

  • The first population Census in India was conducted in 1872.
  • The first complete and synchronous Census was conducted in 1881.
Distribution of Population

India has a highly uneven pattern of population distribution. A few states hold the vast majority of the population, while others have very sparse distributions.

Spatial Patterns of Distribution
  • Uttar Pradesh has the highest population in the country, followed by Maharashtra, Bihar, and West Bengal.
  • Ten states (Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Bihar, West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Karnataka, and Gujarat) together account for about 76 percent of the total population.
  • States with very small shares of population despite large geographical areas include Jammu & Kashmir (1.04%), Arunachal Pradesh (0.11%), and Uttarakhand (0.84%).

Factors Influencing Population Distribution

The uneven spatial distribution of population is determined by a combination of physical, socio-economic, and historical factors:

  1. Physical Factors: Climate, terrain, and availability of water. The North Indian Plains, deltas, and Coastal Plains have higher population concentrations than the interior districts of southern and central India, the Himalayas, or western desert regions.
  2. Socio-Economic and Historical Factors: Evolution of settled agriculture, agricultural development, pattern of human settlement, transport networks, industrialisation, and urbanisation. Urban regions like Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, and Bengaluru have high concentrations due to industrial development and rural-to-urban migration.
Density of Population

Density of Population is expressed as the number of persons per unit area. It provides a better understanding of the spatial distribution of population in relation to land.

The average density of population in India in 2011 was 382 persons per sq km, up steadily from 117 persons per sq km in 1951.

Spatial Variation in Density
  • Lowest Density: Arunachal Pradesh (17 persons per sq km).
  • Highest Density (States): Bihar (1,102 persons per sq km) and West Bengal (1,029 persons per sq km).
  • Highest Density (UTs): NCT Delhi (11,297 persons per sq km).

Physiological and Agricultural Densities

To get a better insight into the human-land ratio and pressure on cultivable land, geographers use physiological and agricultural densities:

Physiological Density:

$$\text{Physiological Density} = \frac{\text{Total Population}}{\text{Net Cultivated Area}}$$

Agricultural Density:

$$\text{Agricultural Density} = \frac{\text{Total Agricultural Population}}{\text{Net Cultivable Area}}$$

Note: Agricultural population includes cultivators, agricultural labourers, and their family members.

Growth of Population

Growth of Population is the change in the number of people living in a particular area between two points of time. Its rate is expressed in percentage. It has two components: natural (births and deaths) and induced (migration).

The annual growth rate of India's population is 1.64 percent (2011).

Phases of Population Growth (1901-2011)

The growth rate of population in India over the last century shows four distinct phases:

  • Phase I (1901-1921) - Period of Stagnant/Stationary Growth: Growth rate was extremely low, even recording a negative growth rate during 1911-1921. Both birth and death rates were high due to poor health services, illiteracy, and food distribution inefficiencies.
  • Phase II (1921-1951) - Period of Steady Growth: An overall improvement in health and sanitation brought down the mortality rate, while the crude birth rate remained high, leading to a steady population increase.
  • Phase III (1951-1981) - Period of Population Explosion: Caused by a rapid fall in the mortality rate alongside high fertility rates. The average annual growth rate was as high as 2.2%. Centralised planning and developmental activities improved living conditions. High international migration (from Tibet, Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan) also contributed.
  • Phase IV (Post-1981 to Present) - Period of High Growth with Signs of Slowing Down: The growth rate, though still high, has started declining gradually due to a downward trend in the crude birth rate, influenced by increased age at marriage and improved female education.

Adolescent Population & Policies

An important aspect of India's population growth is its youth and adolescent share.

  • Adolescents (aged 10-19 years) constitute about 20.9 percent of the population (2011).
  • National Youth Policy (NYP-2014): Launched in February 2014 to empower youth (defined as persons in the age group of 15-29 years) to achieve their full potential.
  • National Policy for Skill Development and Entrepreneurship (2015): Formulated to align skilling activities across the country to common standards.
Population Composition

Population Composition is a field of study within population geography analyzing age, sex, place of residence, language, religion, and occupational characteristics.

Rural-Urban Composition
  • Rural Population: About 68.8 percent of India's total population lives in villages (2011). States like Himachal Pradesh and Bihar have very high proportions of rural population.
  • Urban Population: Stands at 31.16 percent (2011) but is growing rapidly due to economic development and rural-to-urban migration.

Linguistic Composition

India is a highly diverse linguistic nation. According to the Grierson's Linguistic Survey of India (1903-1928), there were 179 languages and 544 dialects. Today, there are 22 Scheduled Languages under the Constitution of India.

Occupational Composition

The population of India is divided into three economic groups based on work status:

  • Main Worker: A person who works for at least 183 days (or 6 months) in a year.
  • Marginal Worker: A person who works for less than 183 days in a year.
  • Non-Worker: A person who does not work.

In India, the proportion of workers (both main and marginal) is 39.8 percent (2011), leaving a large dependent non-working population of about 60 percent.

Occupational Categories (2011 Census):

  1. Cultivators
  2. Agricultural Labourers
  3. Household Industrial Workers
  4. Other Workers
