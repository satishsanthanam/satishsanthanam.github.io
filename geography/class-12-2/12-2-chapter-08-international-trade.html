Geography: Class 12 (Part 2) — Chapter 8: International Trade

Introduction to India's International Trade

India's international trade has undergone a sea change in recent years in terms of volume, composition, and direction. Although India's contribution to world trade is about one per cent of the total volume, it plays a significant role in the world economy.

In 1950-51, India's external trade was worth Rs. 1,214 crore, which rose to Rs. 77,19,796 crore in 2020-21 due to momentum in manufacturing, liberal government policies, and market diversification.

India's Foreign Trade Trends

Though there has been an increase in the total volume of imports and exports, the value of imports continues to be higher than that of exports, leading to a trade deficit.

Changing Pattern of the Composition of India's Exports

The composition of commodities in India's international trade has been undergoing a change over the years. The share of agriculture and allied products and manufactured goods has decreased, whereas the share of crude petroleum and products and other commodities has increased.

The decline in traditional items is largely due to tough international competition. Amongst agricultural products, there is a decline in the export of traditional items like cashew, though an increase has been registered in floricultural products, fresh fruits, marine products, and sugar.

The manufacturing sector alone accounted for 67.8 per cent of India's total value of export in 2021-22. Engineering goods have shown significant growth. Gems and jewellery also contribute a large share of India's foreign trade.

Changing Patterns of the Composition of India's Import

India faced serious food shortages during the 1950s and 1960s. The major items of import at that time were foodgrains, capital goods, machinery, and equipment. After the 1970s, foodgrain imports were discontinued due to the success of the Green Revolution.

Direction of Trade

India has trade relations with most countries and major trading blocks of the world. Asia and ASEAN constitute the largest share of India's import trade.

India aims to double its share in international trade within the next five years by adopting measures such as import liberalisation, reduction in import duties, delicensing, and changing from process to product patents.

Sea Ports as Gateways of International Trade

Most of India's foreign trade is carried through sea and air routes. A small portion is carried through land routes to neighbouring countries like Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan.

India is surrounded by sea on three sides and is bestowed with a long coastline. Water provides a smooth surface for very cheap transport. At present, India has 12 major ports and 200 minor or intermediate ports.

The capacity of Indian ports increased from 20 million tonnes of cargo handling in 1951 to more than 837 million tonnes in 2016.

Major Ports on the West Coast
  • Deendayal Port (Kandla Port): Situated at the head of the Gulf of Kuchchh, developed to cater to the needs of western and north-western India and reduce pressure on Mumbai port. It is specially designed to handle petroleum, petroleum products, and fertilisers.
  • Mumbai Port: A natural harbour and the biggest port of the country. It is situated closer to general routes from the Middle East, Mediterranean countries, North Africa, North America, and Europe.
  • Jawaharlal Nehru Port (Nhava Sheva): Developed as a satellite port to relieve pressure at Mumbai port. It is the largest container port in India.
  • Mormugao Port: Situated at the entrance of the Zuari estuary in Goa, it is a natural harbour that gained significance for handling iron-ore exports to Japan.
  • New Mangalore Port: Located in Karnataka, it caters to the export of iron-ore and iron-concentrates, and handles fertilisers, petroleum products, and edible oils.
  • Cochin Port: Situated at the head of Vembanad Kayal, popularly known as the 'Queen of the Arabian Sea'. It is close to the Suez-Colombo route.

Major Ports on the East Coast
  • Shyama Prasad Mookerjee (Kolkata) Port: Located on the Hugli river, 128 km inland from the Bay of Bengal. It is a riverine port confronted with the problem of silt accumulation. Its hinterland covers U.P., Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Sikkim, and the north-eastern states.
  • Haldia Port: Located 105 km downstream from Kolkata, constructed to reduce congestion at Kolkata port. It handles bulk cargo like iron ore, coal, petroleum, and fertilisers.
  • Paradwip Port: Situated in the Mahanadi delta, about 100 km from Cuttack. It has the deepest harbour specially suited to handle very large vessels, mainly for iron-ore export.
  • Visakhapatnam Port: A land-locked harbour in Andhra Pradesh, connected to the sea by a channel cut through solid rock and sand.
  • Chennai Port: One of the oldest artificial ports on the eastern coast, built in 1859. It is not very suitable for large ships due to shallow waters near the coast.
  • Kamarajar (Ennore) Port: A newly developed port in Tamil Nadu, constructed 25 km north of Chennai to relieve pressure on Chennai port.
  • V.O. Chidambaranar (Tuticorin) Port: Developed to relieve pressure on Chennai port, dealing with coal, salt, food grains, edible oils, and petroleum products.
Airports

Air transport plays an important role in international trade by taking the least time for carriage and handling high-value or perishable goods over long distances. However, it is costly and unsuitable for carrying heavy and bulky commodities.

There were 25 major airports functioning in the country (Annual Report 2016-17). Under the UDAN scheme launched in 2017, a total of 73 unserved/underserved airports, including 9 heliports and 2 water aerodromes, have been operationalised.
