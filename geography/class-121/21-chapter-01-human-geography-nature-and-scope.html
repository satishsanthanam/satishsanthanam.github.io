Class 121 — Chapter 1: Human Geography: Nature and Scope

Introduction to Human Geography

Geography as a field of study is integrative, empirical, and practical. It covers the entire earth's surface, which comprises two major components: nature (physical environment) and life forms including human beings.

Dualism in Geography

Geography has historically been subject to dualism, sparking debates on whether the discipline should be:

  • Law-making/theorising (nomothetic) or descriptive (idiographic).
  • Organised via a regional or systematic approach.
  • Interpreted theoretically or through a historic-institutional approach.

Ultimately, the dichotomy between physical and human geography is invalid as nature and humans are inseparable elements that must be viewed holistically, often described using human anatomical metaphors (e.g., 'face' of the earth, 'eye' of the storm, 'arteries' of circulation).

Definitions of Human Geography

  • Ratzel: "Human geography is the synthetic study of relationship between human societies and earth’s surface." (Emphasis on synthesis).
  • Ellen C. Semple: "Human geography is the study of the changing relationship between the unresting man and the unstable earth." (Emphasis on dynamism).
  • Paul Vidal de la Blache: "Conception resulting from a more synthetic knowledge of the physical laws governing our earth and of the relations between the living beings which inhabit it." (Offers a new conception of interrelationships).
Nature of Human Geography

Human geography studies the inter-relationship between the physical environment and the socio-cultural environment created by human beings through mutual interaction. While the physical environment provides the stage (landforms, soils, climate, water, flora, and fauna), humans create cultural landscapes (houses, villages, cities, road-rail networks, industries, and farms) using resources from nature.

Human-Nature Interactions

Naturalisation of Humans (Environmental Determinism)

In early stages of interaction, human technology was primitive, and social development was low. Humans adapted directly to the dictates of nature, fearing its fury and worshipping it. This direct dependence of primitive societies on nature as "Mother Nature" is termed environmental determinism.

Humanisation of Nature (Possibilism)

With social and cultural development, humans develop better technology, moving from a state of necessity to a state of freedom. They create possibilities with resources obtained from the environment, leaving human imprints (urban sprawls, health resorts, oceanic routes, satellites) on nature. This is termed possibilism.

Neodeterminism (Stop and Go Determinism)

Introduced by geographer Griffith Taylor, Neodeterminism represents a middle path (Madhyam Marg) between environmental determinism and possibilism. It asserts that there is neither a situation of absolute necessity nor absolute freedom. Human beings can conquer nature only by obeying it, responding to environmental signals to avoid ecological crises like global warming, ozone depletion, and greenhouse effects.

Schools of Thought in Human Geography
  • Welfare or Humanistic School: Mainly concerned with different aspects of social well-being of the people, including housing, health, and education.
  • Radical School: Employed Marxian theory to explain the basic causes of poverty, deprivation, and social inequality, linking contemporary social problems to the development of capitalism.
  • Behavioural School: Laid great emphasis on lived experience and the perception of space by social categories based on ethnicity, race, and religion.
