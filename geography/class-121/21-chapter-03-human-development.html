Class 121 — Chapter 3: Human Development

Growth and Development

Both growth and development refer to changes over a period of time, but they differ significantly in their nature and value orientation.

Key Differences
  • Growth is quantitative and value neutral. It can be positive (indicating an increase) or negative (indicating a decrease).
  • Development means a qualitative change which is always value positive. It occurs when there is a positive change in quality and cannot take place without an increment to existing conditions.
  • Positive growth does not always lead to development. Development only occurs when positive growth is accompanied by qualitative improvements (e.g., a city's population growing alongside improvements in housing, basic services, and infrastructure).
Concept of Human Development

The concept of human development was introduced by Dr. Mahbub-ul-Haq and further expanded by Nobel Laureate Prof. Amartya Sen in the late 1980s and early 1990s.

Core Philosophy
  • Human development is defined as development that enlarges people's choices and improves their lives.
  • People are central to all development under this concept.
  • The basic goal of development is to create conditions where people can live a meaningful life.
  • A meaningful life is not just a long one; it must be a life with purpose where people are healthy, able to develop talents, participate in society, and are free to achieve their goals.
  • Prof. Amartya Sen saw an increase in freedom (or decrease in unfreedom) as the main objective of development.
The Four Pillars of Human Development

Human development is supported by four fundamental concepts, which act as its pillars:

1. Equity

Refers to making equal access to opportunities available to everybody, irrespective of gender, race, income, and caste.

2. Sustainability

Means continuity in the availability of opportunities. Each generation must have the same opportunities, requiring the responsible use of environmental, financial, and human resources to avoid limiting future generations.

3. Productivity

Refers to human labour productivity or productivity in terms of human work. This must be constantly enriched by building capabilities in people through education and health facilities.

4. Empowerment

Means to have the power to make choices. This power comes from increasing freedom, capability, good governance, and people-oriented policies.

Approaches to Human Development

There are several historical and contemporary approaches to understanding and addressing human development:

Approach Key Features
Income Approach One of the oldest approaches. Links human development directly to income, assuming higher income reflects higher freedom and development.
Welfare Approach Views human beings as beneficiaries or targets of development. Advocates for high government expenditure on education, health, and social amenities. People are passive recipients.
Basic Needs Approach Proposed by the International Labour Organisation (ILO). Emphasizes the provision of six basic needs: health, education, food, water supply, sanitation, and housing. Ignores the question of human choices.
Capability Approach Associated with Prof. Amartya Sen. Focuses on building human capabilities in health, education, and resource access as the key to increasing human development.
Measuring Human Development

The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) has published the Human Development Report annually since 1990, utilizing key indices to measure progress.

Human Development Index (HDI)

Ranks countries based on their performance across three key areas, with each dimension given an equal weightage:

$$\text{Dimension Weight} = \frac{1}{3}$$
  • Health: Measured by life expectancy at birth.
  • Education: Measured by adult literacy rate and gross enrolment ratio.
  • Access to Resources: Measured in terms of purchasing power in U.S. dollars.

The HDI score ranges between 0 and 1. The closer a score is to 1, the greater the level of human development.

Human Poverty Index (HPI)

A non-income measure that estimates the shortfall in human development. It considers factors such as the probability of not surviving till age 40, adult illiteracy rate, lack of access to clean water, and the number of underweight children.

International Comparisons

International comparisons show that the size of a territory and per capita income are not directly related to human development. Smaller or poorer nations often perform better than their larger or richer neighbors.

Gross National Happiness (GNH)

Bhutan is the only country in the world to officially proclaim Gross National Happiness (GNH) as the measure of its progress, emphasizing spiritual, non-material, and qualitative aspects of development alongside environmental conservation.
