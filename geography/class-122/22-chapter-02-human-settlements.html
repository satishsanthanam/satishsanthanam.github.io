Class 122 — Chapter 2: HUMAN SETTLEMENTS

Introduction to Human Settlements

Human Settlement means a cluster of dwellings of any type or size where human beings live. The process of settlement inherently involves the grouping of people and apportioning of territory as their resource base.

Rural vs. Urban Settlements

  • The rural settlements derive their life support or basic economic needs from land-based primary economic activities, whereas urban settlements depend on processing of raw materials, manufacturing of finished goods, and a variety of services.
  • Cities act as nodes of economic growth, providing goods and services to their rural hinterlands in return for food and raw materials. This functional relationship is facilitated by transport and communication networks.
  • Rural and urban settlements differ in social relationships. Rural social relations are intimate and less mobile, whereas urban life is complex, fast, and social relations are formal.
Types of Rural Settlement

Types of rural settlements are determined by the extent of the built-up area and inter-house distance. Various factors responsible for different types of rural settlements in India include physical features (terrain, altitude, climate, water availability), cultural/ethnic factors (social structure, caste, religion), and security factors (defense against thefts and robberies).

Clustered Settlements

Clustered, agglomerated or nucleated settlements are compact or closely built-up areas of houses. The living area is distinct and separated from surrounding farms, barns, and pastures. They present recognizable geometric shapes like rectangular, radial, or linear. Commonly found in fertile alluvial plains and northeastern states. Examples include Bundelkhand and Nagaland (for security) and Rajasthan (due to water scarcity).

Semi-Clustered Settlements

Semi-clustered or fragmented settlements result from segregation or fragmentation of a large compact village. One or more sections of society are forced or choose to live away from the main cluster. Generally, the land-owning dominant community occupies the central part, while lower strata and menial workers settle on the outer flanks. Widespread in Gujarat plains and parts of Rajasthan.

Hamleted Settlements

Hamleted settlements are fragmented into several units physically separated from each other but bearing a common name. Locally called panna, para, palli, nagla, or dhani. Frequently found in the middle and lower Ganga plain, Chhattisgarh, and lower valleys of the Himalayas.

Dispersed Settlements

Dispersed or isolated settlements appear as isolated huts or hamlets in remote jungles or on small hills with farms or pastures on slopes. Caused by fragmented terrain and land resource bases. Found in Meghalaya, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, and Kerala.

Urban Settlements and Evolution of Towns

Definition of Urban Settlements

According to the Census of India, 1991, urban settlements must meet the following criteria:

  • Presence of a municipality, corporation, cantonment board, or notified town area committee.
  • A minimum population of 5,000 persons: $$\text{Population} \ge 5000$$
  • At least 75% of male main workers engaged in non-agricultural pursuits.
  • A density of population of at least 400 persons per square kilometer: $$\text{Density} \ge 400 \text{ persons/km}^2$$

Evolution of Towns in India

Indian towns can be classified based on their evolution period:

  • Ancient Towns: Developed as religious and cultural centers with histories spanning over 2000 years. Examples: Varanasi, Prayagraj (Prayag), Patna (Pataliputra), Madurai.
  • Medieval Towns: Developed as headquarters of principalities and kingdoms, often as fort towns. Examples: Delhi, Hyderabad, Jaipur, Lucknow, Agra, Nagpur.
  • Modern Towns: Developed by the British and other Europeans, starting as trading ports (Surat, Daman, Goa, Pondicherry) and consolidating around nodes (Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata). Post-independence, administrative headquarters (Chandigarh, Bhubaneswar, Gandhinagar) and industrial centers (Durgapur, Bhilai, Sindri) were established.
Urbanisation in India

The level of urbanisation is measured as the percentage of urban population to total population. In India, the level of urbanisation in 2011 was 31.16%, which is low compared to developed countries. Total urban population increased eleven-fold during the twentieth century.

Functional Classification of Towns

Indian cities and towns can be broadly classified based on their dominant or specialized functions:

  • Administrative towns and cities: Support administrative headquarters of higher order. Examples: Chandigarh, New Delhi, Bhopal, Shillong, Guwahati, Imphal, Srinagar, Gandhinagar, Jaipur, Chennai.
  • Industrial towns: Driven by industries. Examples: Mumbai, Salem, Coimbatore, Modinagar, Jamshedpur, Hugli, Bhilai.
  • Transport Cities: Ports engaged in export/import (Kandla, Cochin, Kozhikode, Vishakhapatnam) or hubs of inland transport (Agra, Dhulia, Pandit Deendayal Upadhyay Nagar, Itarsi, Katni).
  • Commercial towns: Specialise in trade and commerce. Examples: Kolkata, Saharanpur, Satna.
  • Mining towns: Developed in mineral-rich areas. Examples: Raniganj, Jharia, Digboi, Ankaleshwar, Singrauli.
  • Garrison Cantonment towns: Emerged as military/garrison towns. Examples: Ambala, Jalandhar, Mhow, Babina, Udhampur.
  • Educational towns: Major campus towns and centers of education. Examples: Roorkee, Varanasi, Aligarh, Pilani, Prayagraj.
  • Religious and cultural towns: Prominent due to religious or cultural significance. Examples: Varanasi, Mathura, Amritsar, Madurai, Puri, Ajmer, Pushkar, Tirupati, Kurukshetra, Haridwar, Ujjain.
  • Tourist towns: Popular tourist destinations. Examples: Nainital, Mussoorie, Shimla, Pachmarhi, Jodhpur, Jaisalmer, Udagamandalam (Ooty), Mount Abu.

Smart Cities Mission

The objective of the Smart Cities Mission is to promote cities that provide core infrastructure, a clean and sustainable environment, and give a decent quality of life to their citizens through smart solutions.
