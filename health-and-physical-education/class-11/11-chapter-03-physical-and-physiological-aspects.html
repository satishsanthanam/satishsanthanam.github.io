Physical and Health Education: Class 11 — Chapter 03: Physical and Physiological Aspects of Physical Education and Sports

This chapter explores the physical and physiological aspects of physical education and sports, including growth and development, factors affecting them, developmental stages, and the physiological effects of exercise on various body systems.

Growth and Development

Understanding Growth and Development

Growth refers to the biological process through which the body increases in size, shape, and mass. It is a quantitative physical change starting from conception and continuing until physical maturity is reached.

Development is a progressive series of qualitative changes representing the emerging and expanding of an individual's capabilities. It is a lifelong process that includes physical, social, cognitive, and emotional changes.

Factors Affecting Growth and Development

Growth and development are influenced by a combination of internal and external factors:

  • Heredity: The biological transmission of physical and psychological characteristics (e.g., height, eye colour, temperament) from parents to offspring.
  • Environment: The sum total of surroundings, categorized into:
    • Physical: Outer physical surroundings, weather, and climate.
    • Social: Society, family, school, customs, and culture.
    • Psychological: Emotional interactions, love, affection, and relationships.
  • Gender: Influences growth patterns; boys are generally taller and heavier, while girls demonstrate earlier physical growth spurts during adolescence.
  • Nutrition: Essential supply of calories and nutrients required for normal structural and functional development. Malnutrition adversely affects growth.
  • Exercise: Physical activities stimulate muscular strength, blood circulation, and skeletal muscle development.
  • Hormones: Chemical secretions from ductless endocrine glands:
    • Thyroxin (Thyroid gland): Influences skeletal and muscular growth.
    • Adrenaline (Adrenal glands): Regulates heartbeat, blood pressure, and sugar release.
    • Gonads: Affect growth and sexual behaviour.
  • Learning and Reinforcement: Maturation and active learning ("learning by doing") shape cognitive and motor skills.
  • Pollution: Air and indoor pollution (e.g., lead exposure) obstruct brain and organ development.
  • Socio-economic Status: Higher income levels correlate with better nutrition, healthcare, and growth opportunities.
Physical and Physiological Changes During Developmental Stages

Early Childhood

Spans from birth to about eight years. Characterized by rapid physical growth (doubling height and quadrupling weight by age three), development of fine and gross motor skills (scribbling, balancing, skipping), and rapid cognitive and language progression.

Middle Childhood

Characterized by slow and steady physical growth until the onset of puberty. Focuses on building competence, cognitive development, and gaining enthusiasm for learning.

Adolescence

Spans from 12 to 18 years. A period of accelerated growth (growth spurt) where individuals gain significant height (15–20 cm) and weight (8–10 kg). Sexual maturation and major cognitive developments occur during this stage.

Physiological Aspects of Activities

Warming Up

Warm-up is a short-duration light activity performed prior to intense physical work to prepare the body physiologically and psychologically.

  • General Warm-up: Rhythmic movements using large muscle groups (e.g., jogging, stretching, slow running) to increase body temperature and reduce muscle viscosity.
  • Specific Warm-up: Exercises directly related to the sport (e.g., dribbling in basketball, shadow practice in tennis).
  • Methods: Exercise, massage, hot water baths, and sipping hot beverages.

Sports Conditioning

Sports Conditioning aims to complement sports training by enhancing strength, balance, coordination, flexibility, speed, and power, while preventing injuries.

Cool Down (Limbering Down)

Cool down is a session of light exercises and stretching following rigorous activity to transition the body back to a resting state.

  • Helps heart rate and breathing return to normal.
  • Prevents blood pooling and dizziness.
  • Removes metabolic waste products like lactic acid.
  • Reduces muscle soreness.
Effects of Exercise on Various Body Systems

Cardiovascular System

  • Heart Rate: Increases from a resting average of 72 beats/minute up to 140–180 beats/minute during exercise.
  • Stroke Volume: The volume of blood pumped per heartbeat increases.
  • Cardiac Output: The total volume of blood pumped per minute increases. Calculated as:
    $$\text{Cardiac Output} = \text{Heart Rate} \times \text{Stroke Volume}$$
  • Cardiac Hypertrophy: Enlargement of the heart (often called "athlete's heart") due to long-term training.
  • Blood Volume: Endurance training increases resting blood volume, plasma volume, and capillary density.

Respiratory System

  • Tidal Volume: Volume of air inspired or expired per breath increases 5 to 6 times above resting values (500 ml) during exercise.
  • Respiratory Rate: Increases from 12–20 breaths/minute at rest to 2–3 times higher during exercise.
  • Minute Ventilation (PV): The total volume of gas inhaled or exhaled per minute increases. Calculated as:
    $$\text{PV} = \text{Tidal Volume} \times \text{Respiratory Rate}$$
  • Pulmonary Diffusing Capacity: Increases to allow more oxygen to diffuse into the blood.
  • Vital Capacity: The maximum volume of air forcefully expired after maximum inspiration.

Muscular System

  • Muscular Hypertrophy: Increase in muscle fiber size, diameter, and protein content due to regular training.
  • Aerobic Changes: Increased myoglobin content and glycogen oxidation for better oxygen supply.
  • Anaerobic Changes: Increased lactic acid tolerance and improved anaerobic energy pathways.
  • Capillary Supply: Increased capillary-to-fiber ratio and density.

Digestive System

  • Blood Flow: Increases to digestive organs, enhancing overall functioning.
  • Metabolism: Boosts resting metabolic rate.
  • Constipation Prevention: Stimulates natural contraction of intestinal muscles.
  • Disease Prevention: Strengthens internal organs like the liver, stomach, and intestines.
