Physical and Health Education: Class 11 (Part 06) — Chapter 06: Yoga and its Relevance in the Modern Times

Introduction to Yoga

Yoga is a science of living that works on the physical, mental, emotional, social, and spiritual levels of human beings. It brings harmony between body and mind, serving as both an art and a science for healthy living.

Etymology and Meaning

The word 'yoga' is derived from the Sanskrit root yuj, which means to join, to yoke, or to unite. It represents an ancient system of self-development and the natural process of human evolution.

Modern Relevance and Stress Management

In modern times, children and adolescents face immense stress from school, home, and social environments. Prolonged stress leads to psychosomatic diseases and social unrest. Yoga addresses these issues by restoring balance between the physical, mental, and spiritual dimensions of life, countering the negative impacts of a materialistic lifestyle, attachment, and endless ambitions.

History and Development of Yoga

Yoga has a legacy as old as human civilization, with historical links to ancient scriptures and cultures:

  • Indus Valley Civilisation (circa 2000 B.C.): Stone seals restored from these sites depict figures practicing yoga.
  • Vedas: References to yoga are found in all four Vedas: Rigveda, Yajurveda, Samaveda, and Atharvaveda.
  • Upanishads: Explain Moksha (liberation) as the ultimate goal of yoga.
  • Buddhism & Jainism: Contributed through teachings like Buddha's Arya Asthangic Marga (Eightfold Path) and Jainism's five great vows.
  • Epics: The Ramayana, Mahabharata, and particularly the Bhagavad Gita serve as classical treatises on yoga.
  • Shad Darshans: Contain foundational descriptions of yoga philosophies.
  • Maharishi Patanjali: Codified the comprehensive system of Ashtang Yoga (Eight Limbs of Yoga) around the second century B.C.
  • Hatha Yoga Tradition: Developed through the Nath culture, focusing on physical health and body-mind harmony. Key texts include Hatha Yoga Pradeepika and Gheranda Samhita.
  • Modern Era: Preached to the masses by 19th and 20th-century spiritual leaders like Ramakrishna Paramahansa, Swami Vivekananda, Maharishi Aurobindo, and Ramana Maharishi.
Schools of Yoga

To attain bliss and true self-knowledge, ancient seers established different paths or schools of yoga:

Karma Yoga (Path of Action)

Focuses on attaining union with the higher-self by harmonizing actions without attachment to the results, as taught in the Bhagavad Gita.

  • Karma as duty: Performing actions dutifully with total involvement leads to joy.
  • Karma Sukaushalam: Performing actions with skill, concentration, and detachment.
  • Nishkama Karma: Action performed without personal motives or expectation of rewards.

Jnana Yoga (Path of Knowledge)

A philosophical path utilizing intellect to attain knowledge of the 'self' and overcome Avidya (ignorance), which is the root cause of suffering.

  • Develops Viveka (discriminating knowledge) to distinguish between reality and unreality.
  • Three key stages: Shravan (hearing), Manana (reflection), and Nidhidhyasana (meditation).

Raja Yoga (Path of Psychic Control)

A systematic, scientific process of culturing the mind and controlling mental modifications (Chittavrittis).

  • Emphasizes Abhyasa (continuous practice) and Vairagya (detachment).
  • Based on the eight-limbed system (Ashtanga Yoga) propounded by Patanjali.

Bhakti Yoga (Path of Devotion)

A systematic method of engaging the mind in divine, selfless, and unconditional love for God.

The nine forms of Bhakti (Navadha Bhakti) are:

  1. Shravan
  2. Kirtan
  3. Smaran
  4. Padsevan
  5. Archana
  6. Vandana
  7. Dasya
  8. Sakhya
  9. Atmanivedan
Guidelines for Yoga Practice

Before the Practice

  • Maintain cleanliness (Shauch) of surroundings, body, and mind.
  • Perform in a calm, quiet atmosphere with a relaxed body.
  • Practice on an empty stomach; empty bladder and bowels beforehand.
  • Use a comfortable mattress, durry, or folded blanket on an even surface.
  • Wear light, comfortable cotton clothing.
  • Avoid practicing during exhaustion, illness, or hurry.

During the Practice

  • Start with a prayer to create a conducive environment.
  • Perform movements slowly, maintaining breath awareness and relaxation.
  • Breathe only through the nostrils unless instructed otherwise.
  • Do not strain; practice according to individual capacity.
  • Consult a doctor or yoga therapist in case of chronic cardiac or medical conditions.

After the Practice

  • Wait 15 to 30 minutes before taking a bath or consuming food.
  • Conclude sessions with Shavasana, followed by meditation and Shanti Path.
Yogic Principles and Practices for Wellness

Wellness is a state of multidimensional health (physical, emotional, intellectual, social, environmental, and spiritual). Yoga promotes wellness through several core mechanisms:

  • Shodhana Kriyas: Cleanse accumulated toxins from the body.
  • Yogic Lifestyle: Incorporates a nourishing diet to neutralize free radicals.
  • Yogasana: Establishes physical balance, emotional stability, and physiological harmony.
  • Pranayama: Regulates autonomic respiratory mechanisms, enhancing energy and emotional control.
  • Dharana: Focuses the mind positively, improving blood circulation and energy flow.
  • Dhyana: Creates a calm internal environment through contemplative practices.
Yogic Cleansing Practices (Shodhanakriyas)

Neti (Nasal Cleansing)

A Hatha yogic kriya concerned with cleaning the nasal passage and throat, serving as a prerequisite for Pranayama.

  • Jala Neti (Water Cleansing): Uses lukewarm salted water poured through a Neti pot from one nostril and allowed to flow out the other. Helps cure chronic headaches, insomnia, and improves eyesight.
  • Sutra Neti (Thread Cleansing): Involves inserting a soft thread or catheter through the nose and pulling it out through the mouth. Stimulates nasal nerves and increases resistance to viral invasions.

Kapalbhati (Skull Shining Practice)

Derived from Kapala (skull) and Bhati (to shine). Rejuvenates mental functions and purifies frontal air sinuses.

  • Technique: Active, forceful exhalations with passive, natural inhalations.
  • Ratio: Maintain a rhythmic pattern, starting with 10 strokes and progressing up to 30–40 strokes per round.

Agnisara (Stoking the Digestive Fire)

Involves rapid contraction and expansion of the abdominal muscles while holding the breath outside (Bahya Kumbhaka).

  • Benefits: Massages abdominal organs, improves digestion, and strengthens core muscles.
Surya Namaskara (Sun Salutation)

A complete practice consisting of a sequence of 7 distinct asanas performed in 12 steps, synchronized with breathing patterns:

  1. Namaskarasana (Prayer Pose) - Exhale
  2. Hastottanasana (Raised Arms Pose) - Inhale
  3. Padhastasana (Hand to Foot Pose) - Exhale
  4. Ashwasanchalanasana (Equestrian Pose) - Inhale
  5. Parvatasana (Mountain Pose) - Exhale
  6. Ashtanga Namaskarasana (Eight-Limbed Salutation) - Retain/Suspend Breath
  7. Bhujangasana (Cobra Pose) - Inhale
  8. Parvatasana (Mountain Pose) - Exhale
  9. Ashwasanchalanasana (Equestrian Pose) - Inhale
  10. Padhastasana (Hand to Foot Pose) - Exhale
  11. Hastottanasana (Raised Arms Pose) - Inhale
  12. Namaskarasana (Prayer Pose) - Exhale

Benefits: Stimulates and balances all bodily systems, improves blood circulation, aids digestion, and enhances concentration.

Yogasanas (Postures)

Asanas are physical postures practiced to achieve physical stability, mental clarity, and overall wellness.

Standing Asanas

  • Tadasana (Palm Tree Pose): Stretches muscles and nerves, helps increase height in growing children.
  • Vrikshasana (Tree Pose): Improves neuromuscular coordination, balance, and concentration.
  • Padahastasana (Hand-to-Feet Pose): Makes the spine flexible, improves digestion, and prevents constipation.
  • Ardha Chakrasana (Half Wheel Pose): Relaxes neck and back muscles, improves lung capacity.
  • Trikonasana (Triangle Pose): Strengthens calf, thigh, and waist muscles; improves spine flexibility.
  • Parshvakonasana (Side Angle Pose): Strengthens heart muscles, reduces fat from waist and hips.

Sitting Asanas

  • Padmasana (Lotus Pose): Induces mental calmness, improves concentration, and tones sacral nerves.
  • Vajrasana (Thunderbolt Pose): The only posture that can be practiced immediately after meals; aids digestion.
  • Bhadrasana (Gentle Pose): Keeps the spine erect, beneficial for women's pelvic health.
  • Ushtrasana (Camel Pose): Useful for back and neck pain, reduces abdominal fat.
  • Shashankasana (Hare Pose): Tones reproductive organs, relieves sciatica, and reduces stress and anger.
  • Uttanamandukasana (Upright Frog Pose): Improves diaphragmatic movements and lung capacity.
  • Paschimottanasana (Seated Forward Bend): Stimulates abdominal organs, reduces obesity, and stretches the entire back.
  • Vakrasana (Twisted Pose): Twists the spine, useful in managing high blood pressure and sciatica.
  • Ardhamatsyendrasana (Half Fish Pose): Stimulates the pancreas, kidneys, and liver; highly beneficial for diabetes.
  • Gomukhasana (Cow's Face Pose): Strengthens back muscles, useful in frozen shoulder and respiratory diseases.

Prone Asanas (Lying on Abdomen)

  • Makarasana (Crocodile Pose): A relaxation posture beneficial for asthma, anxiety, and fatigue.
  • Bhujangasana (Cobra Pose): Relocates slipped discs, keeps the spine supple, and tones ovaries/uterus.
  • Shalabhasana (Locust Pose): Stimulates the parasympathetic nervous system, relieves lower backache.

Supine Asanas (Lying on Back)

  • Pawanmuktasana (Wind-Relieving Pose): Helps eliminate toxic gases from the abdomen, relieves constipation.
  • Setubandhasana (Bridge Pose): Strengthens back muscles, stretches the colon, and improves lung capacity.
  • Viparitkarani (Inverted Pose): Improves blood circulation to the brain, balances thyroid and thymus glands.
  • Shavasana (Corpse Pose): Deep relaxation posture that removes physical and mental fatigue, managing high blood pressure and anxiety.
Pranayama, Bandhas, and Dhyana

Pranayama (Breathing Regulation)

Derived from Prana (vital force) and Ayama (restraint/regulation). It consists of three distinct phases:

  • Puraka: Gentle, deep, and conscious inhalation.
  • Kumbhaka: Retention of breath (internal or external).
  • Rechaka: Controlled, prolonged exhalation (ideally twice the duration of Puraka).

Key Pranayama Practices:

  • Nadishodhana (Anuloma-Viloma): Alternate nostril breathing that purifies the energy channels (Nadis), balances brain hemispheres, and calms the mind. Maintains an inhalation-to-exhalation ratio of $1:1$.
  • Ujjayi Pranayama: Involves contracting the throat to produce a soft, continuous whispering sound during inhalation. Removes throat phlegm and cools the head.

Bandhas (Yogic Locks)

Bandhas are locks applied to direct and channelize the flow of Prana:

  • Jalandhara Bandha (Throat Lock): Performed by contracting the throat and pressing the chin against the jugular notch. Improves thyroid function.
  • Uddiyana Bandha (Abdominal Lock): Performed by pulling the abdominal muscles upward and inward toward the spine after complete exhalation. Tones and strengthens all abdominal organs.

Dhyana (Meditation)

A state of continuous, focused attention on a single point, mantra, or object. It serves as the gateway to the inner world within Ashtanga Yoga.

  • Benefits: Activates the parasympathetic nervous system, normalizes heart rate, controls blood pressure, and reduces anxiety.
