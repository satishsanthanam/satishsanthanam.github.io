Physical and Health Education: Class 11 (Part 11) — Chapter 11: Adventure Sports

Introduction to Adventure Sports

Adventure Sports are extraordinary activities performed by individuals in search of excitement, extra enthusiasm, and a desire to explore nature. These sports satisfy robust human behavior and are broadly classified into three categories:

  • Adventure Water Sports (e.g., rafting, surfing)
  • Adventure Land Sports (e.g., mountaineering, trekking)
  • Adventure Air Sports (e.g., paragliding, jumping)

This chapter focuses on two exciting and classical adventure sports: Paragliding and Surfing.

Paragliding

Paragliding is a recreational and competitive adventure sport for flying paragliders. It is an engine-less, motor-free sport that requires courage and quick decision-making.

Historical Development of Paragliding

  • 1954: Domina C. Jalbert invented advanced governable gliding parachutes with multi-cells and controls for lateral glide (patented the parafoil in 1963).
  • Flight Magazine Article: Walter Neumark predicted that a glider pilot would launch by running over a cliff edge or down a slope.
  • Pierre Lemoigne: French engineer who produced improved parachute designs leading to the Para-commander.
  • 1965: David Barish developed the "sail wing" for slope-soaring as a summer activity for ski resorts.
  • 1985: Canadian authors Patrick Gilligan and Bertrand Dubois wrote "The Paragliding Manual", officially coining the term "Paragliding".
  • 1989: The first official Paragliding World Championship was held in Austria.

Classification of Paragliding

  1. Light-weight gliding
  2. Free flying glider
  3. Foot-launched glider: An aircraft with no rigid primary structure, launched by the pilot running.

Sitting Position & Flight Dynamics

The pilot sits in a harness suspended below a fabric wing comprising interconnected baffled cells. The wing shape is maintained by suspension lines. Despite having no engine, flights can last many hours and cover hundreds of kilometers by exploiting sources of lift to climb to altitudes of a few thousand meters.

Instruments Used in Paragliding

  • Variometer: Helps the pilot find and stay in the "core" of a thermal to maximize height gain, or indicates sinking air. It uses audio signals (beeps) and visual displays to show climb/sink rates and altitude.
  • Radio: Used in training to communicate with other pilots, report landing intentions, or occasionally talk to air traffic control.
  • GPS (Global Positioning System): Essential for competitions to track flight paths, analyze flying techniques, determine wind drift, avoid restricted airspace, and aid retrieval teams.

Flying Techniques & Controls

Launching and landing are always done into the wind. Paragliders do not "jump" to launch.

Launching Techniques:

  1. Forward launch: Used in low winds; the pilot runs forward with the wing behind so air pressure inflates it.
  2. Reverse launch: Used in higher winds; the pilot faces the wing to bring it up into a flying position, then turns around to run and complete the launch.

Landing:

Pilots lose height by flying a figure-of-8 pattern over the landing zone, line up into the wind at full speed, and "stall" (pause) the glider about a meter above the ground to land.

Control Mechanism:

Controlled via brakes and an accelerator attached to the paraglider, known as the speed bar mechanism. Brakes are held in the pilot's hands to adjust speed.

Types of Competitions

  1. Cross-country flying: The classical form of paragliding competition.
  2. Aerobatic competitions: Pilots perform maneuvers individually or in synchronous pairs.
  3. Bivouac flying: Competitors fly or hike along a certain route over several days.
Surfing

Surfing is a surface water sport in which a wave rider (surfer) rides on the forward or deep face of a moving wave that carries them towards the shore. It was inducted into the Olympic Games starting in the year 2020.

Historical Development of Surfing

  • Surfing was central to ancient Polynesian culture and was first observed by British explorers in Tahiti in 1767.
  • George Freeth (1883–1919) is credited as the "Father of Modern Surfing" and is thought to be the first modern surfer.
  • The International Surfing Association (ISA) is the highest governing body. Professional contests began in 1975, with Margo Oberg becoming the first female professional surfer.

Types of Surfing

  1. Stand-up Surfing: The surfer rides a wave standing up on a surfboard. Includes long surf-boarding and short surf-boarding.
  2. Body Surfing or Body Boarding: Riding a wave on a body board (lying on the belly or drop knee), or using only the surfer's body without a board (considered the purest form of surfing).
  3. Surf Matting: Surfing on inflatable mats using foils.
  4. Tow-surfing: Associated with big waves where a motorized watercraft (like a jet ski) tows the surfer into a large, fast-moving wave front.

Dangers during Surfing

  • Seabed: Falling can cause the wave to toss the surfer downward, leading to violent collisions with shallow reefs or sandbeds.
  • Rip currents: Strong water channels flowing away from the shore. Swimmers or surfers can be carried out beyond breaking waves. Escaping requires paddling parallel to the shore.

Surfing-related Sports

  • Non-wave dependent: Paddle boarding, Sea kayaking
  • Wind-powered: Kite surfing, Wind-surfing
  • Boat-powered: V-drive boat wave surfing (surfing on the wake of a boat)
Safety and Security in Paragliding and Surfing

Due to the high risks involved in adventure sports, the following safety measures must be strictly observed:

  1. A paraglider must be certified and highly skillful.
  2. Carry essential safety instruments: Variometer, GPS, and Radio.
  3. Wear proper protective equipment: helmet, knee guards, chest guards, etc.
  4. Gliding must only be done under the assistance of licensed personnel.
  5. Surfers must recognize sea wave currents to stay in safe zones.
  6. Surfers must identify the dangerous deep face of moving waves.
  7. Surfers should practice skills in artificial wave pools before entering the deep sea.
  8. Regularly inspect paraglider brakes and lining.
  9. Ensure sufficient practice before attempting long-route paragliding.
  10. Maintain high physical and mental fitness standards for quick decision-making.
