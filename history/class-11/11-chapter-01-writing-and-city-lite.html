History: Class 11 — Chapter 1: Writing and City Life

Theme 1: Writing and City Life

In this section, we will read about early societies, tracing back to the beginnings of human existence millions of years ago. We explore how humans first emerged in Africa, how they transitioned from nomadic hunter-gatherer lifestyles to settled agriculture, and how the earliest cities developed in Mesopotamia (present-day Iraq) around temples and trade routes.

How to Read Timelines

As you study the timelines, remember:

  • Processes through which ordinary women and men have shaped history are far more difficult to date than events such as a war between kings.
  • Some dates indicate the beginning of a process, or when it reaches maturation.
  • Historians constantly revise dates in the light of new evidence.
  • While divided geographically for convenience, actual historical developments often transcend these divisions.

1. Mesopotamia and its Geography

Mesopotamia—derived from the Greek words mesos (middle) and potamos (river)—is the land between the Euphrates and the Tigris rivers, now part of the Republic of Iraq. It is celebrated for its prosperity, city life, rich literature, mathematics, and astronomy.

The geography of Iraq is highly diverse:

  • North-East: Green, undulating plains rising to tree-covered mountain ranges with clear streams and enough rainfall for agriculture, which began here between 7000 and 6000 BCE.
  • North: A stretch of upland called a steppe, where animal herding offers a livelihood. Sheep and goats feed on grasses and low shrubs after winter rains.
  • East: Tributaries of the Tigris provide routes of communication into the mountains of Iran.
  • South: A desert region where the first cities and writing emerged. The Euphrates and Tigris carry loads of fertile silt, depositing it when they flood or when their waters are channeled into fields.

2. The Significance of Urbanism

Cities and towns are not just places with large populations. They develop when an economy advances in spheres other than food production, making it advantageous for people to cluster in towns. Urban economies comprise trade, manufactures, and services. City dwellers cease to be self-sufficient and depend on the products or services of others, establishing a continuous interaction and a clear division of labour.

The Warka Head

This famous woman’s head was sculpted in white marble at Uruk before 3000 BCE. The eyes and eyebrows were likely inlaid with lapis lazuli (blue), shell (white), and bitumen (black). It is admired for its delicate modeling of the cheeks, mouth, and chin, and was sculpted from hard stone imported from a great distance.

3. Movement of Goods into Cities

While southern Mesopotamia was rich in food resources, its mineral resources were scarce. Most parts lacked stones for tools, seals, and jewels. The wood of the local date-palm and poplar was not sturdy enough for carts, wheels, or boats, and there was no metal for tools or vessels. Consequently, Mesopotamians traded their abundant textiles and agricultural produce for wood, copper, tin, silver, gold, shell, and stones from Turkey, Iran, or across the Gulf.

Efficient transport is vital for urban development. Carrying goods on pack animals or bullock carts over land was expensive and slow. The cheapest mode of transport was over water, using river boats and barges propelled by river currents or wind along the canals and natural channels of the Euphrates.

4. The Development of Writing

All societies have languages to convey meaning through spoken sounds. Writing is also verbal communication, but it represents spoken sounds through visible signs. The first Mesopotamian tablets, written around 3200 BCE, contained picture-like signs and numbers representing lists of goods (oxen, fish, bread loaves) brought into or distributed from the temples of Uruk.

Mesopotamians wrote on tablets of clay. A scribe would wet clay, pat it into a comfortable size, and smoothen its surfaces. Using the sharp end of a reed cut obliquely, the scribe would press wedge-shaped (cuneiform, from Latin cuneus meaning "wedge" and forma meaning "shape") signs onto the moist clay. Once dried in the sun, the clay hardened, making the tablet almost indestructible.

By 2600 BCE, the letters became cuneiform and the language was Sumerian. Writing was used for keeping records, making dictionaries, giving legal validity to land transfers, and narrating the deeds of kings. Sumerian was gradually replaced by Akkadian around 2400 BCE.

5. Literacy and the Uses of Writing

Very few Mesopotamians could read and write, as there were hundreds of complex signs to master. Writing reflected the spoken language. A letter from an official to a king would be read aloud, beginning with: "To my lord A, speak: Thus says your servant B..."

The connection between city life, trade, and writing is illustrated in the Sumerian epic poem about Enmerkar, one of the earliest rulers of Uruk. Enmerkar wanted lapis lazuli and precious metals to beautify a temple and sent his messenger to the chief of Aratta. The messenger traveled across mountain ranges but was unable to get the chief to agree. When the messenger became "weary of mouth" and mixed up the messages, Enmerkar formed a clay tablet and wrote the words down—marking the legendary origin of writing on clay.

6. Urbanisation in Southern Mesopotamia: Temples and Kings

From 5000 BCE, settlements began to develop in southern Mesopotamia. The earliest cities emerged from these settlements and were of two main types: those that developed around temples, and those that grew as centers of trade.

Early settlers built and rebuilt temples at selected spots. The earliest known temple was a small shrine made of unbaked bricks. Temples were the residences of various gods, such as the Moon God of Ur, or Inanna, the Goddess of Love and War. Over time, temples became larger, with several rooms around open courtyards. The temple functioned as the main urban institution, organizing agricultural production, employing merchants, and keeping written records of distributions of grain, animals, bread, beer, and fish.

7. Life in the City

A ruling elite emerged in Mesopotamian cities, securing a major share of the society's wealth. This is evident from the lavish goods (jewellery, gold vessels, ceremonial daggers) buried with kings and queens at Ur. For ordinary people, legal texts show that the nuclear family was the norm, with the father serving as the head of the household.

At Ur, ordinary houses were systematically excavated. The town lacked planning, featuring narrow, winding streets where wheeled carts could not pass. Sacks of grain and fuel arrived on donkey-back. There were no street drains; instead, clay pipes and sumps were located in inner courtyards to collect rainwater from sloping roofs. Residents swept household refuse directly into the streets, causing street levels to rise over time.

8. A Trading Town in a Pastoral Zone: Mari

After 2000 BCE, the royal capital of Mari flourished upstream on the Euphrates. Mari was situated in a region where agriculture and animal rearing were practiced close to one another. While some communities had both farmers and pastoralists, most of the territory was used for pasturing sheep and goats.

This close proximity led to both exchange and conflict. Herders exchanged young animals, cheese, leather, and meat for grain and metal tools. However, mobile herdsmen could raid agricultural villages or ruin crops by driving flocks across sown fields. Through history, nomadic groups from the western desert filtered into the agricultural heartland as herders, harvest laborers, or soldiers, with some gaining power to establish their own rule, such as the Akkadians, Amorites, Assyrians, and Aramaeans.

The Palace at Mari of King Zimrilim (1810-1760 BCE)

The great palace of Mari was the residence of the royal family, the hub of administration, and a place of production for precious metal ornaments. It was famous across the region, featuring 260 rooms and covering an area of 2.4 hectares. Daily lists show vast quantities of food presented for the king's table, where he dined with others in a white-paved courtyard.

9. The Legacy of Writing

The greatest legacy of Mesopotamia to the world is its scholarly tradition of time reckoning and mathematics. Tablets dating to around 1800 BCE contain multiplication and division tables, square and square-root tables, and compound interest tables. For example, the square root of 2 was calculated as:

$$1 + rac{24}{60} + rac{51}{60^2} + rac{10}{60^3}$$

This yields $1.41421296$, which is remarkably close to the correct value of $1.41421356$.

Mesopotamians also established the division of the year into 12 months based on the moon's revolution around the earth, the month into four weeks, the day into 24 hours, and the hour into 60 minutes. These divisions were adopted by the successors of Alexander, transmitted to the Roman world, and eventually passed to medieval Europe.

An Early Library

In the iron age, the Assyrian king Assurbanipal (668-627 BCE) collected a vast library at his capital, Nineveh. He gathered tablets on history, epics, astrology, hymns, and poems, sending scribes south to copy old tablets. The library housed around 1,000 texts, totaling about 30,000 tablets, carefully catalogued by subject.

And, an Early Archaeologist!

Nabonidus, the last ruler of independent Babylon, was instructed in a dream to appoint a high priestess at Ur. To find the correct attire and features of the office, which had been forgotten, he excavated and found a stele of an early king from c. 1150 BCE. On another occasion, his men found a broken statue of Sargon, the king of Akkad (c. 2370 BCE), which Nabonidus carefully repaired out of reverence for the past.
