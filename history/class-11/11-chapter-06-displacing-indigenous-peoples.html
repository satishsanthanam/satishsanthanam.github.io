History: Class 11 — Chapter 6: Displacing Indigenous Peoples

Section IV: Towards Modernisation (Introduction)

This section introduces the transition of the medieval and early modern world into the modern era, driven by two monumental global developments: the Industrial Revolution and a series of democratic political revolutions.

The Dual Engines of Modernity
  • Political Transformations: The transition of subjects into rights-bearing citizens began with the American Revolution (1776–81) and the French Revolution (1789–94), establishing the doctrine of popular sovereignty.
  • Industrial Trajectories: Britain emerged as the first industrial nation, initially driven by coal and cotton textiles, followed by railways. Other nations like Russia bypassed early stages, starting directly with heavy industry and state-led railway expansion in the late 19th century.
  • Human and Ecological Costs: Early industrialisation caused severe exploitation of child labour, extreme urban poverty, and deadly epidemics of cholera and tuberculosis . In Japan, industrial pollution led to devastating cadmium and mercury poisoning, sparking early mass environmental movements.
Imperialism, Capitalism, and Nationalism
  • Imperial Typologies: Western and Japanese capitalisms established diverse colonial systems: direct imperial control (e.g., British rule in India), settler colonies (e.g., America, Australia), and semi-colonial exploitation without direct state takeover (e.g., Western and Japanese intervention in China).
  • Civic vs. Ethnic Nationalism: Civic nationalism defines nationhood through shared citizenship and universal rights regardless of ethnicity or gender. Ethnic nationalism builds solidarity around a single language, religion, or ethnic identity (e.g., German reaction to French occupation in 1806).
  • Diverse Paths to Modernity: Japan successfully resisted colonisation, achieving rapid industrialisation and universal primary education by 1910, despite internal tensions between militarism and democracy. China overthrew foreign imperialism and domestic elites through peasant mobilisation led by the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) in 1949, later transitioning into an economic powerhouse by adopting market principles.
Theme 6: Displacing Indigenous Peoples

This theme explores the history of the native peoples of North America and Australia, detailing their displacement by European settlers and their subsequent struggles for civil rights and cultural recognition.

European Imperialism and Settler Colonialism
  • Global Expansion: Following the decline of Spanish and Portuguese expansion after the 17th century, France, Holland, and England extended mercantile empires. In South Asia, the East India Company established political control, collecting land taxes and building railways to extract resources.
  • The Settler Concept: The term settler applies to European immigrants who established permanent agricultural and urban communities in South Africa (Dutch), Ireland, New Zealand, Australia (British), and the Americas.
  • Toponymic Legacy: Despite the marginalisation of native populations, indigenous names survive in major geographical features, including Ohio , Mississippi , and Seattle in the USA; Saskatchewan in Canada; and Wollongong and Parramatta in Australia.

North America: Native Societies and European Encounters
Indigenous Lifestyles and Ecological Harmony
  • Origins: The earliest inhabitants migrated from Asia over 30,000 years ago across a land-bridge over the Bering Straits , moving south during the last Ice Age. The oldest recovered artifact is an 11,000-year-old arrow-point.
  • Socio-Economic Systems: Native populations lived in bands along river valleys. They practiced subsistence farming (maize and vegetables) and hunted bison . They did not practice intensive agriculture, produce economic surpluses, or establish centralized empires.
  • Cultural Values: Land was viewed as a shared ecological resource rather than private property. Formal alliances were sealed by exchanging wampum belts (made of coloured shells). Native cultures valued gift exchange over commercial transactions and possessed rich oral histories.
The Fur Trade and Mutual Perceptions
  • Economic Exchange: In the 17th century, European traders exchanged blankets, iron vessels, guns, and alcohol for fish and furs. Native hunters, expert at tracking, willingly assisted the traders.
  • Asymmetrical Perceptions: Europeans defined civilization by literacy, organized religion, and urbanism, viewing the natives as "uncivilised" or as "noble savages" (as conceptualized by Jean-Jacques Rousseau). To the natives, goods were gifts of friendship; to Europeans, they were commodities for profit.
  • Ecological and Social Disruption: European demand for furs led to the mass slaughter of beavers, distressing the natives who feared spiritual retribution. The introduction of alcohol caused widespread addiction, allowing Europeans to dictate unfair terms of trade.
The Loss of Land, Gold Rush, and Industrial Growth
Territorial Expansion and Forced Displacement
  • Imperial Expansion: The USA expanded its territory through massive land purchases, such as the Louisiana Purchase from France and Alaska from Russia, and through military victories over Mexico, completely ignoring native consent.
  • The Trail of Tears: Despite adopting English and assimilating to American lifestyles, the Cherokee tribe of Georgia was denied citizenship rights. In 1832, US Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that the Cherokees were a distinct sovereign community. President Andrew Jackson refused to respect the judgment, ordering the US Army to evict 15,000 Cherokees to the Great American Desert; over a quarter died on this forced march.
  • Reservations: Native tribes were systematically confined to small, isolated tracts of land called reservations . Armed resistance, such as the US Army campaigns (1865–90) and the Metis rebellions in Canada (1869–85), was brutally suppressed.

"How can you buy or sell the sky, the warmth of the land? The idea is strange to us. If you do not own the freshness of the air and the sparkle of the water, how can one buy them? Every part of the earth is sacred to my people..."

Analytical Historical Context & Interpretation: This excerpt from the 1854 letter of native leader Chief Seattle to the US President highlights the fundamental ideological clash between indigenous ecological communalism and the European capitalist concept of private property and land commodification.

The Gold Rush and Industrialisation
  • The Gold Rush: The discovery of gold in California in the 1840s triggered a massive influx of European immigrants. This drove the rapid construction of transcontinental railways, completed in the USA by 1870 and in Canada by 1885, utilizing thousands of Chinese laborers.
  • Industrial Revolution in North America: Unlike Britain, where industrialisation was driven by land enclosure and peasant displacement, North American industrialisation developed to manufacture railway equipment and agricultural machinery for large-scale farming.
  • Ecological Devastation: By 1890, the wild bison was nearly exterminated to clear land for farming, ending the traditional hunting lifestyle of the plains Indians. The invention of barbed wire in 1873 secured private agricultural properties, ending the open frontier.
Constitutional Rights and the Winds of Change
  • Exclusionary Democracy: Early American democratic and property rights were strictly limited to white men. The southern economy relied heavily on African slave labour, leading to the American Civil War (1861–65) and the formal abolition of slavery.
  • The Meriam Report (1928): This landmark social survey exposed the horrific health and educational conditions in native reservations, prompting the Indian Reorganisation Act of 1934 , which allowed reservations to buy land and secure loans.
  • Modern Rights Assertion: In 1954, the Declaration of Indian Rights asserted citizenship without the loss of reservation autonomy. In Canada, native activism defeated the 1969 government proposal to end aboriginal rights, culminating in the Constitution Act of 1982 , which formally recognized existing treaty rights.
Australia: Colonisation, Impact, and Redress
Indigenous Society and the British Penal Colony
  • Deep History: Australian Aborigines arrived from New Guinea over 40,000 years ago via a land-bridge. They lived in over 350 distinct linguistic communities. Their historical consciousness is rooted in the Dreamtime , a concept where past and present are interconnected.
  • The Penal Experiment: Following the loss of the American colonies, Britain used Australia as a penal colony. The first fleet of British convicts arrived in Sydney in 1788. Released convicts were granted land, leading to the violent displacement of native populations.
  • Demographic Collapse: Nearly 90% of the indigenous population died in the 19th and 20th centuries due to exposure to European germs, loss of ancestral lands, and frontier violence.

"Aboriginal production had been dramatically disturbed by the British presence. The arrival of a thousand hungry mouths, followed by hundreds more, put unprecedented pressure on local food resources... To them such large-scale destruction of sacred places and strange, violent behaviour towards their land was inexplicable."

Analytical Historical Context & Interpretation: This passage from Creating a Nation describes the immediate ecological shock and cultural trauma experienced by the Daruk people of Sydney in 1790, as British settlers cleared forests and disrupted sacred sites without any comprehensible ritual or economic justification.

Economic Development and Racial Exclusion
  • Pastoral Economy: Australian economic development centered on vast sheep farms, mining, vineyards, and wheat farming. The capital city, Canberra (derived from the native word kamberra , meaning "meeting place"), was established in 1911.
  • The White Australia Policy: Fearing non-white immigration, the government banned Chinese immigrants in the late 19th century and maintained a strict policy to keep out South and Southeast Asian immigrants until 1974.
The Struggle for Historical and Legal Recognition
  • Challenging the Silence: Anthropologist W.E.H. Stanner 's 1968 lecture "The Great Australian Silence" and Henry Reynolds' book Why Weren't We Told? criticized historians for ignoring Aboriginal history. This led to the adoption of official multiculturalism in 1974.
  • The Mabo Case (1992): The Australian High Court legally rejected the doctrine of terra nullius (land belonging to no one), recognizing native land claims prior to 1770.
  • The Stolen Generations: Inquiries exposed the systematic, forced separation of mixed-blood children from their Aboriginal families. This led to the establishment of National Sorry Day on May 26, 1999, as a public apology for these historical injustices.
