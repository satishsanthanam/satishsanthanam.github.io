Bricks, Beads and Bones: The Harappan Civilisation

Theme One: Bricks, Beads and Bones

The Harappan Civilisation

The Harappan seal is possibly the most distinctive artefact of the Harappan or Indus Valley civilisation. Made of a stone called steatite, seals like this often contain animal motifs and signs from a script that remains undeciphered. Yet, we know a great deal about the lives of the people who lived in the region from what they left behind – their houses, pots, ornaments, tools, and seals – in other words, from archaeological evidence.

Terminologies, Places and Time

  • The Harappan Civilisation is also known as the Indus Valley Civilisation. The term Harappa is derived from the place where the civilisation was first identified.
  • The total time span of the civilisation ranges from 6000 BCE to 1300 BCE.
  • Early Harappan (6000 BCE - 2600 BCE): A formative phase of the civilisation.
  • Mature Harappan (2600 BCE - 1900 BCE): The urban, most prosperous phase.
  • Late Harappan (1900 BCE - 1300 BCE): The decadent, declining phase.
  • Geographical Spread: Distinctive pottery, bricks, seals, weights, and beads have been found as far apart as Afghanistan, Baluchistan, Sind, Punjab, Jammu and Kashmir, Haryana, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat, and Maharashtra.

Key Abbreviations

  • BP: Before Present
  • BCE: Before Common Era
  • CE: Common Era
  • c.: Latin word circa, meaning "approximate."

1. Beginnings

There were several archaeological cultures in the region prior to the Mature Harappan. These cultures were associated with distinctive pottery, evidence of agriculture and pastoralism, and some crafts. Settlements were generally small, and there were virtually no large buildings. Recent archaeological evidence indicates the emergence of Harappan culture from early farming communities dated around 7000 BCE. The urban phase (Mature Harappan) was a result of gradual transformation and internal development.

Harappan Settlements Overview

So far, more than 2000 Harappan archaeological sites have been discovered in the Indian subcontinent. Most are located between the Indus and the Saraswati river basins. Nearly two-thirds of these settlements are in the Saraswati basin, suggesting its immense importance. Five major cities have been identified:

  • Rakhigarhi
  • Mohenjodaro
  • Harappa
  • Dholavira
  • Ganweriwala

The remaining sites are categorized as Regional Centres, agricultural villages, ports, and manufacturing centres.

2. Subsistence Strategies

The Harappans consumed a wide range of plant and animal products, including fish. Archaeologists have reconstructed dietary practices from finds of charred grains and seeds, studied by archaeo-botanists (specialists in ancient plant remains).

  • Grains found: Wheat, barley, lentil, chickpea, and sesame. Millets are found at sites in Gujarat. Finds of rice are relatively rare.
  • Animal remains: Bones of cattle, sheep, goat, buffalo, and pig. Studies by archaeo-zoologists indicate these animals were domesticated. Bones of wild species such as boar, deer, and gharial, as well as fish and fowl, have also been found.

2.1 Agricultural Technologies

While the prevalence of agriculture is indicated by grain finds, reconstructing actual practices is more complex:

  • Representations on seals and terracotta sculptures indicate that the bull was known, and oxen were likely used for ploughing.
  • Terracotta models of the plough have been found at sites in Cholistan and at Banawali (Haryana).
  • Evidence of a ploughed field has been found at Kalibangan (Rajasthan), showing two sets of furrows at right angles, suggesting two different crops were grown together.
  • Most Harappan sites are located in semi-arid lands where irrigation was required. Traces of canals have been found at Shortughai (Afghanistan), but not in Punjab or Sind. Water drawn from wells and stored in reservoirs (such as in Dholavira, Gujarat) was also used for agriculture.
Source 1: How Artefacts are Identified

"Saddle querns … are found in considerable numbers … and they seem to have been the only means in use for grinding cereals. As a rule, they were roughly made of hard, gritty, igneous rock or sandstone and mostly show signs of hard usage... Two main types have been found: those on which another smaller stone was pushed or rolled to and fro, and others with which a second stone was used as a pounder..."

3. Mohenjodaro: A Planned Urban Centre

The development of planned urban centres is a unique feature of the Harappan civilisation. Mohenjodaro is the most well-known site, though Harappa was the first to be discovered.

The settlement was divided into two sections:

  1. The Citadel: Smaller but higher, built on mud-brick platforms and physically walled off from the rest of the city.
  2. The Lower Town: Much larger but lower, also walled. Buildings were constructed on platforms serving as foundations, requiring massive mobilization of labor.

Standardized bricks (whether sun-dried or baked) with a ratio of 4:2:1 (length:breadth:height) were used across all Harappan settlements.

The Plight of Harappa

Although Harappa was the first site discovered, it was badly destroyed by brick robbers. In 1875, Alexander Cunningham (the first Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India) noted that the amount of brick taken from the site was enough to lay bricks for "about 100 miles" of the railway line between Lahore and Multan. Consequently, Mohenjodaro was far better preserved.

3.1 Laying Out Drains

One of the most distinctive features of Harappan cities was the carefully planned drainage system. Roads and streets were laid out along an approximate "grid" pattern, intersecting at right angles. Streets with drains were laid out first, and then houses were built along them, requiring every house to have at least one wall along a street to connect to the domestic waste system.

3.2 Domestic Architecture

Residential buildings in the Lower Town of Mohenjodaro were centered on a courtyard with rooms on all sides. Key features included:

  • Privacy: No windows in the walls along the ground level, and main entrances did not give a direct view of the interior.
  • Sanitation: Every house had its own bathroom paved with bricks, with drains connected to the street drains.
  • Infrastructure: Remains of staircases to reach a second storey or roof, and numerous wells (estimated at about 700 in Mohenjodaro) accessible to passers-by.

3.3 The Citadel

The Citadel contains structures probably used for special public purposes:

  • The Warehouse: A massive structure where the lower brick portions remain, while the upper wooden portions decayed long ago.
  • The Great Bath: A large rectangular tank in a courtyard surrounded by a corridor on all four sides. It was made watertight using bricks set on edge and gypsum mortar. It featured steps on the north and south, rooms on three sides (including one with a large well), and was likely used for special ritual baths.

4. Tracking Social Differences

4.1 Burials

To identify social or economic differences, archaeologists study burials. At Harappan sites, the dead were generally laid in pits. Some pits were lined with bricks, and some graves contained pottery and ornaments (such as shell rings, jasper beads, and micro-beads), indicating a belief in the afterlife. However, on the whole, the Harappans did not believe in burying precious things with the dead.

4.2 Looking for "Luxuries"

Archaeologists classify artefacts into two categories:

  • Utilitarian: Objects of daily use made easily from ordinary materials (e.g., querns, pottery, needles, flesh-rubbers).
  • Luxuries: Rare objects made from costly, non-local materials or with complicated technologies (e.g., little pots of faience used as perfume bottles).

5. Finding Out About Craft Production

Chanhudaro was a tiny settlement (less than 7 hectares) almost exclusively devoted to craft production, including bead-making, shell-cutting, metal-working, seal-making, and weight-making.

  • Materials used: Stones (carnelian, jasper, crystal, quartz, steatite), metals (copper, bronze, gold), and shell, faience, and terracotta.
  • Bead-making techniques: Steatite (a very soft stone) was easily molded from paste. Carnelian's red color was obtained by firing yellowish raw material. Specialized drills have been found at Chanhudaro, Lothal, and Dholavira.
  • Shell production: Nageshwar and Balakot were specialized coastal centres for making shell objects like bangles, ladles, and inlay.

6. Strategies for Procuring Materials

The Harappans used various land routes (using bullock carts) and riverine/coastal routes to procure raw materials:

  • Shortughai (Afghanistan): Source of highly valued blue stone, lapis lazuli.
  • Lothal (Gujarat): Source of carnelian.
  • Rajasthan & Gujarat: Sources of steatite and metal.
  • Khetri region (Rajasthan): Source of copper (associated with the archaeological Ganeshwar-Jodhpura culture).
  • South India: Source of gold.

6.2 Contact with Distant Lands

Chemical analyses show that both Omani copper and Harappan artefacts contain traces of nickel, suggesting a common origin. Mesopotamian texts datable to the third millennium BCE refer to copper coming from a region called Magan (likely Oman) and mention contacts with Dilmun (Bahrain) and Meluhha (the Harappan region). Meluhha is described as a land of seafarers, and Harappan seals depict ships and boats.

7. Seals, Script, Weights

  • Seals and Sealings: Used to facilitate long-distance communication and secure bags of goods. An intact sealing assured the receiver that the contents had not been tampered with.
  • An Enigmatic Script: Harappan script remains undeciphered. It was not alphabetical and had between 375 and 400 signs, written from right to left.
  • Weights: Regulated by a precise system of weights made of a stone called chert. They were cubical with no markings, utilizing binary lower denominations (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 up to 12,800) and decimal higher denominations.

8. Ancient Authority

The extraordinary uniformity of Harappan artefacts (pottery, seals, weights, and bricks) and planned settlements suggests complex decision-making. Theories on political power include:

  • There were no rulers, and everyone enjoyed equal status.
  • There was no single ruler, but several (e.g., Mohenjodaro had one, Harappa another).
  • There was a single state, given the high level of planning and standardization.

A stone statue found at Mohenjodaro is conventionally labeled the "priest-king", drawing parallels to Mesopotamian history, though its political role remains speculative.

9. The End of the Civilisation

By c. 1800 BCE, most Mature Harappan sites in regions like Cholistan were abandoned. Population expanded into new settlements in Gujarat, Haryana, and western Uttar Pradesh. Late Harappan material culture was marked by the disappearance of distinctive weights, seals, writing, and long-distance trade. Proposed causes for the collapse include:

  • Climatic change and deforestation
  • Excessive floods
  • Shifting and/or drying up of rivers
  • Overuse of the landscape
Source 3: The Myth of the "Invasion"

Historically, scholars like R.E.M. Wheeler tried to link skeletal remains in Mohenjodaro's "Deadman Lane" to an Aryan invasion mentioned in the Rigveda. However, in the 1960s, George Dales demonstrated that the skeletons did not belong to the same archaeological period, and there was no evidence of a final military defense or destruction level, proving the "massacre" was mythical.

Recent Archaeogenetic Research (Rakhigarhi)

Recent DNA analysis of skeletal remains from Rakhigarhi (Haryana) indicates that the Harappans are the indigenous people of this region, with genetic roots going back to 10,000 BCE. The study rules out large-scale immigration of the so-called Aryans and shows an unbroken genetic continuity of 5000 years in the region.

10. Discovering the Harappan Civilisation

  • Cunningham's Confusion: Alexander Cunningham, the first Director-General of the ASI, missed the significance of Harappa because he believed Indian history began with the first cities in the Ganga valley. He tried to fit Harappan artefacts into the early historic timelines of Chinese Buddhist pilgrims.
  • John Marshall's Announcement: In 1924, John Marshall, Director-General of the ASI, announced the discovery of a new civilisation in the Indus valley to the world. However, his method of excavating along regular horizontal units ignored the site's stratigraphy, leading to a loss of valuable contextual data.
  • R.E.M. Wheeler's Precision: In 1944, Wheeler rectified this by introducing stratigraphic excavation methods, bringing military precision to Indian archaeology.

11. Problems of Piecing Together the Past

Since the Harappan script is undeciphered, archaeologists rely entirely on material evidence (stone, clay, metal). Organic materials like cloth, leather, and wood decompose in tropical climates.

  • Classifying Finds: Archaeologists classify objects by material (stone, clay, metal) and function (tool, ornament, ritual object), often using present-day analogies.
  • Problems of Interpretation: Reconstructing religious beliefs is highly speculative. Unusual objects like heavily jeweled female terracotta figurines are classified as "mother goddesses", and seals depicting a figure in a yogic posture surrounded by animals are interpreted as "proto-Shiva". Conical stone objects are classified as lingas.
