History: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 2: Kings, Farmers and Towns: Early States and Economies (c. 600 BCE-600 CE)

Theme Two: Kings, Farmers and Towns

Early States and Economies (c. 600 BCE - 600 CE)

There were several developments in different parts of the subcontinent during the long span of 1,500 years following the end of the Harappan civilisation. This was also the period during which the Rigveda was composed by people living along the Indus and its tributaries. Agricultural settlements emerged in many parts of the subcontinent, including north India, the Deccan Plateau, and parts of Karnataka. Besides, there is evidence of pastoral populations in the Deccan and further south. New modes of disposal of the dead, including the making of elaborate stone structures known as megaliths, emerged in central and south India from the first millennium BCE. In many cases, the dead were buried with a rich range of iron tools and weapons.

From c. sixth century BCE, there is evidence of other trends as well. Perhaps the most visible was the emergence of early states, empires and kingdoms. Underlying these political processes were other changes, evident in the ways in which agricultural production was organised. Simultaneously, new towns appeared almost throughout the subcontinent.

Key Vocabulary & Concepts

  • Epigraphy: The study of inscriptions.
  • Janapada: The land where a jana (a people, clan or tribe) sets its foot or settles. It is a word used in both Prakrit and Sanskrit.
  • Oligarchy: A form of government where power is exercised by a group of men.
  • Gahapati: The owner, master or head of a household, who exercised control over the women, children, slaves and workers who shared a common residence.
  • Agrahara: Land granted to a Brahmana, who was usually exempted from paying land revenue and other dues to the king, and was often given the right to collect these dues from the local people.
  • Votive Inscriptions: Inscriptions that record gifts made to religious institutions.
  • Numismatics: The study of coins, including visual elements such as scripts and images, metallurgical analysis and the contexts in which they have been found.

1. Prinsep and Piyadassi

Some of the most momentous developments in Indian epigraphy took place in the 1830s. This was when James Prinsep, an officer in the mint of the East India Company, deciphered Brahmi and Kharosthi, two scripts used in the earliest inscriptions and coins. He found that most of these mentioned a king referred to as Piyadassi – meaning “pleasant to behold”; there were a few inscriptions which also referred to the king as Asoka, one of the most famous rulers known from Buddhist texts.

This gave a new direction to investigations into early Indian political history as European and Indian scholars used inscriptions and texts composed in a variety of languages to reconstruct the lineages of major dynasties that had ruled the subcontinent.

2. The Earliest States

2.1 The Sixteen Mahajanapadas

The sixth century BCE is often regarded as a major turning point in early Indian history. It is an era associated with early states, cities, the growing use of iron, the development of coinage, etc. It also witnessed the growth of diverse systems of thought, including Buddhism and Jainism. Early Buddhist and Jaina texts mention sixteen states known as mahajanapadas. Although the lists vary, some names such as Vajji, Magadha, Koshala, Kuru, Panchala, Gandhara and Avanti occur frequently.

While most mahajanapadas were ruled by kings, some, known as ganas or sanghas, were oligarchies, where power was shared by a number of men, often collectively called rajas. Both Mahavira and the Buddha belonged to such ganas.

Each mahajanapada had a capital city, which was often fortified. From c. sixth century BCE onwards, Brahmanas began composing Sanskrit texts known as the Dharmasutras. These laid down norms for rulers, who were ideally expected to be Kshatriyas.

2.2 First Amongst the Sixteen: Magadha

Between the sixth and the fourth centuries BCE, Magadha (in present-day Bihar) became the most powerful mahajanapada. Modern historians explain this development in a variety of ways:

  • Magadha was a region where agriculture was especially productive.
  • Iron mines (in present-day Jharkhand) were accessible and provided resources for tools and weapons.
  • Elephants, an important component of the army, were found in forests in the region.
  • The Ganga and its tributaries provided a means of cheap and convenient communication.

Early Buddhist and Jaina writers attributed its power to the policies of ruthlessly ambitious kings of whom Bimbisara, Ajatasattu and Mahapadma Nanda are the best known. Initially, Rajagaha (modern Rajgir in Bihar) was the capital of Magadha. Later, in the fourth century BCE, the capital was shifted to Pataliputra (modern Patna).

3. An Early Empire

The growth of Magadha culminated in the emergence of the Mauryan Empire. Chandragupta Maurya, who founded the empire (c. 321 BCE), extended control as far northwest as Afghanistan and Baluchistan, and his grandson Asoka conquered Kalinga (present-day coastal Orissa).

3.1 Finding out about the Mauryas

Historians use a variety of sources to reconstruct Mauryan history:

  • Archaeological finds, especially sculpture.
  • Contemporary works, such as the account of Megasthenes (a Greek ambassador to the court of Chandragupta Maurya).
  • The Arthashastra, parts of which were composed by Kautilya or Chanakya (traditionally believed to be the minister of Chandragupta).
  • Later Buddhist, Jaina, and Puranic literature, as well as Sanskrit literary works.
  • The inscriptions of Asoka on rocks and pillars.
Source 1: What the king’s officials did

Here is an excerpt from the account of Megasthenes: “Of the great officers of state, some … superintend the rivers, measure the land, as is done in Egypt, and inspect the sluices by which water is let out from the main canals into their branches... They collect the taxes, and superintend the occupations connected with land; as those of the woodcutters, the carpenters, the blacksmiths, and the miners.”

3.2 Administering the Empire

There were five major political centres in the empire – the capital Pataliputra and the provincial centres of Taxila, Ujjayini, Tosali and Suvarnagiri, all mentioned in Asokan inscriptions. Administrative control was likely strongest in areas around the capital and the provincial centres, which were carefully chosen along key trade and resource routes.

Source 2: Capturing elephants for the army

The Arthashastra lays down minute details of administrative and military organisation. It describes how guards of elephant forests, assisted by those who rear and enchain elephants, trace the whereabouts of herds of wild elephants by following their tracks, dung, and urine, using female elephants to help tether them.

4. New Notions of Kingship

4.1 Chiefs and kings in the south

The new kingdoms that emerged in the Deccan and further south, including the chiefdoms of the Cholas, Cheras and Pandyas in Tamilakam (the ancient Tamil country), proved to be stable and prosperous. We know about these states from early Tamil Sangam texts, which contain poems describing chiefs and the ways they acquired and distributed resources.

Source 3: The Pandya chief Senguttuvan visits the forest

An excerpt from the Silappadikaram, an epic written in Tamil, describes forest dwellers coming down the mountain singing and dancing, bringing gifts such as ivory, fragrant wood, honey, sandalwood, spices, fruits, and animals to show respect to the victorious king.

4.2 Divine Kings

One means of claiming high status was to identify with a variety of deities. This strategy is best exemplified by the Kushanas (c. first century BCE - first century CE), who ruled over a vast kingdom extending from Central Asia to northwest India. They projected their power through coins, colossal sculptures, and titles like devaputra (“son of god”).

By the fourth century, larger states like the Gupta Empire emerged. Many of these depended on samantas, local power-holders who offered homage and military support to rulers. Histories of the Gupta rulers have been reconstructed from literature, coins, and inscriptions, including prashastis (eulogies composed by poets in praise of kings).

Source 4: In praise of Samudragupta

An excerpt from the Prayaga Prashasti (composed in Sanskrit by Harishena, the court poet of Samudragupta): “He was without an antagonist on earth; he, by the overflowing of the multitude of (his) many good qualities adorned by hundreds of good actions, has wiped off the fame of other kings with the soles of (his) feet...”

5. A Changing Countryside

5.1 Popular perceptions of kings

Ordinary people rarely left written accounts of their thoughts. Historians reconstruct popular perceptions by examining oral tales recorded in anthologies like the Jatakas and the Panchatantra. For example, the Gandatindu Jataka describes the plight of subjects under a wicked king, showing that relations between rulers and ruled were often strained due to high tax demands.

5.2 Strategies for increasing production

To meet growing tax demands, agricultural production was increased through several strategies:

  • A shift to plough agriculture, which spread in fertile alluvial river valleys like the Ganga and Kaveri from c. sixth century BCE.
  • The introduction of transplantation for paddy cultivation, which dramatically increased yields.
  • The use of irrigation through wells, tanks, and canals, constructed by both communities and individual rulers.
Source 5: The Sudarshana Lake in Gujarat

A Sanskrit rock inscription (c. second century CE) records the achievements of the Shaka ruler Rudradaman, who repaired the artificial reservoir Sudarshana Lake using his own resources without imposing taxes on his subjects after a terrible storm broke its embankments.

5.3 Differences in rural society

While agricultural technologies increased production, the benefits were uneven. There was growing social differentiation among those engaged in agriculture:

  • Landless agricultural labourers, small peasants, and large landholders.
  • In Buddhist texts, the term gahapati was used to designate second and third categories.
  • In early Tamil literature (Sangam texts), different categories included large landowners (vellalar), ploughmen (uzhavar), and slaves (adimai).

5.4 Land grants and new rural elites

From the early centuries of the Common Era, records of land grants (mostly to Brahmanas or religious institutions) were inscribed on stone or copper plates. A notable example is Prabhavati Gupta, daughter of Chandragupta II, who made land grants despite Sanskrit legal texts generally restricting women's independent access to land.

Source 8: Prabhavati Gupta and the village of Danguna

In her inscription, Prabhavati Gupta commands the householders, Brahmanas, and others of the village of Danguna that she has donated the village to the Acharya Chanalasvamin, granting exemptions typical of an agrahara, such as freedom from entry by soldiers and police, and exemption from various taxes and dues.

6. Towns and Trade

6.1 New cities

Urban centres emerged across the subcontinent from c. sixth century BCE. Many were capitals of mahajanapadas and were located along major communication routes (e.g., Pataliputra on riverine routes, Ujjayini along land routes, and Puhar near the coast).

6.2 Urban populations: Elites and craftspersons

Fortified cities housed ruling elites. Archaeological excavations have recovered artefacts such as Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW), ornaments, tools, and weapons. Short votive inscriptions record donations by various urban groups, including washing folk, weavers, scribes, potters, goldsmiths, and merchants organized into guilds or shrenis.

6.3 Trade in the subcontinent and beyond

From the sixth century BCE, extensive land and river routes connected the subcontinent to Central Asia, West Asia, North Africa, Southeast Asia, and China. Highly successful merchants, known as masattuvan in Tamil and setthis or satthavahas in Prakrit, traded goods like salt, grain, textiles, timber, medicinal plants, and spices (especially pepper, which was in high demand in the Roman Empire).

6.4 Coins and kings

Exchanges were facilitated by the introduction of coinage:

  • Punch-marked coins of silver and copper (c. sixth century BCE onwards) were the earliest to be minted.
  • The first coins bearing the names and images of rulers were issued by the Indo-Greeks (c. second century BCE).
  • The Kushanas issued the first gold coins (c. first century CE), identical in weight to Roman and Parthian coins.
  • The Guptas issued highly pure gold coins that facilitated long-distance transactions.

7. How Are Inscriptions Deciphered?

7.1 Deciphering Brahmi

Most modern Indian scripts are derived from Brahmi. In 1838, James Prinsep completed the decipherment of Asokan Brahmi after decades of painstaking comparison with contemporary Bengali and Devanagari scripts.

7.2 How Kharosthi was read

Kharosthi, used in northwestern inscriptions, was deciphered using bilingual coins of Indo-Greek kings containing names written in both Greek and Kharosthi scripts.

7.3 Historical evidence from inscriptions

Historians compare different inscriptions to match titles like devanampiya (“beloved of the gods”) and piyadassi (“pleasant to behold”) with historical figures like Asoka, while carefully evaluating the truthfulness and context of the claims made within them.

8. The Limitations of Inscriptional Evidence

Epigraphy has several limitations:

  • Technical issues: letters may be faintly engraved, damaged, or missing.
  • Reconstruction challenges: the exact meaning of words can be uncertain or highly specific to a time and place.
  • Selective recording: inscriptions usually record grand, unique events of those who commissioned them, leaving out routine agricultural practices and the daily lives of ordinary people.
