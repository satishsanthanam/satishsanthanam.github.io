History: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 2: Bhakti-Sufi Traditions

Changes in Religious Beliefs and Devotional Texts (c. eighth to eighteenth century)

Introduction and Methodology

By the mid-first millennium CE, the landscape of the subcontinent was dotted with a variety of religious structures—stupas, monasteries, temples. Beyond architectural evidence, complex belief systems are reconstructed using Puranas and oral compositions attributed to poet-saints. These compositions were expressed in regional vernacular languages and later compiled by disciples. Historians also utilize hagiographies (saints' biographies written by followers) to gauge how these devotional figures were perceived.

1. A Mosaic of Religious Beliefs and Practices

This phase is marked by the intensive integration of diverse local cults alongside extended worship of major deities like Vishnu, Shiva, and the goddess.

1.1 Intellectual Categorization & The Integration of Cults
  • Sociological Framework: Twentieth-century sociologist Robert Redfield coined the terms great traditions (rituals emanating from dominant category elites like priests/kings) and little traditions (localized peasant customs). These categories constantly influenced one another through ongoing interaction.
  • Puranic Dissemination: Brahmanas compiled Puranic texts in simple Sanskrit verse to make them accessible to women and Shudras, who were traditionally excluded from Vedic learning.
  • The Jagannatha Model: At Puri (Orissa), a local tribal deity whose cult image was constructed of wood by tribal specialists was integrated into the Puranic layout and formally recognized as a form of Vishnu (Jagannatha).
  • Goddess Incorporation: Local goddess cults—often worshipped as an ochre-smeared stone—were brought into the fold by identifying them as wives of major male deities (e.g., Lakshmi paired with Vishnu, Parvati with Shiva).
1.2 Strifts, Divergences, and Tantric Intersections
  • Tantric Modalities: Goddess worship was heavily associated with Tantric practices, which were open to all genders and explicitly ignored caste/class distinctions within a ritual context. These ideas strongly influenced Shaivism and Buddhism across northern and eastern India.
  • Vedic vs. Puranic Tension: Vedic pantheon deities (Agni, Indra, Soma) became marginal, while newer Puranic mythologies grew dominant. Orthodox groups adhering to closely regulated Vedic sacrifices often clashed with Tantric and ecstatic sectarian movements.
2. Poems of Prayer: Early Traditions of Bhakti

Bhakti traditions are broadly classified by historians into two analytical frameworks: saguna (devotion focused on specific deities conceptualized in anthropomorphic forms) and nirguna (worship of an abstract, formless ultimate reality).

2.1 The Alvars and Nayanars of Tamil Nadu

Led by the Alvars (those immersed in devotion to Vishnu) and the Nayanars (devotees of Shiva), early movements (c. sixth century CE) traveled across the Tamil landscape singing hymns to identify specific shrines as sacred abodes.

  • The Tamil Veda: The major anthology of Alvar compositions, the Nalayira Divyaprabandham, was explicitly claimed by devotees to be as scripturally significant as the four Sanskrit Vedas.
  • Caste Erasure: Devotees hailed from diverse strata, ranging from elite Brahmanas to artisans, cultivators, and communities traditionally branded as "untouchable".
2.2 Women Devotees & Social Defiance
  • Andal: A woman Alvar who expressed her intense devotion by viewing herself as the spiritual beloved of Vishnu.
  • Karaikkal Ammaiyar: A dedicated Nayanar devotee of Shiva who adopted extreme asceticism, actively flouting patriarchal expectations of passive domestic beauty.

Source 3: A demon? "The female Pey (demoness) with... bulging veins, protruding eyes, white teeth and shrunken stomach... shouts and wails while wandering in the forest... This is the forest of Alankatu, which is the home of our father (Shiva)..." — Karaikkal Ammaiyar

Analytical Historical Context & Interpretation: By using highly unconventional imagery to depict her body, Karaikkal Ammaiyar systematically subverted standard societal norms of feminine presentation to establish an autonomous, direct connection with Shiva.

2.3 Patronage Dynamics with the Chola State

Powerful Chola rulers (ninth to thirteenth centuries) built monumental stone temples (at Chidambaram, Thanjavur, and Gangaikondacholapuram) to win support from the agrarian peasantry who revered the poet-saints.

  • Rulers organized the compilation of Tamil Shaiva hymns into the authoritative text known as the Tevaram.
  • Inscriptional evidence from c. 945 CE indicates that King Parantaka I consecrated metal bronze images of Appar, Sambandar, and Sundarar to be carried in public religious processions.
3. The Virashaiva Tradition in Karnataka
Basavanna, Lingayats, and Social Reform

The twelfth century witnessed a radical movement in Karnataka led by a Brahmana minister named Basavanna (1106–68). His followers were known as Virashaivas or Lingayats.

  • Ritual Regimes: Lingayats wear a miniature silver-cased linga over the left shoulder and follow wandering monks known as jangamas. Believing that devotees are united with Shiva upon death, they reject cremation rituals and ceremonially bury their dead.
  • Anti-Brahmanical Reform: They challenged the ideas of caste pollution and rebirth, attracting marginalized social groups. They also promoted practices rejected by the Dharmashastras, such as post-puberty marriage and the remarriage of widows.
  • The Vachana Corpus: Historical understanding of this movement is drawn from vachanas, which are direct, oral sayings composed in Kannada by ordinary women and men.

Source 4: Rituals and the real world: "When they see a serpent carved in stone they pour milk on it. If a real serpent comes they say: 'Kill. Kill.'... But to the image of the god which cannot eat they offer dishes of food." — Basavanna

Analytical Historical Context & Interpretation: This vachana critiques hypocritical external rituals, championing direct spiritual authenticity and human care over inanimate stone idols.

4. Religious Ferment in North India
Non-Conformist Ascetics and the Coming of the Sultanate

Unlike South India, northern regions show little evidence of institutionalized vernacular bhakti movements until the fourteenth century, primarily due to the strong position of Brahmana advisors within Rajput states.

  • Artisanal Non-Conformists: Leaders like the Naths, Jogis, and Siddhas gained ground among artisanal communities (such as weavers) in expanding urban trade nodes. They openly questioned Vedic authority using vernacular idioms.
  • The Turkish Catalyst: The establishment of the Delhi Sultanate (thirteenth century) weakened traditional Rajput configurations and Brahmanical dominance, setting the stage for new religious developments and the arrival of Sufism.
5. New Strands in the Fabric: Islamic Traditions
5.1 Administrative Categories: Zimmi, Ulama, and Shari'a
  • The Governance Matrix: Following Arab general Muhammad Qasim's conquest of Sind in 711 CE, Muslim dynasties established states across the subcontinent. While guided by the ulama (scholars of Islamic studies) to enforce the shari'a (law derived from the Qur'an, hadis, qiyas, and ijma), rulers adopted flexible policies for non-Muslims.
  • The Zimmi Status: Non-Muslim subjects were classified as zimmi (protected peoples). By paying a tax called jizya, their right to internal cultural autonomy and protection was guaranteed by the state—a status extended to Hindus as well. Rulers like Akbar and Aurangzeb issued land grants and tax exemptions to non-Muslim shrines.
5.2 Popular Acculturation and Mosque Architecture

All converts accepted the five fundamental pillars of Islam (Monotheism, Namaz prayers, Zakat alms, Sawm fasting during Ramzan, and the Hajj pilgrimage). However, these universal features were often integrated with local customary traditions.

  • The Ismaili Ginan: The Khojahs (a Shi'a branch) communicated Quranic ideas through indigenous genres called ginan—devotional poems composed in Punjabi, Sindhi, and Gujarati, sung in classical ragas.
  • Malabar Adaptations: Arab traders along the Malabar coast adopted the Malayalam language and embraced local systems like matriliny and matrilocal residence.
  • Architectural Hybrids: While mosques retained universal features like the mihrab (prayer niche facing Mecca), building materials and roofs varied locally, as seen in the wooden, spire-topped architecture of the Shah Hamadan mosque in Srinagar.
6. The Growth of Sufism
Khanqahs, Silsilas, and Dargah Devotion

Sufis turned to mysticism (tasawwuf) to protest the growing materialism of the early Caliphate, emphasizing salvation through intense personal love for God and emulation of the Prophet.

  • Hospice Networks: Sufis organized communities inside a hospice or khanqah, managed by a master titled a shaikh, pir, or murshid, who trained disciples (murids) and appointed successors (khalifas).
  • Genealogies & Ziyarat: Sufi orders or silsilas (literally, chains) traced a spiritual lineage back to the Prophet. Upon a master's death, his tomb-shrine or dargah became a center for pilgrimage or ziyarat, especially on his death anniversary (urs, signifying the soul's marriage union with God).
  • Be-shari'a Mystics: In contrast to ba-shari'a groups who complied with Islamic law, radical mendicants like the Qalandars or Madaris deliberately defied the shari'a and lived as wandering ascetics.
7. The Chishtis in the Subcontinent

The Chishtis became the most influential sufi order in India by successfully adapting to local environments and embracing indigenous devotional traditions.

7.1 Life Inside the Jama'at Khana

Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya's 14th-century khanqah at Ghiyaspur (on the outskirts of Delhi) featured a large central hall (jama'at khana) where inmates and visitors lived and prayed together.

  • Futuh Economy: The open kitchen or langar was funded entirely by unasked-for charity or futuh, serving everyone from soldiers and slaves to Hindu jogis.
  • Acculturation Rituals: To connect with local traditions, they adopted practices like bowing before the Shaikh, shaving the heads of initiates, and incorporating yogic exercises.
7.2 Chishti Devotionalism: Ziyarat, Qawwali, and Royal Patronage
  • Gharib Nawaz: The dargah of Khwaja Muinuddin Sijzi at Ajmer is the most revered Chishti shrine. It attracted early visits from Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlaq and extensive funding from the Sultans of Malwa.
  • Akbar's Pilgrimages: Emperor Akbar visited the Ajmer shrine fourteen times, offering gifts like a massive cooking cauldron (degh) and commissioning a compound mosque.
  • Sama' and Zikr: Devotion was expressed by reciting God's names (zikr) and performing mystical music or sama'. Scribes like Amir Khusrau formalized this style by introducing the qaul opening hymn to create modern qawwali performance systems.
7.3 Vernacular Literature and State Intersections
  • Masnavis & Dakhani Poetry: Sufis used local languages like Hindavi or composed long allegorical poems (masnavis) like Malik Muhammad Jayasi's Padmavat. In the Deccan, short poems in Dakhani (like charkhanama spinning songs) helped integrate Islamic concepts into rural settings.
  • Legitimation & Conflict: Sultans granted tax-free lands (inam) and trusts (auqaf) to khanqahs to secure religious legitimacy. Tension flared over protocols of authority, as seen when Nizamuddin's followers addressed him as sultan-ul-mashaikh (Sultan amongst shaikhs).
8. New Devotional Paths: Dialogue and Dissent in Northern India

Poet-saints in North India engaged in explicit debates, systematically challenging entrenched social structures and conventional religious institutions.

8.1 Weaving a Divine Fabric: Kabir

Active around the 14th–15th centuries, Kabir stands out as a key figure of non-conformist devotion. His verses were preserved across three main overlapping text archives: the Kabir Bijak, the Kabir Granthavali, and the Adi Granth Sahib.

  • Sant Bhasha & Ulatbansi: Wrote in the vernacular sant bhasha dialect or utilized ulatbansi (upside-down sayings), inverted expressions designed to highlight the difficulty of capturing ultimate reality in words.
  • Syncretic Imagery: He described the Ultimate Reality by drawing on both Islamic monotheism (Allah, Khuda, Hazrat, Pir) and Vedantic terms (Alakh, Nirakar, Brahman, Atman).
  • Hagiographical Debates: Vaishnava hagiographies written centuries later claimed he was born a Hindu but raised by a poor Muslim weaver family (julahas), identifying his guru as Ramananda.
8.2 Baba Guru Nanak and the Sacred Word

Born in 1469 into a merchant family at Nankana Sahib, Baba Guru Nanak advocated a strict form of nirguna bhakti, rejecting sacrifices, image worship, and the formal scriptures of both Hindus and Muslims.

  • Shabad & Sangat: He conceptualized the Divine as formless (rab) and introduced the collective singing of hymns (shabad) accompanied by the rabab instrument. He established rules for congregational worship (sangat).
  • The Granth Compilation: The fifth preceptor, Guru Arjan, compiled these hymns along with verses by Baba Farid, Ravidas, and Kabir into the Adi Granth Sahib. The tenth preceptor, Guru Gobind Singh, added Guru Tegh Bahadur's compositions to create the finalized Guru Granth Sahib. He also founded the military Khalsa Panth, defining its five symbols (uncut hair, dagger, shorts, comb, steel bangle).
8.3 Mirabai, the Devotee Princess

Mirabai (c. 15th–16th centuries) is the best-known woman poet of the bhakti tradition. A Rajput princess from Merta, she rejected her forced marriage to a prince of Mewar, recognizing Krishna as her true spiritual lover.

  • Caste Defiance: Traditions name her preceptor as Raidas, a leather worker, demonstrating her active defiance of caste boundaries. Her emotional songs remain highly popular among lower-caste communities in Gujarat and Rajasthan.
9. Historiographical Methodologies & Source Typologies

Historians analyze a wide range of texts produced around sufi khanqahs and bhakti circles to reconstruct this dynamic history:

  1. Sufi Treatises: Manuals like Ali Hujwiri's 11th-century Kashf-ul-Mahjub explain the operational meaning of tasawwuf.
  2. Malfuzat: Collections of the conversations of sufi saints. A primary example is the Fawa'id-al-Fu'ad, which records Nizamuddin Auliya's conversations as compiled by Persian poet Amir Hasan Sijzi.
  3. Maktubat: Compiled volumes of letters written by sufi masters to their disciples, such as the 17th-century letters of Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi.
  4. Tazkiras: Biographical accounts of saints, like the 14th-century Siyar-ul-Auliya or Abdul Haqq's 17th-century Akhbar-ul-Akhyar.
