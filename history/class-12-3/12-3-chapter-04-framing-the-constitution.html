History: Class 12 (Book 3) — Chapter 4: Framing the Constitution: The Beginning of a New Era

A Tumultuous Prelude to Constitution-Making

The years immediately preceding the drafting of the Indian Constitution were marked by intense political mobilization, hope, and severe communal trauma. The Constituent Assembly met in a highly volatile atmosphere, shaped by the aftermath of the war, popular struggles, and the partition of the subcontinent.

The Post-War Crisis and Popular Upsurges
  • The Quit India Movement (1942): A widespread, aggressive popular struggle against the British Raj that remained fresh in public memory.
  • Armed Struggles: The military campaign led by Subhas Chandra Bose to win freedom through armed struggle with foreign assistance.
  • The Royal Indian Navy (RIN) Mutiny (1946): A major uprising of naval ratings in Bombay and other cities in the spring of 1946, which evoked massive public sympathy and demonstrated strong Hindu-Muslim unity.
  • Peasant and Labor Protests: Periodic mass protests of workers and peasants across different parts of the country throughout the late 1940s.
The Tragedy of Partition and Communal Violence
  • Failure of Political Reconciliation: The Indian National Congress and the Muslim League repeatedly failed to reach a settlement on religious reconciliation and power-sharing.
  • The Great Calcutta Killings (August 1946): Marked the beginning of a year of continuous, brutal rioting across northern and eastern India.
  • Mass Migration and Massacres: The announcement of Partition led to unprecedented violence, forced displacement, and massacres of millions of refugees (Muslims moving to East/West Pakistan; Hindus and Sikhs moving to India).
The Problem of the Princely States
  • Ambiguous Constitutional Status: Under the British Raj, approximately one-third of the subcontinent was ruled by nawabs and maharajas who owed allegiance to the British Crown.
  • Threat of Balkanization: With the withdrawal of the British, many rulers harbored ambitions of declaring independent power, threatening the territorial integrity of the new nation.
Structure and Composition of the Constituent Assembly

The Constituent Assembly was not elected through universal adult franchise but was formed through indirect election by the provincial legislatures chosen in the winter of 1945–46.

Party Dynamics and Public Participation
  • Congress Dominance: The Congress held 82% of the seats in the Assembly, as the Muslim League chose to boycott the sessions to press its demand for Pakistan.
  • Internal Diversity of Congress: The Congress was not a monolithic party; it included socialists, defenders of landlordism, secularists, and those close to communal groups.
  • Public Debates and Submissions: Assembly deliberations were reported in newspapers and debated publicly. The public was invited to send submissions, allowing linguistic minorities, religious groups, and Dalits to voice their demands for safeguards and social justice.

The Dominant Voices of the Assembly
  • The Congress Trio:
    • Jawaharlal Nehru : Moved the crucial Objectives Resolution and the resolution for the National Flag.
    • Vallabhbhai Patel : Worked behind the scenes, drafting reports and reconciling opposing viewpoints.
    • Rajendra Prasad : Served as the President of the Assembly, steering discussions along constructive lines.
  • The Legal Experts:
    • B.R. Ambedkar : Served as the Chairman of the Drafting Committee. Despite being a long-time political opponent of the Congress, he was invited by Mahatma Gandhi to join the cabinet as Law Minister.
    • K.M. Munshi (Gujarat) and Alladi Krishnaswamy Aiyar (Madras): Provided critical legal inputs during the drafting process.
  • The Civil Servants:
    • B. N. Rau : Constitutional Advisor to the Government of India, who prepared background papers based on global political systems.
    • S. N. Mukherjee : Chief Draughtsman, highly skilled in translating complex political proposals into precise legal language.
The Visionary Framework: Objectives Resolution

On 13 December 1946, Jawaharlal Nehru introduced the Objectives Resolution , outlining the defining ideals of the Constitution of independent India.

Core Ideals of the Resolution
  • Proclaimed India as an "Independent Sovereign Republic".
  • Guaranteed justice, equality, and freedom to all citizens.
  • Assured adequate safeguards for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed classes.
Creative Adaptation vs. Blind Copying

"We are not going just to copy... We seek to learn from their success and to avoid their failures... whatever system of government we may establish here must fit in with the temper of our people and be acceptable to them."

Analytical Historical Context & Interpretation: Nehru located the Indian constitutional experiment within the global history of struggles for liberty (referencing the American, French, and Russian Revolutions). However, he emphasized that India would not mechanically copy Western democratic models. Instead, the objective was to fuse liberal democratic ideas with socialist principles of economic justice, adapting them creatively to the Indian context.

The Imperial Shadow and Popular Sovereignty
  • The Communist Critique: Communist member Somnath Lahiri warned that the Assembly was operating under the shadow of British imperialism, as the British military and Viceroy still held ultimate authority during the interim administration.
  • Nehru's Response: Nehru acknowledged the limitations imposed by the British but asserted that the Assembly derived its true strength not from state papers, but from the will and passions of the Indian masses.
  • Historical Roots of Rights: The ideals of democracy, equality, and justice were not British concessions; they were forged through decades of social reform (e.g., campaigns by Swami Vivekananda, Jyotiba Phule) and peasant-labor struggles.
The Debate Over Rights and Safeguards

Defining the rights of individual citizens and determining special protections for marginalized groups was one of the most intensely debated aspects of the Assembly.

The Question of Separate Electorates
  • The Plea for Separate Electorates: B. Pocker Bahadur (Madras) argued that separate electorates were essential to ensure that Muslims had a meaningful voice in governance and to prevent their political marginalization.
  • The Nationalist Opposition: Most nationalists strongly opposed separate electorates, viewing them as a British tool of division that had ultimately led to Partition.
    • Sardar Patel declared separate electorates to be a "poison that has entered the body politic of our country."
    • Govind Ballabh Pant argued that separate electorates would permanently isolate minorities, making them vulnerable and preventing them from integrating into the nation.
  • Internal Muslim Opposition: Some Muslim members, such as Begum Aizaz Rasul , also opposed separate electorates, arguing they were self-destructive and isolated minorities from the majority.
Defining "Minorities" in Economic and Social Terms
  • N.G. Ranga's Perspective: The socialist peasant leader argued that the real minorities were not defined by religion, but by poverty and oppression. He asserted that the poor and downtrodden needed "props" and a "ladder" to enjoy their constitutional rights.
  • Jaipal Singh and Tribal Rights: Representing the Adibasis, Jaipal Singh welcomed the Objectives Resolution but highlighted 6,000 years of neglect and dispossession. He demanded reservation of seats in the legislature to compel others to hear the tribal voice, rather than separate electorates.
Redressing Caste Oppression and Depressed Castes
  • The Nature of Untouchability: Members of the Depressed Castes (such as J. Nagappa and K.J. Khanderkar ) argued that their suffering was not due to numerical insignificance, but to systematic social marginalization, lack of education, and exclusion from administration.
  • Constitutional Remedies: The Assembly recommended:
    • The absolute abolition of untouchability.
    • Opening of Hindu temples to all castes.
    • Reservation of seats in legislatures and jobs in government offices for the lowest castes.
Division of Powers: Federalism vs. Centralization

The balance of power between the Central Government and the provincial states was a subject of vigorous debate, heavily influenced by the chaos of Partition.

The Case for a Strong Centre
  • Post-Partition Realities: Nehru and other leaders argued that a weak central authority would be incapable of maintaining peace, coordinating vital matters of common concern, or representing the country internationally.
  • The Three Lists: The Draft Constitution provided for three lists of subjects: Union (exclusive central control), State (provincial control), and Concurrent (shared responsibility). The Union list was heavily favored with key resources, minerals, and industries.
  • Article 356: Gave the Centre the power to take over a state's administration on the recommendation of the Governor.
The Defense of State Autonomy
  • K. Santhanam's Warning: K. Santhanam (Madras) argued that an overburdened Centre would become weak, not strong. He warned that fiscal centralization would impoverish the provinces, leaving them unable to carry out basic development projects (education, sanitation, roads) and potentially leading to a provincial revolt.
  • Fiscal Federalism: The Constitution created a complex tax-sharing system where the Centre retained lucrative taxes (customs, company taxes) while states relied on land revenue, sales tax, and liquor duties.
The Language Controversy

The debate over the national language was highly acrimonious, reflecting deep regional anxieties and cultural identities.

Hindustani as the National Ideal
  • Mahatma Gandhi's Vision: In the 1930s, the Congress accepted that Hindustani —a blend of Hindi and Urdu—should be the national language. Gandhi believed it was a composite, multi-cultural language that could easily be understood by common people and unify different communities.
  • The Split: Over time, communal conflicts led to the Sanskritisation of Hindi and the Persianisation of Urdu, causing the two languages to grow apart.
The Aggressive Plea for Hindi
  • R.V. Dhulekar's Stance: R. V. Dhulekar (United Provinces) made aggressive demands for Hindi to be used as the language of constitution-making and declared as the National Language, sparking intense opposition from non-Hindi-speaking members.
The Compromise Formula
  • The Language Committee's Solution: To resolve the deadlock, the Language Committee proposed a gradual transition:
    • Hindi in the Devanagari script would be the official language (not the national language).
    • English would continue to be used for all official purposes for the first fifteen years.
    • Each province was allowed to choose a regional language for official work within its territory.
  • Southern Concerns: G. Durgabai (Madras) and T.A. Ramalingam Chettiar warned that aggressive propaganda for Hindi created fear and suspicion in the South, pleading for mutual adjustment and caution.
Core Pillars of the New Republic & Timeline

Despite the intense debates, the Constitution emerged as a document of consensus, anchored by two revolutionary features.

Key Features
  • Universal Adult Franchise: The granting of the vote to every adult Indian was an unprecedented act of faith, contrasting with Western democracies where voting rights were granted slowly and in stages.
  • Secularism: The Constitution guaranteed freedom of religion (Articles 25-28), cultural and educational rights (Articles 29, 30), and equality (Articles 14, 16, 17). It established a "judicious distance" between State and religion rather than absolute separation, allowing legal space for social reforms like the abolition of untouchability.
