Section A: What Makes Me 'I'

2A.1 Introduction to Self and Identity

Every individual is unique, possessing a distinct sense of self that differentiates 'I' from 'you', 'them', and 'others'. The concepts of self, identity, and personality are closely related and often interchanged in common usage, though psychologists distinguish them by definition.

2A.2 What is Self?

  • Self: The totality of a person's experiences, ideas, thoughts, and feelings with regard to herself/himself; the characteristic way we define ourselves.
  • Dimensions of Self: Can be broadly categorized into:
    • Personal Self: Aspects relating solely to the individual (e.g., personal potential, capabilities, beliefs, bodily self, feelings, and emotions).
    • Social Self: Aspects where the individual is involved with others, including sharing, cooperation, support, unity, and social roles (e.g., son/daughter, student, friend).
  • Self-concept: A description of oneself that answers the question "Who am I?". It includes our qualities, feelings, thoughts, and capabilities.
  • Self-esteem: An individual's judgment or evaluation of themselves according to standards they have set, which are largely influenced by society.

2A.3 What is Identity?

Identity refers to the unmistakable sense of continuity and sameness that an individual maintains throughout life, despite physical, cognitive, and social changes.

  • Personal Identity: Attributes of a person that make her/him different from others.
  • Social Identity: Aspects of the person that link her/him to professional, social, or cultural groups (e.g., being Indian, Gujarati, Mizo, a teacher, or a farmer).
Section B: Development and Characteristics of the Self

2B.1 Self during Infancy

  • Self-awareness: Infants are not born with self-awareness; they do not initially realize they are separate from the outside world.
  • Self-recognition: Emerges gradually. Around 18 months of age, infants recognize their own self-image (demonstrated by the lipstick mirror test, where an infant with a red dot on their cheek touches their own face rather than the mirror reflection).
  • Pronoun Usage: By the latter half of the second year, infants begin using personal pronouns like "I", "me", and "mine" to indicate possession or describe actions.

2B.2 Self during Early Childhood (3 to 6 Years)

Young children's self-understanding is characterized by five main features:

  1. Physical Descriptions: Use of concrete physical attributes or material possessions (e.g., "I am tall", "I have a toy") in absolute terms without comparison to others.
  2. Active Descriptions: Defining themselves in terms of actions and play activities (e.g., "I can ride a cycle").
  3. Concrete Terms: Descriptions are visible and tangible (e.g., "I have a television").
  4. Overestimation: Unrealistic positive self-evaluations (e.g., "I am never scared").
  5. Single-Attribute Thinking: Inability to recognize that they can possess multiple or contradictory attributes (e.g., being both 'good' and 'bad' at different times).

2B.3 Self during Middle Childhood (6 to 11 Years)

Self-evaluations become more complex and realistic through five key shifts:

  1. Internal Characteristics: Shift from physical to psychological traits (e.g., "I am hard-working", "I am good at making friends").
  2. Social Descriptions: Defining oneself in terms of social groups (e.g., "I am in the school music choir").
  3. Social Comparisons: Differentiating oneself from others in comparative terms (e.g., "I can run faster than Kiran").
  4. Real vs. Ideal Self: Distinguishing between actual competencies and desired attributes.
  5. Realistic Self-descriptions: Increased accuracy in self-assessment due to the ability to see things from others' perspectives.

2B.4 Self during Adolescence

Adolescence is a critical period for identity development, often characterized by what psychologist Erik H. Erikson termed an identity crisis.

  • Identity Crisis Reasons:
    1. Intense preoccupation with trying to understand oneself.
    2. The need to establish a lasting, integrated sense of selfhood.
    3. Influence of rapid biological changes and changing social expectations.
  • Cultural Variations:
    • Western Cultures: Emphasize independence and separation from parents.
    • Non-Western/Indian Cultures: Emphasize interdependence, family connectedness, and collective identity ("we" instead of "I").
  • Role Confusion: Difficulty integrating newly emerging selves, leading to temporary inability to concentrate, schedule conflicts, or behavioral contradictions.
  • Characteristics of Adolescent Self-Understanding:
    1. Abstract Descriptions: Focus on inner, non-physical personality traits (e.g., sensitive, quiet, emotional).
    2. Contradictory Self-descriptions: Expressing opposing traits (e.g., "I am calm but get easily disturbed").
    3. Fluctuating Self: Self-understanding varies across different situations and times.
    4. Ideal vs. Real Self: Highly prominent ideal self-constructs, which can motivate or cause distress.
    5. Self-Consciousness: Preoccupation with self, creating an imaginary "being on stage" feeling where they believe everyone is watching them.
Section C: Influences on Identity

2C.1 Biological and Physical Changes

Adolescence is marked by universal physical and biological changes leading to sexual maturity.

  • Puberty: The attainment of sexual maturity. Marked by menarche in girls and spermatozoa production in boys.
  • Pubescence: The period of rapid physical and biological changes leading up to puberty (typically 11-13 years for girls, 13-15 years for boys).

2C.2 Socio-cultural Contexts

  • Traditional vs. Modern Societies: Traditional societies offer fewer occupational choices, leading to faster, less conflicted identity integration. Modern, technology-driven societies offer diverse choices, extending the training period and delaying adulthood, which can increase conflict with authority figures.
  • Family Influences:
    • Affectionate Parenting: Warm, supportive, and encouraging parenting combined with firm discipline fosters healthy identity, independence, and self-reliance.
    • Individuality and Connectedness: A healthy family atmosphere promotes both individuality (opportunity to have one's own point of view) and connectedness (sensitivity to and respect for others' views).
  • Peer Pressure: Adolescents have a strong need for peer acceptance. Peer values can conflict with parental values, leading to positive conformity or negative behaviors (e.g., smoking, bullying, substance use).

2C.3 Emotional and Cognitive Changes

  • Emotional Changes: Preoccupation with physical appearance, body image issues (e.g., acne, growth rate concerns), mood swings, and sudden bursts of anger as they process rapid developmental changes.
  • Cognitive Changes: Transition to formal operational thought, enabling:
    • Abstract thinking beyond immediate physical reality.
    • Hypothetical-deductive reasoning (imagining possibilities and outcomes).
    • Future-oriented planning, such as career path mapping.
Practicals

Practical 1: Study of One's Physical Self

Theme: Recording and analyzing physical growth parameters during adolescence to understand physical development and garment sizing.

  • Tasks: Record height, weight, hip size, waist, chest/bust, age of menarche (girls), growth of beard/voice change (boys), and hair/eye color.
  • Measurement Guidelines:
    • Around Hip: Widest portion of hips with two fingers between tape and body.
    • Around Bust/Chest: Fullest part of bust/chest, held firmly but not tight.
    • Around Waist: Smallest portion of the waistline with one finger clearance.
    • Around Neck: Base of the neck where the measurement settles.
    • Across Back: Between lateral ends of scapulae (shoulder blades).

Practical 2: Influences on Identity (Emotions)

Theme: Recording, reflecting on, and managing daily emotions to build emotional self-awareness.

  • Tasks: Maintain a daily log tracking time of day, specific emotion experienced, context/situation, personal reaction, and observations. Compare notes in peer groups to analyze common triggers and alternative coping mechanisms.
