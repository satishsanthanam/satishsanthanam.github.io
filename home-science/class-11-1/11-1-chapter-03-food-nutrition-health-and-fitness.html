3.1 Introduction and Core Definitions

The onset of adolescence brings profound physiological changes, including a dramatic growth spurt driven by hormonal activity. This makes healthy eating and understanding nutritional science critical during this developmental stage.

Core Concepts and Definitions

  • Food: Any solid or liquid substance which, when swallowed, digested, and assimilated, provides the body with essential nutrients and keeps it well. It is the basic necessity of life, supplying energy, enabling growth, repairing tissues, and regulating body functions.
  • Nutrition: The science of foods, nutrients, and other substances they contain, and of their actions within the body (including ingestion, digestion, absorption, metabolism, and excretion). It also encompasses social, psychological, and economic dimensions.
  • Nutrients: Constituents in food that must be supplied to the body in suitable amounts. They include carbohydrates, proteins, fats, minerals, vitamins, water, and fibre.

Classification of Nutrients

Nutrients are classified based on the quantity required by the body daily:

  • Macronutrients: Required in large amounts by the body. These include Carbohydrates, Proteins, Fats, Water, and Fibre/Roughage.
  • Micronutrients: Required in small amounts by the body. These include Vitamins and Minerals (such as Calcium, Iron, and Iodine).
3.2 Balanced Diet and Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA)

Balanced Diet

A Balanced Diet is one which includes a variety of foods in adequate amounts and correct proportions to meet the day's requirements of all essential nutrients, while providing a safety margin or reserve to withstand short durations of deprivation.

A balanced diet ensures the following:

  1. Includes a variety of food items.
  2. Meets the RDA for all nutrients.
  3. Includes nutrients in correct proportions.
  4. Provides a safety margin for nutrients.
  5. Promotes and preserves good health.
  6. Maintains acceptable body weight for height.

Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA)

The Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA) are nutrient guidelines formulated keeping extra allowances in mind to cover the needs of practically all healthy individuals. The relationship between requirements and RDA is expressed as:

$$\text{RDA} = \text{Requirements} + \text{Margin of safety}$$
3.3 Health and Fitness

Health

According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), Health is defined as "the state of complete physical, emotional, and social well-being, not merely the absence of diseases or infirmity."

  • Mental Health: A state of emotional and psychological well-being in which an individual is able to use their cognitive and emotional capabilities, function in society, and meet the ordinary demands of everyday life.

Physical Fitness

Physical Fitness is the body's ability to function efficiently and effectively in work and leisure activities, to be healthy, to resist diseases, and to meet emergency situations. It is divided into five categories:

  1. Aerobic fitness
  2. Muscular strength
  3. Muscular endurance
  4. Flexibility
  5. Body composition

Caloric Conversion Values

Energy release values per gram of macronutrient:

  • $1\text{ g Carbohydrate} = 4\text{ Kcal}$
  • $1\text{ g Protein} = 4\text{ Kcal}$
  • $1\text{ g Fat} = 9\text{ Kcal}$

Food Guide Pyramid

The Food Guide Pyramid is a graphic depiction of the daily food guide illustrating variety, moderation, and proportion:

  • Apex (Use Sparingly): Fats, oils, and sugars.
  • Middle-Upper Tier (2-3 Servings): Milk, yogurt, meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dry beans, and nuts.
  • Middle-Lower Tier (3-5 Servings): Vegetables and fruits.
  • Base (6-11 Servings): Bread, cereal, rice, and pasta.

Vegetarian Food Guide

Vegetarians rely on plant foods but can achieve nutritional adequacy by selecting meat alternatives like legumes, seeds, nuts, tofu, and fortified soy milk (fortified with calcium, vitamin D, and vitamin B12).

3.5 Dietary Patterns and Eating Behaviour in Adolescence

Adolescent eating habits are highly complex and influenced by both internal and external factors, often leading to nutritional vulnerabilities.

Common Adolescent Eating Habits

  • Irregular meals and skipping meals: Breakfast is the most frequently omitted meal, especially among girls due to the pursuit of thinness, which can slow metabolism and lead to weight gain.
  • Snacking: Frequent snacking is common; it can help maintain energy levels if healthy choices are made, but relying solely on snacks is harmful.
  • Fast foods: Highly popular in urban areas due to convenience and social factors, but typically high in fat, sodium, and empty calories.

Factors Affecting Food Behaviour

  • External Factors: Family unit, parenting practices, peers, social/cultural norms, mass media, fast foods, food fads, and personal experiences.
  • Internal Factors: Physiological needs, body image, self-concept, personal values, food preferences, psychosocial development, and health status.
3.6 Clinical Anomalies and Eating Disorders

Adolescence and Anaemia

Anaemia is a global health issue primarily caused by iron deficiency. It affects women and girls disproportionately.

  • According to the NFHS-3 Survey (2005-06), $56\%$ of adolescent girls and $30\%$ of adolescent boys in India are anaemic.
  • Causes include poverty, inadequate diet, repetitive pregnancy, lactation, and poor access to health services.
  • Interventions include iron-rich foods and iron supplementation.

Eating Disorders

  • Anorexia Nervosa: A psychological eating disorder characterized by extreme obsession with thinness, severe body image distortion, and self-starvation. It can lead to delayed menstruation, permanent stature minimization, osteoporosis, and even death.
  • Bulimia: An eating disorder characterized by cycles of bingeing (overeating) followed by purging (induced vomiting or laxative abuse). It can cause convulsions, renal failure, irregular heartbeats, and dental erosion.
3.7 Modifying Diet-Related Behaviour

Adolescents can adopt healthier lifestyles through targeted behavioural modifications:

  • Limiting television viewing: Restrict to 1-2 hours daily to prevent passive overeating and sedentary habits.
  • Healthy eating habits: Consume three balanced meals of average size and two nutritious, low-calorie snacks daily.
  • Drinking water: Drink 4-6 glasses of water daily, especially before meals, while avoiding high-calorie soft drinks and juices.
  • Diet journal: Maintain a weekly record of food intake, physical activity, and weight.
  • Exercise: Engage in regular physical activity for 20-30 minutes, 3-4 times a week (e.g., walking, cycling, swimming, yoga).
