Unit II Overview: Nutrition, Food Science and Technology

Food is more than a biological necessity; it contributes to cultural identity, social and religious practices, and serves as a symbol of hospitality, status, and power. Knowledge of food and nutrition is essential to ensure optimal body function and build resistance to infection.

The Double Burden of Malnutrition in India
  • Undernutrition: Historically high prevalence in India, leading to severe developmental and health challenges.
  • Overnutrition: Gradually increasing in recent years, leading to lifestyle disorders such as obesity, cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, and diabetes.
  • Sunrise Sector: The food processing industry in India is termed the 'sunrise sector' because India is a major producer of fruits, vegetables, and milk, yet wastes 1/5th to 1/3rd of its produce. Processing prevents spoilage and adds economic value.
Chapter 2: Clinical Nutrition and Dietetics

Clinical nutrition is the specialized area of nutrition dealing with the nutritional management of patients during illness. In recent times, this field is widely referred to as Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT).

Significance of Clinical Nutrition
  • Illness and medical treatments can impair a person's ability to eat, swallow, digest, absorb, metabolize, or excrete nutrients, leading to nutrient imbalances even in previously healthy individuals.
  • MNT uses a systematic, logical, and holistic approach to address each patient's unique nutritional needs, making the patient the primary focus of the care process.
  • With the rise of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) like diabetes, obesity, and heart disease occurring at younger ages, India is projected to become the diabetes capital of the world, increasing the demand for trained clinical nutritionists.

FSSAI Regulatory Classifications

According to the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), specialized food categories are defined as follows:

  • Nutraceuticals / Functional Foods: Foods specially processed or formulated to satisfy particular dietary requirements existing due to physical, physiological, or disease conditions. They may contain plants, botanicals, minerals, vitamins, proteins, or approved animal-origin substances.
  • Medical Foods: Specially manufactured products for dietary management of a disease under medical supervision, requiring a doctor's prescription.
  • Phytochemicals / Bioactive Compounds: Non-nutrient constituents present in foods that possess physiological or biological activity and influence health.
Basic Concepts in Diet Therapy

Diet Therapy is the branch of dietetics concerned with using diet as a therapeutic measure to prevent, manage, or treat disease conditions.

Objectives of Diet Therapy
  1. Formulate diets to meet the nutritional needs of the patient, keeping their food habits in mind.
  2. Modify existing diets to ameliorate and control the disease condition.
  3. Correct existing nutritional deficiencies.
  4. Prevent short-term and long-term complications in chronic illnesses.
  5. Educate and counsel patients to adhere to their prescribed therapeutic diets.

The Nutritional Care Process

Nutritional care during illness is an organized group of activities consisting of four key steps:

  1. Assessing Nutritional Status: Collecting detailed health, diet, personal, and medication histories; taking anthropometric measurements; and relating these to laboratory findings and physician diagnoses.
  2. Diagnosis of Nutritional Problems: Identifying specific nutritional deficiencies or risks.
  3. Planning and Prioritising Interventions: Designing customized dietary plans to meet specific nutritional needs.
  4. Monitoring and Evaluating Outcomes: Tracking progress and making necessary dietary adjustments.
Types of Diets and Feeding Routes

Classification of Diets
  • Standard / Regular Diet: Includes all food groups and meets the needs of healthy individuals; in hospitals, it is modified to be lower in fried foods, sweets, and strong spices.
  • Modified Diets: Adjusted to meet medical needs. Modifications can include changes in consistency, energy intake, nutrient levels, or meal frequency.

Dietary Consistency Modifications
  • Liquid Diets: Fluid at room temperature; highly digestible and free of fiber (e.g., coconut water, clear soups, milk, buttermilk). Clear liquid diets are prescribed immediately post-surgery but cannot meet complete nutritional needs long-term.
  • Soft Diets: Semi-solid foods that are lightly seasoned, easy to chew, and low in fiber/gas-forming agents (e.g., khichdi, sago kheer).
  • Mechanical Soft Diet: Specifically modified for elderly patients with chewing difficulties; includes mashed, pureed, and soft-cooked foods.
  • Therapeutic Soft Diet: Soft in consistency, containing only simple, easily digestible foods with no harsh fiber, high fat, or spices; used to rest the digestive system.

Feeding Routes
  • Oral Feeding: The most preferred and natural route of feeding.
  • Tube Feeding (Enteral Nutrition): Nutritionally complete feeds delivered via a tube introduced through the nose into the stomach (nasogastric tube); preferred if the gastrointestinal tract is functional but the patient cannot swallow.
  • Intravenous Feeding (Parenteral Nutrition): Infusing sterile nutrient solutions directly into a vein; used when the gastrointestinal tract is non-functional.
Preparing for a Career in Clinical Nutrition

Educational Pathways

To practice as a professional clinical nutritionist or dietitian, the standard educational progression is:

  1. Complete 10+2 level education.
  2. Obtain a B.Sc. degree in Home Science or B.Sc. with a specialization in Nutrition/Food Technology.
  3. Complete a Postgraduate Diploma in Dietetics along with a registered internship to qualify as a Registered Dietitian (RD).
  4. Alternatively, pursue an M.Sc. in Food Science and Nutrition or Dietetics to specialize further.
  5. Clear the National or State Eligibility Tests (NET/SLET) conducted by the UGC and complete a Ph.D. for academic and research careers.

Career Avenues

Professionals in this field can find employment in various sectors:

  • Dietitians with consultants/physicians in hospitals, health clubs, or gymnasiums.
  • Dietitians in institutional catering services (hospitals, schools, industrial canteens).
  • Entrepreneurs developing and supplying specialty foods for specific health purposes.
  • Academics, teachers, and researchers in universities and clinical research organizations.
  • Technical writers and nutrition marketing consultants.
