Knowledge Traditions And Practices Of India: Class 11 — Chapter 6: Mathematics in India

Historical Overview & Numerical Symbolism

Ancient Roots & Cultural Importance

  • Mohenjodaro (3000 BCE): Archaeological evidence reveals highly organized urban planning and advanced civilization along the Sindhu river.
  • Brāhmaṇa Literature (2000 BCE): Post-Vedic texts containing ritualistic and philosophical treatises, showing continuous intellectual progress.
  • Jainism: Highly valued mathematics, integrating gaṇita anuyoga (exposition of mathematical principles) and saṁkhyāna (science of numbers) as essential priestly accomplishments.
  • Buddhism: Regarded arithmetic (gaṇanā saṁkhyāna) as the first and noblest of arts.

Development of Numerical Symbolism

  • Decimal Base: Number ten has historically formed the basis of numeration in India.
  • High Numerals: Vedic works like the Yajurveda Saṁhitā express numerical denominations as large as $10^{12}$.
  • Brahmi Numerals: Purely Indian invention dating back to King Asoka (300 BCE); early notations for 1, 2, and 3 were represented by horizontal lines stacked vertically.
  • Discovery of Zero: Attributed to Āryabhaṭa I (born 496 CE) in connection with decimal place-value notation; represented as a circle (śūnya) to fill vacant places.

Fundamental Arithmetic (Pāṭigaṇita)

Concept of Pāṭigaṇita & Dhūli-karma

  • Pāṭigaṇita: Compound word from pāṭi (board) and gaṇita (science of calculation), meaning mathematics requiring writing boards.
  • Dhūli-karma: Literally "dust-work"; calculations performed by writing figures on a thin layer of dust spread over a board or ground, allowing easy erasure and correction.
  • Conciseness: Indian scientific treatises favored highly compact, brief statements of formulae and results, often requiring expert commentary to interpret.

The Eight Fundamental Operations (Aṣṭa-parikarma)

Classical Indian arithmetic recognizes eight core operations, which Bhāskara I simplified into two primary categories: increase (addition) and decrease (subtraction).

  1. Addition (Saṁkalita / Saṁkalana)
  2. Subtraction (Vyutkalita / Vyutkalana)
  3. Multiplication (Guṇana)
  4. Division (Bhāgahara)
  5. Square (Varga)
  6. Square-Root (Varga-mūla)
  7. Cube (Ghana)
  8. Cube-Root (Ghana-mūla)

Addition & Subtraction Methods

  • Addition (Saṁkalita): Defined by Aryabhata II as "making into one of several numbers." Processes include:
    • Direct Process: Adding digits from right to left (units place first, carrying over to tens, and so on).
    • Inverse Process: Adding digits from left to right (highest place value first), correcting previous digits as carries emerge.
  • Subtraction (Vyutkalita): Defined as taking out a quantity from the total (sarvadhana or viyojya) to find the remainder (śeṣa or antara). The subtrahend is termed viyojaka. Performed in both direct and inverse orders.

Multiplication & Division Methods

  • Multiplication (Guṇana): Historically defined as a process of repeated addition. Terminology: multiplicand (guṇya), multiplier (guṇaka), and product (guṇana-phala or pratyutpanna).
    • Methods: Brahmagupta lists gomūtrikā, khaṇḍa, bheda, and iṣṭa.
    • Kapaṭa-sandhi: "Door-junction" method described by Śrīpati, where the multiplier is placed above the multiplicand and shifted progressively left or right as digits are multiplied and rubbed out.
  • Division (Bhāgahara): Regarded as the inverse of multiplication. Terminology: dividend (bhājya), divisor (bhājaka), and quotient (labdhi). Performed on the board by shifting the divisor to the right and rubbing out digits of the dividend to write the partial quotients.

Fractions (Bhāga)

  • Vedic Era: The Ṛgveda mentions fractions like one-half (ardha) and three-fourths (tri-pāda). The Maitrāyaṇī Saṁhitā records one-sixteenth (kalā), one-twelfth (kuṣṭha), one-eighth (śapha), and one-fourth (pāda).
  • Sulbasūtras: Systematically used fractions to state and solve geometric problems. Unit fractions were denoted by combining cardinal numbers with bhāga or aṁśa (e.g., pañca-daśa-bhāga for $1/15$).
  • Composite Fractions: Expressed compound terms such as tri-aṣṭama ($3/8$) and dvi-saptama ($2/7$).

Square & Square-Root Algorithms

  • Square (Varga / Kṛti): Defined by Āryabhaṭa I as the product of two equal quantities or the area of a four-sided equilateral figure. Calculated on the board by squaring digits and multiplying intermediate terms systematically.
  • Square-Root (Varga-mūla): Derived from mūla or pāda (meaning root, base, or foundation). The Āryabhaṭīya provides a highly concise algorithm: divide the even place by twice the square-root of the preceding odd place, subtract the square of the quotient from the next odd place, and repeat.
Geometry & Algebra

Geometry in the Sulbasūtras

  • Sulbasūtras: Oldest known Indian mathematical texts (composed around 800 BCE by Baudhāyana, Āpastamba, and Kātyāyana) detailing geometry for constructing Vedic fire altars.
  • Altar Designs: Required precise geometric shapes representing symbolic forms, such as a falcon in flight (śyena-cit), a chariot-wheel, or a tortoise.
  • Triangles: Classified as equilateral (sama), isosceles (dvisama), and scalene (viṣamatribhūja).
  • Pythagoras Theorem: Explicitly stated and applied in the Sulbasūtras for geometric constructions, such as transforming a rectangle into a square of equal area.
  • Area Formulae: Āryabhaṭa I provided correct formulae for the area and perimeter of common geometric figures; Brahmagupta formulated the area of a cyclic quadrilateral given its sides.

Algebra (Bījagaṇita)

  • Terminology: Known as Bījagaṇita (science of calculation with elements/analysis) or avyakta-gaṇita (calculation with unknowns), in contrast to vyakta-gaṇita (arithmetic with knowns).
  • Kuṭṭaka: Meaning "pulveriser"; a branch of algebra dealing with first-degree indeterminate equations. Brahmagupta referred to algebra as kuṭṭaka-gaṇita.
  • Origins: Traced to geometric transformations in the Sulbasūtras, where converting a square to a rectangle of a given side is equivalent to solving a linear equation of the form $ax = c^2$.
  • Binomial Expansion: Contributions by Āryabhaṭa I were utilized to evaluate powers, roots, and algebraic expansions.
Trigonometry & Mathematical Constants

Trigonometric Concepts

  • Functions: Indians invented the sine (jyā or jīvā) and cosine (ko-jyā) functions, defining them using a right triangle within a quadrant of a circle.
  • Fundamental Identity: Expressed as: $$(R ext{ Sin } heta)^2 + (R ext{ Cos } heta)^2 = R^2$$ where $R$ is the radius of the circle.
  • Sine Tables: Āryabhaṭa I calculated accurate values for $R ext{ Sin } heta$ at intervals of $15^ ext{o}$ and $3.75^ ext{o}$.
  • Interpolation Formulae: Brahmagupta and Govindaswami (880 CE) developed second-order interpolation methods to compute intermediate sine values, pre-dating Newton-Stirling and Newton-Gauss formulas.

Approximations of Pi ($oldsymbol{ ext{Pi}}$)

Indian mathematicians calculated highly accurate values for the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter ($ ext{Pi}$):

  • Āryabhaṭa I (499 CE): Approximated $ ext{Pi}$ as $3.1416$.
  • Mādhava (1500 CE): Calculated $ ext{Pi}$ to eleven decimal places as $3.14159265359$.
  • Nīlakaṇṭha (1500 CE): Expressed the approximation as the fraction $355/113$.
