Knowledge Traditions And Practices Of India: Class 11 — Chapter 7: Introducing Āyurveda: The Science of Health and Disease

Introduction to Āyurveda

Āyurveda is a holistic health system encompassing physical, physiological, psychological, environmental, and ecological well-being.

  • Āyurveda: Literally translates to the "science of life".
  • Core Philosophy: Focuses on preventive, promotive, and systemic approaches to health, optimizing lifespan and well-being.
  • Antiquity: Codified systems date back at least 4,000 years (approx. 1,500 years prior to Hippocrates), with roots in the Vedas.
  • Cultural Integration: Deeply interwoven with Indian daily life, cuisine, and household remedies (e.g., turmeric and pepper in hot milk for common colds).
Biological Systems & Literature

The Three Branches of Biological Science

Ancient Indian sages recognized common physical laws and elements governing all living entities (microcosm) and the universe (macrocosm):

  • Manuṣya Āyurveda: Human health and medicine.
  • Mṛga Āyurveda: Veterinary science dealing with animals.
  • Vṛkṣa Āyurveda: Plant science, botany, and agriculture.

A vast body of clinical observations and therapeutic methodologies documented across millennia:

Botany and Veterinary Literature

  • Botanical Texts:
    • Vṛkṣa Āyurveda by Surapālā: Explains plant life-cycles, seed origins, structures, functions, diseases, plantation rules, and harvesting.
    • Kṛṣi Parāśara by Parāśara: Focuses on ancient agricultural practices and soil management.
  • Veterinary Texts:
    • Śālihotra Saṁhitā / Haya Āyurveda by Śālihotra (father of veterinary medicine): Treatises on equine health, anatomy, and treatment.
    • Hasti Āyurveda / Gaja Śāstram by Pālakāpya: Specialized texts on elephant medicine and management.
Theoretical Framework & Basic Sciences

Epistemological Foundations (Darśanas)

Unlike Western medicine's historical reliance on Newtonian reductionist physics (which views the body as a sum of isolated structural parts), Ayurvedic theory is rooted in the darśanas (Indian philosophical systems):

  • Contributing Darśanas: Vaiśeṣika, Nyāya, Pūrva Mīmāṁsā, Sāṅkhya, Yoga, and Uttara Mīmāṁsā/Vedānta.
  • Aṇu (Atom): Conceptualized in Vaiśeṣikadarśana as the fundamental particle of physical matter.
  • Pañcamahābhūtas: The five fundamental elements (space, air, fire, water, earth) composing both the human body and the universe.
  • Interdisciplinary Integration: Ayurveda draws from metallurgy (for surgical instruments), civil engineering (hospital architecture), chemistry (Rasa Śāstra), astronomy, mathematics, and culinary science.

The Four Domains of the Human System

Ayurveda conceptualizes the human body as a seamless, interconnected entity across four domains:

  1. Structural Domain: Networked through physical channels (srotas) such as blood vessels and nerves.
  2. Physiological Domain: Networked through biophysical properties defined by the tridoṣa (vāta, pitta, kapha).
  3. Psychological Domain: Connected by mental parameters and attributes (mānasikadoṣa / triguṇa).
  4. Consciousness Domain: Connected by levels of awareness defined by the five sheaths (pañcakoṣa).
The Tridoṣa System & Clinical Application

The Three Functional Principles (Tridoṣa)

  • Vāta: Governs movement, biophysical transport, and nervous system activity.
  • Pitta: Governs metabolism, digestion, thermal regulation, and chemical transformation.
  • Kapha: Governs growth, structural support, lubrication, and fluid balance.

Chronobiological Regimens

To maintain the equilibrium of the VPK network, Ayurveda prescribes daily and seasonal routines aligned with natural biological rhythms:

  • Dinacaryā: Daily regimen regulating sleep, hygiene, exercise, and diet in sync with circadian (24-hour) cycles.
  • Ṛtucaryā: Seasonal regimen adjusting lifestyle and diet to counter environmental changes across circa-annual cycles.
Diet, Nutrition & Pharmacology

Dietary Principles & The Six Tastes (Rasa)

Ayurveda assesses nutrition through functional indices and the balance of six tastes rather than simple calorie counting:

  • The Six Tastes (Rasa): Sweet (madhura), Sour (amla), Salty (lavaṇa), Pungent (kaṭu), Bitter (tikta), and Astringent (kaṣāya).
  • Personalized Nutrition: Diet is tailored to an individual's unique bio-psychological constitution (prakṛti).
  • Culinary Antidotes: Traditional cooking combines ingredients to neutralize side effects (e.g., cooking wheat halwa with ghee to pacify the Kapha-increasing property of wheat; adding cardamom and saffron to aid digestion).

Ayurvedic Pharmacology (Dravyaguṇa Vijñāna)

  • Synergistic Whole-Plant Action: Uses entire plant parts to extract complex groups of compounds, rather than isolating single active molecules.
  • Extraction Mediums:
    • Water-soluble polar compounds: Extracted via decoctions (kaṣāya / kvātha).
    • Polar and non-polar compounds: Extracted via fermented alcoholic preparations (ariṣṭa, āsava).
    • Lipid-soluble non-polar compounds: Extracted using medicated oils and clarified butter (tailam, ghṛtam).
Contemporary Relevance & Milestones

Alignment with Modern Medical Concepts

Modern Western medicine is increasingly validating Ayurvedic paradigms through emerging fields:

  • Systems Biology: Parallels Ayurveda's focus on network interactions over isolated cellular components.
  • Personalized Medicine: Corresponds to the Ayurvedic customization of treatment based on prakṛti.
  • Therapeutic Nutrition & Circadian Rhythms: Validates Ayurvedic dietary timing and chronobiological regimens (recognized by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Medicine).

Modern Institutional Timeline

  • 1827: First formal Ayurvedic course started at Government Sanskrit College, Calcutta.
  • 1833: Discontinuation of the course by British authorities.
  • 1920: Indian National Congress resolves to integrate Ayurveda as India's national healthcare system.
  • 1970: Passage of the Indian Medicine Central Council Act to regulate education and practice standards.
  • 1995: Creation of the Department of Indian Systems of Medicine and Homoeopathy (ISM&H).
  • 2005: ISM&H renamed as AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, and Homoeopathy).
  • 2014: Department of AYUSH upgraded to an independent Ministry.
  • Professional Education: Over 250 colleges in India offer the 5.5-year undergraduate degree BAMS (Bachelor of Ayurvedic Medicine and Surgery / Āyurvedācārya), followed by 3-year MD specializations.
