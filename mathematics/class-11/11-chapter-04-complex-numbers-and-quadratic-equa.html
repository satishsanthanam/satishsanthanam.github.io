Mathematics: Class 11 — Chapter 4: Complex Numbers and Quadratic Equations

Introduction to Complex Numbers

The system of real numbers is insufficient to solve quadratic equations of the form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ where the discriminant $D = b^2 - 4ac < 0$. For instance, the simple equation $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real solution because the square of any real number is non-negative. To overcome this limitation, the real number system is extended to a larger system called the complex number system.

Definition and Basic Representation

We denote the square root of negative unity, $\sqrt{-1}$, by the symbol $i$ (iota), such that $i^2 = -1$. This means $i$ is a solution to the equation $x^2 + 1 = 0$.

A number of the form $z = a + ib$, where $a$ and $b$ are real numbers, is defined to be a complex number.

  • Real Part: The real number $a$ is called the real part of $z$, denoted by $\text{Re } z$.
  • Imaginary Part: The real number $b$ is called the imaginary part of $z$, denoted by $\text{Im } z$.

For example, if $z = 2 + i5$, then $\text{Re } z = 2$ and $\text{Im } z = 5$.

Equality of Complex Numbers

Two complex numbers $z_1 = a + ib$ and $z_2 = c + id$ are defined to be equal if and only if their corresponding real and imaginary parts are equal:

$$z_1 = z_2 \iff a = c \text{ and } b = d$$

Algebra of Complex Numbers

We define fundamental algebraic operations—addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division—on the set of complex numbers.

Addition and Subtraction

Let $z_1 = a + ib$ and $z_2 = c + id$ be two complex numbers.

  • Addition: $z_1 + z_2 = (a + c) + i(b + d)$
  • Difference: $z_1 - z_2 = z_1 + (-z_2) = (a - c) + i(b - d)$

The addition of complex numbers satisfies the following properties:

  1. Closure Law: The sum of two complex numbers is always a complex number.
  2. Commutative Law: $z_1 + z_2 = z_2 + z_1$
  3. Associative Law: $(z_1 + z_2) + z_3 = z_1 + (z_2 + z_3)$
  4. Existence of Additive Identity: The complex number $0 + i0$ (denoted as $0$) acts as the identity, such that $z + 0 = z$.
  5. Existence of Additive Inverse: For any $z = a + ib$, there exists $-z = -a + i(-b)$ such that $z + (-z) = 0$.
Multiplication and Division

Let $z_1 = a + ib$ and $z_2 = c + id$.

  • Multiplication: $z_1 z_2 = (ac - bd) + i(ad + bc)$
  • Division: For $z_2 \neq 0$, $\frac{z_1}{z_2} = z_1 \times \frac{1}{z_2}$

Multiplication satisfies the following properties:

  1. Closure Law: The product of two complex numbers is a complex number.
  2. Commutative Law: $z_1 z_2 = z_2 z_1$
  3. Associative Law: $(z_1 z_2) z_3 = z_1 (z_2 z_3)$
  4. Existence of Multiplicative Identity: The complex number $1 + i0$ (denoted as $1$) satisfies $z \times 1 = z$.
  5. Existence of Multiplicative Inverse: For any non-zero complex number $z = a + ib$, there exists a unique complex number $z^{-1}$ or $\frac{1}{z}$ given by:$$z^{-1} = \frac{a}{a^2 + b^2} + i \frac{-b}{a^2 + b^2}$$such that $z \times z^{-1} = 1$.
  6. Distributive Law: $z_1(z_2 + z_3) = z_1 z_2 + z_1 z_3$ and $(z_1 + z_2)z_3 = z_1 z_3 + z_2 z_3$.
Powers of $i$ and Square Roots of Negative Numbers
Integral Powers of $i$

The powers of $i$ exhibit a cyclic pattern of length 4:

  • $i^1 = i$
  • $i^2 = -1$
  • $i^3 = i^2 \times i = -i$
  • $i^4 = (i^2)^2 = 1$

In general, for any integer $k$:

$$i^{4k} = 1, \quad i^{4k+1} = i, \quad i^{4k+2} = -1, \quad i^{4k+3} = -i$$

Negative powers of $i$ can be computed similarly, e.g., $i^{-1} = \frac{1}{i} = -i$, $i^{-2} = -1$, $i^{-3} = i$, $i^{-4} = 1$.

Square Roots of Negative Real Numbers

For any positive real number $a$, the square roots of $-a$ are $\sqrt{a}i$ and $-\sqrt{a}i$. The symbol $\sqrt{-a}$ is defined to represent $\sqrt{a}i$ only.

Critical Caveat: The identity $\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ holds true when at least one of $a$ or $b$ is non-negative. However, if both $a < 0$ and $b < 0$, then:

$$\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b} = (\sqrt{|a|}i) \times (\sqrt{|b|}i) = \sqrt{|ab|}i^2 = -\sqrt{ab} \neq \sqrt{ab}$$

Modulus, Conjugate, and Identities
Modulus and Conjugate Definitions

Let $z = a + ib$ be a complex number.

  • Modulus: The modulus of $z$, denoted by $|z|$, is the non-negative real number defined by:$$|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$$
  • Conjugate: The conjugate of $z$, denoted by $\bar{z}$, is the complex number defined by:$$\bar{z} = a - ib$$
Properties of Modulus and Conjugate

For any complex numbers $z, z_1, z_2$:

Property Type Mathematical Identity / Formula Operational Meaning
Inverse Relation $z \bar{z} = |z|^2$ or $z^{-1} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2}$ Relates a complex number, its conjugate, and its modulus square.
Product Modulus $|z_1 z_2| = |z_1| |z_2| The modulus of a product is the product of the moduli.
QN Modulus $\left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$ (for $z_2 \neq 0$) The modulus of a quotient is the quotient of the moduli.
Product Conjugate $\overline{z_1 z_2} = \bar{z}_1 \bar{z}_2$ The conjugate of a product is the product of the conjugates.
Sum/Difference Conjugate $\overline{z_1 \pm z_2} = \bar{z}_1 \pm \bar{z}_2$ The conjugate distributes over addition and subtraction.
Quotient Conjugate $\overline{\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)} = \frac{\bar{z}_1}{\bar{z}_2}$ (for $z_2 \neq 0$) The conjugate distributes over division.
Algebraic Identities

Standard algebraic identities hold true for all complex numbers $z_1$ and $z_2$:

  • $(z_1 + z_2)^2 = z_1^2 + 2z_1z_2 + z_2^2$
  • $(z_1 - z_2)^2 = z_1^2 - 2z_1z_2 + z_2^2$
  • $(z_1 + z_2)^3 = z_1^3 + 3z_1^2z_2 + 3z_1z_2^2 + z_2^3$
  • $(z_1 - z_2)^3 = z_1^3 - 3z_1^2z_2 + 3z_1z_2^2 - z_2^3$
  • $z_1^2 - z_2^2 = (z_1 + z_2)(z_1 - z_2)$
The Argand Plane

The Argand plane (or complex plane) is a geometric representation where each complex number $z = x + iy$ is uniquely mapped to an ordered pair $(x, y)$ and plotted as a point $P(x, y)$ in a two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system.

  • Real Axis: The $x$-axis represents the real part of the complex number ($y = 0$).
  • Imaginary Axis: The $y$-axis represents the imaginary part of the complex number ($x = 0$).
  • Geometric Modulus: The modulus $|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ represents the straight-line distance of the point $P(x, y)$ from the origin $O(0, 0)$.
  • Geometric Conjugate: The conjugate $\bar{z} = x - iy$ corresponds to the point $Q(x, -y)$, which is the mirror image of $P(x, y)$ across the real axis ($x$-axis).
Algorithmic Solved Examples

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the real values of $x$ and $y$ if $4x + i(3x - y) = 3 + i(-6)$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Equate the real parts on both sides of the equation:$$4x = 3 \implies x = \frac{3}{4}$$
  2. Equate the imaginary parts on both sides of the equation:$$3x - y = -6$$
  3. Substitute the value of $x$ into the imaginary equation:$$3\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) - y = -6 \implies \frac{9}{4} - y = -6$$
  4. Solve for $y$:$$y = \frac{9}{4} + 6 = \frac{9 + 24}{4} = \frac{33}{4}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $x = \frac{3}{4}, \quad y = \frac{33}{4}$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the multiplicative inverse of $z = 2 - 3i$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify the real and imaginary parts: $a = 2$, $b = -3$.
  2. Compute the modulus squared $|z|^2$:$$|z|^2 = a^2 + b^2 = 2^2 + (-3)^2 = 4 + 9 = 13$$
  3. Find the conjugate $\bar{z}$:$$\bar{z} = 2 + 3i$$
  4. Apply the multiplicative inverse formula $z^{-1} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2}$:$$z^{-1} = \frac{2 + 3i}{13} = \frac{2}{13} + i\frac{3}{13}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $z^{-1} = \frac{2}{13} + i\frac{3}{13}$

Mathematical Problem Statement: If $x + iy = \frac{a + ib}{a - ib}$, prove that $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Take the modulus on both sides of the given equation:$$|x + iy| = \left|\frac{a + ib}{a - ib}\right|$$
  2. Use the quotient property of modulus $|\frac{z_1}{z_2}| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$:$$|x + iy| = \frac{|a + ib|}{|a - ib|}$$
  3. Calculate the moduli:$$\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}{\sqrt{a^2 + (-b)^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} = 1$$
  4. Square both sides to obtain the final result:$$x^2 + y^2 = 1$$

Final Evaluation Output: $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ (Proved)

Historical Chronicle / Epigraph: Evolution of Imaginary Numbers

  • Historical Context & Milestones: The impossibility of square roots of negative numbers was recognized by early Greek mathematicians. Indian mathematician Mahavira (850 AD) explicitly stated in Ganitasara Sangraha that a negative quantity is not a square and thus has no square root. Bhaskara (1150 AD) reiterated this in Bijaganita. Cardan (1545 AD) attempted to solve $x + y = 10, xy = 40$, obtaining $5 + \sqrt{-15}$ and $5 - \sqrt{-15}$, but dismissed them as "useless".
  • Theoretical Foundations: Albert Girard (1625 AD) accepted negative roots to match the degree of polynomial equations. Leonhard Euler was the first to introduce the symbol $i$ for $\sqrt{-1}$. William Rowan Hamilton (1830 AD) formalized complex numbers as ordered pairs of real numbers $(a, b)$, providing a rigorous algebraic foundation.
