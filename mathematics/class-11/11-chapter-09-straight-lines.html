Mathematics: Class 11 — Chapter 9: Straight Lines

Introduction & Historical Foundations

Historical Chronicle: René Descartes (1596 - 1650)

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Descartes published his landmark book La Géométrie in 1637, introducing the systematic study of geometry using algebra.
  • Theoretical Foundations: This work established the concept of the equation of a curve and analytical methods, giving rise to what is now known as analytical geometry or coordinate geometry.

Coordinate geometry combines algebra and geometry to study geometric figures in a two-dimensional coordinate plane. In earlier classes, basic concepts such as coordinate axes, coordinate planes, plotting points, and basic formulas were introduced.

Recall of Basic Coordinate Geometry Formulas

The following fundamental formulas form the basis of two-dimensional coordinate geometry:

Formula Type Mathematical Expression Description & Application Limits
Distance Formula $$PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$$ Calculates the straight-line distance between two points $P(x_1, y_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2)$.
Section Formula (Internal) $$\left(\frac{mx_2 + nx_1}{m+n}, \frac{my_2 + ny_1}{m+n}\right)$$ Finds the coordinates of a point dividing the line segment joining $P(x_1, y_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2)$ internally in the ratio $m:n$.
Mid-point Formula $$\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$$ A special case of the section formula where the ratio $m = n$.
Area of a Triangle $$\frac{1}{2} |x_1(y_2 - y_3) + x_2(y_3 - y_1) + x_3(y_1 - y_2)|$$ Computes the area of a triangle with vertices $(x_1, y_1)$, $(x_2, y_2)$, and $(x_3, y_3)$.
Collinearity Condition

Three points $A$, $B$, and $C$ are said to be collinear (lying on the same straight line) if and only if the area of the triangle formed by them is zero:

$$x_1(y_2 - y_3) + x_2(y_3 - y_1) + x_3(y_1 - y_2) = 0$$
Slope of a Line

The slope or gradient of a non-vertical line is a measure of its steepness and direction.

Definition & Inclination

The angle $\theta$ ($0^\circ \le \theta \le 180^\circ$) made by a line $l$ with the positive direction of the $x$-axis, measured anticlockwise, is called the inclination of the line.

  • The slope $m$ of a line with inclination $\theta$ is defined as: $$m = \tan \theta, \quad \theta \neq 90^\circ$$
  • The slope of a horizontal line (parallel to or coinciding with the $x$-axis) is $0$ (since $\theta = 0^\circ$).
  • The slope of a vertical line (parallel to or coinciding with the $y$-axis) is undefined (since $\theta = 90^\circ$).
Slope of a Line through Two Given Points

If a non-vertical line passes through two distinct points $P(x_1, y_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2)$, its slope $m$ is given by:

$$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$$

This formula holds true whether the inclination angle $\theta$ is acute or obtuse.

Parallelism and Perpendicularity Conditions
  • Parallelism: Two non-vertical lines $l_1$ and $l_2$ with slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$ are parallel if and only if their slopes are equal: $$m_1 = m_2$$
  • Perpendicularity: Two non-vertical lines $l_1$ and $l_2$ with slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$ are perpendicular if and only if their slopes are negative reciprocals of each other: $$m_1 m_2 = -1 \quad \text{or} \quad m_2 = -\frac{1}{m_1}$$
Angle Between Two Lines

The acute angle $\theta$ between two non-vertical intersecting lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ with slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$ is given by:

$$\tan \theta = \left| \frac{m_2 - m_1}{1 + m_1 m_2} \right|, \quad 1 + m_1 m_2 \neq 0$$

The obtuse angle $\phi$ between the lines can be found using $\phi = 180^\circ - \theta$.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the slope of the line passing through the points $(3, -2)$ and $(-1, 4)$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify the coordinates: $(x_1, y_1) = (3, -2)$ and $(x_2, y_2) = (-1, 4)$.
  2. Apply the two-point slope formula: $m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$.
  3. Substitute the values: $m = \frac{4 - (-2)}{-1 - 3} = \frac{6}{-4} = -\frac{3}{2}$.

Final Evaluation Output: $m = -\frac{3}{2}$

Various Forms of the Equation of a Line

Depending on the given geometric conditions, the equation of a straight line can be expressed in several standard algebraic forms:

1. Horizontal and Vertical Lines
  • Horizontal Line: A line parallel to the $x$-axis at a distance $a$ has the equation: $$y = a \quad \text{or} \quad y = -a$$
  • Vertical Line: A line parallel to the $y$-axis at a distance $b$ has the equation: $$x = b \quad \text{or} \quad x = -b$$
2. Point-Slope Form

The equation of a non-vertical line with slope $m$ passing through a fixed point $(x_0, y_0)$ is:

$$y - y_0 = m(x - x_0)$$
3. Two-Point Form

The equation of a non-vertical line passing through two given points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is:

$$y - y_1 = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}(x - x_1)$$
4. Slope-Intercept Form
  • With $y$-intercept $c$: If a line with slope $m$ cuts the $y$-axis at a distance $c$ from the origin (i.e., at $(0, c)$), its equation is: $$y = mx + c$$
  • With $x$-intercept $d$: If a line with slope $m$ cuts the $x$-axis at a distance $d$ from the origin (i.e., at $(d, 0)$), its equation is: $$y = m(x - d)$$
5. Intercept Form

If a line makes non-zero intercepts $a$ and $b$ on the $x$-axis and $y$-axis respectively, its equation is:

$$\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1$$
6. General Equation of a Line

Any first-degree polynomial equation in two variables $x$ and $y$ represents a straight line. The general linear equation is written as:

$$Ax + By + C = 0$$

where $A$ and $B$ are not zero simultaneously.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the equation of the line passing through the points $(1, -1)$ and $(3, 5)$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify the points: $(x_1, y_1) = (1, -1)$ and $(x_2, y_2) = (3, 5)$.
  2. Use the two-point form: $y - y_1 = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}(x - x_1)$.
  3. Substitute the coordinates: $y - (-1) = \frac{5 - (-1)}{3 - 1}(x - 1)$.
  4. Simplify the slope: $\frac{6}{2} = 3$.
  5. Write the equation: $y + 1 = 3(x - 1) \implies y + 1 = 3x - 3$.
  6. Rearrange into general form: $3x - y - 4 = 0$.

Final Evaluation Output: $3x - y - 4 = 0$

Distance of a Point From a Line

This section covers the formulas for finding the perpendicular distance from a point to a line and the distance between two parallel lines.

Perpendicular Distance of a Point from a Line

The perpendicular distance $d$ of a point $P(x_1, y_1)$ from a line $Ax + By + C = 0$ is given by:

$$d = \frac{|Ax_1 + By_1 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}$$
Distance Between Two Parallel Lines

Since parallel lines have the same slope, they can be represented in two ways:

  • Slope-intercept form: For lines $y = mx + c_1$ and $y = mx + c_2$: $$d = \frac{|c_1 - c_2|}{\sqrt{1 + m^2}}$$
  • General form: For lines $Ax + By + C_1 = 0$ and $Ax + By + C_2 = 0$: $$d = \frac{|C_1 - C_2|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the distance of the point $(3, -5)$ from the line $3x - 4y - 26 = 0$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify the parameters: $A = 3$, $B = -4$, $C = -26$, and $(x_1, y_1) = (3, -5)$.
  2. Apply the perpendicular distance formula: $d = \frac{|Ax_1 + By_1 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}}$.
  3. Substitute the values: $d = \frac{|3(3) + (-4)(-5) - 26|}{\sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2}}$.
  4. Calculate the numerator: $|9 + 20 - 26| = |3| = 3$.
  5. Calculate the denominator: $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
  6. Compute the final distance: $d = \frac{3}{5}$.

Final Evaluation Output: $d = \frac{3}{5}$
