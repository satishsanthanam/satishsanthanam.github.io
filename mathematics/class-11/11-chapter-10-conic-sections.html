Mathematics: Class 11 — Chapter 10: Conic Sections

10.1 Introduction & Sections of a Cone

Conic sections (or conics) are curves obtained by intersecting a double-napped right circular cone with a plane. These curves have extensive applications in planetary motion, telescope design, reflectors, and acoustics.

Geometric Generation of a Cone

Let $l$ be a fixed vertical line (the axis) and $m$ be another line (the generator) intersecting $l$ at a fixed point $V$ (the vertex) at an angle $\alpha$ (the semi-vertical angle). Rotating $m$ around $l$ while keeping $\alpha$ constant generates a double-napped right circular hollow cone consisting of an upper nappe and a lower nappe meeting at $V$.

Classification of Conic Sections

Let $\beta$ be the angle made by the intersecting plane with the vertical axis $l$ of the cone. Depending on the value of $\beta$, different curves are formed:

Section Type Angle Condition Geometric Description Standard Equation
Circle $\beta = 90^\circ$ The plane is perpendicular to the axis and cuts across a single nappe. $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$
Ellipse $\alpha < \beta < 90^\circ$ The plane cuts obliquely across one entire nappe. $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$
Parabola $\beta = \alpha$ The plane is parallel to the generator line and cuts only one nappe. $y^2 = 4ax$
Hyperbola $0 \le \beta < \alpha$ The plane is parallel to the axis, cutting through both the upper and lower nappes. $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$
Degenerate Conic Sections

When the intersecting plane passes directly through the vertex $V$ of the cone, the resulting sections are called degenerate conics:

  • Point: Formed when $\alpha < \beta \le 90^\circ$.
  • Single Straight Line: Formed when $\beta = \alpha$ (the plane contains a generator of the cone).
  • Pair of Intersecting Lines: Formed when $0 \le \beta < \alpha$.
10.2 The Circle

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are equidistant from a fixed point (the centre) in the plane. The constant distance is called the radius.

Standard Equation of a Circle

The equation of a circle with centre $(h, k)$ and radius $r$ is derived using the distance formula:

$$(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2$$

If the centre is at the origin $(0, 0)$, the equation simplifies to:

$$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$$
Solved Examples

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the centre and radius of the circle given by the equation $x^2 + y^2 + 8x + 10y - 8 = 0$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Group the $x$ and $y$ terms: $(x^2 + 8x) + (y^2 + 10y) = 8$.
  2. Complete the square for $x$: add $(\frac{8}{2})^2 = 16$ to both sides.
  3. Complete the square for $y$: add $(\frac{10}{2})^2 = 25$ to both sides.
  4. Rewrite the equation: $(x^2 + 8x + 16) + (y^2 + 10y + 25) = 8 + 16 + 25$.
  5. Simplify: $(x + 4)^2 + (y + 5)^2 = 49$.
  6. Express in standard form: $(x - (-4))^2 + (y - (-5))^2 = 7^2$.

Final Evaluation Output: Centre is $(-4, -5)$ and Radius is $7$.

10.3 The Parabola

A parabola is the set of all points in a plane that are equidistant from a fixed line (the directrix) and a fixed point (the focus) not on the line.

Standard Equations and Properties

The simplest form of a parabola has its vertex at the origin $(0,0)$ and its axis along one of the coordinate axes. There are four standard orientations:

Equation Focus Directrix Axis of Symmetry Direction of Opening
$y^2 = 4ax$ $(a, 0)$ $x = -a$ $y = 0$ ($x$-axis) Opens Right
$y^2 = -4ax$ $(-a, 0)$ $x = a$ $y = 0$ ($x$-axis) Opens Left
$x^2 = 4ay$ $(0, a)$ $y = -a$ $x = 0$ ($y$-axis) Opens Upward
$x^2 = -4ay$ $(0, -a)$ $y = a$ $x = 0$ ($y$-axis) Opens Downward
Latus Rectum

The latus rectum of a parabola is a line segment perpendicular to the axis of the parabola, passing through the focus, with endpoints lying on the parabola. For any standard parabola, the length of the latus rectum is:

$$\text{Length} = 4a$$
Solved Examples

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the coordinates of the focus, the equation of the directrix, and the length of the latus rectum for the parabola $y^2 = 8x$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Compare the given equation $y^2 = 8x$ with the standard form $y^2 = 4ax$.
  2. Solve for $a$: $4a = 8 \implies a = 2$.
  3. Determine the focus: Since the axis is along the $x$-axis and $a > 0$, the focus is $(a, 0) = (2, 0)$.
  4. Determine the directrix: The equation of the directrix is $x = -a \implies x = -2$.
  5. Calculate the length of the latus rectum: $\text{Length} = 4a = 8$.

Final Evaluation Output: Focus: $(2, 0)$, Directrix: $x = -2$, Latus Rectum Length: $8$.

10.4 The Ellipse

An ellipse is the set of all points in a plane, the sum of whose distances from two fixed points (the foci) in the plane is a constant ($2a$).

Standard Equations and Key Parameters

Let $2a$ be the length of the major axis, $2b$ be the length of the minor axis, and $2c$ be the distance between the foci. The fundamental relationship between these parameters is:

$$a^2 = b^2 + c^2 \implies c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$$

The eccentricity ($e$) of an ellipse is the ratio of the distance of a focus from the centre to the length of the semi-major axis:

$$e = \frac{c}{a} \quad (0 < e < 1)$$
Standard Equation Major Axis Along Foci Coordinates Vertices Coordinates Latus Rectum Length
$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad (a > b)$ $x$-axis $(\pm c, 0)$ $(\pm a, 0)$ $\frac{2b^2}{a}$
$\frac{x^2}{b^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2} = 1 \quad (a > b)$ $y$-axis $(0, \pm c)$ $(0, \pm a)$ $\frac{2b^2}{a}$
Solved Examples

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the coordinates of the foci, the vertices, the eccentricity, and the length of the latus rectum of the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify $a^2$ and $b^2$ by comparing with $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$: $a^2 = 25 \implies a = 5$, and $b^2 = 9 \implies b = 3$.
  2. Since $a^2 > b^2$ is under $x^2$, the major axis is along the $x$-axis.
  3. Calculate $c$: $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = \sqrt{25 - 9} = \sqrt{16} = 4$.
  4. Determine foci: $(\pm c, 0) = (\pm 4, 0)$.
  5. Determine vertices: $(\pm a, 0) = (\pm 5, 0)$.
  6. Calculate eccentricity: $e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$.
  7. Calculate latus rectum length: $\frac{2b^2}{a} = \frac{2(9)}{5} = \frac{18}{5} = 3.6$.

Final Evaluation Output: Foci: $(\pm 4, 0)$, Vertices: $(\pm 5, 0)$, Eccentricity: $0.8$, Latus Rectum Length: $3.6$.

10.5 The Hyperbola

A hyperbola is the set of all points in a plane, the difference of whose distances from two fixed points (the foci) in the plane is a constant ($2a$).

Standard Equations and Key Parameters

Let $2a$ be the length of the transverse axis, $2b$ be the length of the conjugate axis, and $2c$ be the distance between the foci. The fundamental relationship is:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \implies b = \sqrt{c^2 - a^2}$$

The eccentricity ($e$) of a hyperbola is:

$$e = \frac{c}{a} \quad (e > 1)$$
Standard Equation Transverse Axis Along Foci Coordinates Vertices Coordinates Latus Rectum Length
$\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ $x$-axis $(\pm c, 0)$ $(\pm a, 0)$ $\frac{2b^2}{a}$
$\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1$ $y$-axis $(0, \pm c)$ $(0, \pm a)$ $\frac{2b^2}{a}$

An equilateral hyperbola is a special case where $a = b$, simplifying the equation to $x^2 - y^2 = a^2$.

Solved Examples

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the coordinates of the foci, the vertices, the eccentricity, and the length of the latus rectum of the hyperbola $\frac{x^2}{9} - \frac{y^2}{16} = 1$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Compare with the standard form $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$: $a^2 = 9 \implies a = 3$, and $b^2 = 16 \implies b = 4$.
  2. Calculate $c$: $c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
  3. Determine foci: $(\pm c, 0) = (\pm 5, 0)$.
  4. Determine vertices: $(\pm a, 0) = (\pm 3, 0)$.
  5. Calculate eccentricity: $e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{5}{3}$.
  6. Calculate latus rectum length: $\frac{2b^2}{a} = \frac{2(16)}{3} = \frac{32}{3}$.

Final Evaluation Output: Foci: $(\pm 5, 0)$, Vertices: $(\pm 3, 0)$, Eccentricity: $\frac{5}{3}$, Latus Rectum Length: $\frac{32}{3}$.

10.6 Historical Chronicle & Summary

Historical Chronicle: Apollonius of Perga (262 B.C. - 190 B.C.)

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Known as the "Great Geometer," Apollonius wrote the definitive eight-volume treatise Conics, which introduced the terms parabola, ellipse, and hyperbola.
  • Theoretical Foundations: He demonstrated that all three curves can be generated by intersecting a single double-napped cone with a plane at varying angles, replacing earlier methods that required different cones for each curve.

Historical Chronicle: René Descartes & Pierre de Fermat

  • Historical Context & Milestones: In the 17th century, Descartes published La Géométrie (1637), establishing analytical geometry (Cartesian geometry) which allowed geometric curves to be studied using algebraic equations.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Fermat independently discovered the fundamental principles of analytical geometry, enabling the algebraic representation of conic sections as second-degree equations.
