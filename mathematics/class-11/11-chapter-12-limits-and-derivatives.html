Mathematics: Class 11 — Chapter 12: Limits and Derivatives

1. Introduction & Intuitive Idea of Derivatives

Calculus is the branch of mathematics that studies how the value of a function changes as the points in its domain change. It provides the mathematical framework to analyze dynamic systems, rates of change, and limiting behaviors.

Physical Motivation: The Falling Body Problem

Consider a body dropped from a tall cliff. Physical experiments confirm that the distance $s$ (in meters) covered by the body as a function of time $t$ (in seconds) is given by:

$$s(t) = 4.9t^2$$

To find the velocity of the body at exactly $t = 2$ seconds, we analyze the average velocity over smaller and smaller time intervals ending or starting at $t = 2$.

  • Average Velocity between $t = t_1$ and $t = t_2$ is defined as: $$\text{Average Velocity} = \frac{\text{Distance travelled between } t_1 \text{ and } t_2}{\text{Time interval } (t_2 - t_1)} = \frac{s(t_2) - s(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}$$
Tabular Analysis of Average Velocity

By calculating the average velocity over intervals ending at $t = 2$ (using $t_1$ values approaching 2 from the left) and starting at $t = 2$ (using $t_2$ values approaching 2 from the right), we observe the following limiting behavior:

Interval Type Time Parameter ($t$) Average Velocity Formula ($v$) Limiting Value (m/s)
Left-hand intervals ($t_1 \to 2^-$) $t_1 \in \{0, 1, 1.5, 1.8, 1.9, 1.95, 1.99\}$ $v = \frac{s(2) - s(t_1)}{2 - t_1} = \frac{19.6 - 4.9t_1^2}{2 - t_1}$ Approaches $19.6 \text{ m/s}$ from below ($19.551$ at $t_1 = 1.99$)
Right-hand intervals ($t_2 \to 2^+$) $t_2 \in \{4, 3, 2.5, 2.2, 2.1, 2.05, 2.01\}$ $v = \frac{s(t_2) - s(2)}{t_2 - 2} = \frac{4.9t_2^2 - 19.6}{t_2 - 2}$ Approaches $19.6 \text{ m/s}$ from above ($19.649$ at $t_2 = 2.01$)

This limiting process indicates that the instantaneous velocity at $t = 2$ is exactly 19.6 m/s. Geometrically, this corresponds to the slope of the tangent to the curve $s = 4.9t^2$ at the point $A(2, 19.6)$.

2. Limits

The concept of a limit describes the behavior of a function $f(x)$ as the independent variable $x$ approaches a specific value $a$, regardless of the value of the function at $a$ itself.

Left-Hand and Right-Hand Limits

To understand limits comprehensively, we must analyze the behavior of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from both directions:

  • Left-Hand Limit (LHL): The expected value of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from values strictly less than $a$. $$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$$
  • Right-Hand Limit (RHL): The expected value of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $a$ from values strictly greater than $a$. $$\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$$
  • Existence of a Limit: The limit of $f(x)$ as $x \to a$ exists if and only if both the left-hand and right-hand limits exist and are equal: $$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = l$$
Algebra of Limits

Theorem 1: Let $f$ and $g$ be two functions such that both $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x)$ exist. Then the following algebraic properties hold:

  1. Sum Rule: The limit of a sum is the sum of the limits. $$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x) + g(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) + \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$$
  2. Difference Rule: The limit of a difference is the difference of the limits. $$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x) - g(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) - \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$$
  3. Product Rule: The limit of a product is the product of the limits. $$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x) \cdot g(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \cdot \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$$
  4. Minus / Quotient Rule: The limit of a quotient is the quotient of the limits, provided the denominator's limit is non-zero. $$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\lim_{x \to a} f(x)}{\lim_{x \to a} g(x)} \quad \text{where } \lim_{x \to a} g(x) \neq 0$$
  5. Scalar Multiplication: For any real number $\lambda$: $$\lim_{x \to a} [\lambda \cdot f(x)] = \lambda \cdot \lim_{x \to a} f(x)$$
Limits of Polynomials and Rational Functions
  • For any polynomial function $f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_n x^n$, the limit at $x = a$ is simply the value of the polynomial at $a$: $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$$
  • For a rational function $f(x) = \frac{g(x)}{h(x)}$ where $g(x)$ and $h(x)$ are polynomials:
    • If $h(a) \neq 0$, then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \frac{g(a)}{h(a)}$.
    • If $h(a) = 0$ and $g(a) \neq 0$, the limit does not exist.
    • If $h(a) = 0$ and $g(a) = 0$, we obtain the indeterminate form $\frac{0}{0}$. We must factor out common terms of $(x-a)$ from both numerator and denominator to evaluate the limit.

Theorem 2: For any positive integer $n$ (and indeed any rational number $n$ when $a > 0$):

$$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1}$$
Algorithmic Solved Examples

Mathematical Problem Statement: Evaluate the limit: $\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{15} - 1}{x^{10} - 1}$

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Divide both the numerator and the denominator by $(x - 1)$ to express the limit in the standard form of Theorem 2: $$\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{15} - 1}{x^{10} - 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\frac{x^{15} - 1}{x - 1}}{\frac{x^{10} - 1}{x - 1}}$$
  2. Apply the quotient rule of limits: $$\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{15} - 1}{x^{10} - 1} = \frac{\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{15} - 1}{x - 1}}{\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{10} - 1}{x - 1}}$$
  3. Apply Theorem 2 ($\lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1}$) to both numerator ($n=15, a=1$) and denominator ($n=10, a=1$): $$\text{Numerator Limit} = 15(1)^{14} = 15$$ $$\text{Denominator Limit} = 10(1)^9 = 10$$
  4. Compute the final ratio: $$\text{Limit} = \frac{15}{10} = \frac{3}{2}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $\frac{3}{2}$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Evaluate the limit: $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^3 - 4x^2 + 4x}{x^2 - 4}$

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Substitute $x = 2$ directly to check the form: $$\text{Numerator: } 2^3 - 4(2)^2 + 4(2) = 8 - 16 + 8 = 0$$ $$\text{Denominator: } 2^2 - 4 = 0$$ This is an indeterminate form of $\frac{0}{0}$.
  2. Factorize the numerator and denominator to isolate the vanishing factor $(x - 2)$: $$\text{Numerator: } x^3 - 4x^2 + 4x = x(x^2 - 4x + 4) = x(x - 2)^2$$ $$\text{Denominator: } x^2 - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2)$$
  3. Rewrite the limit expression and cancel the common factor $(x - 2)$ (valid since $x \neq 2$): $$\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x(x - 2)^2}{(x - 2)(x + 2)} = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x(x - 2)}{x + 2}$$
  4. Substitute $x = 2$ into the simplified expression: $$\text{Limit} = \frac{2(2 - 2)}{2 + 2} = \frac{0}{4} = 0$$

Final Evaluation Output: $0$

3. Limits of Trigonometric Functions

Evaluating limits of trigonometric functions requires specialized theorems to handle the oscillatory and transcendental nature of these functions near critical points.

The Sandwich Theorem (Squeeze Theorem)

Theorem 4: Let $f$, $g$, and $h$ be real-valued functions such that:

$$f(x) \le g(x) \le h(x)$$

for all $x$ in a common domain of definition (except possibly at $x = a$). If:

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = l$$

then the limit of the middle function $g(x)$ is also forced to be $l$:

$$\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = l$$
Fundamental Trigonometric Limits

Using geometric proofs and the Sandwich Theorem, we establish the following fundamental limits (where angles are measured in radians):

Trigonometric Limit Identity Mathematical Condition Geometric/Algebraic Basis
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$$ $x$ in radians, $x \neq 0$ Sandwiched by the inequality: $\cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1$ for $0 < |x| < \frac{\pi}{2}$
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0$$ $x$ in radians, $x \neq 0$ Derived using the identity $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$
Algorithmic Solved Example

Mathematical Problem Statement: Evaluate the limit: $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 4x}{\sin 2x}$

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Express the limit by introducing the appropriate scaling factors in the numerator and denominator to match the standard form $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$: $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 4x}{\sin 2x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \left[ \frac{\frac{\sin 4x}{4x} \cdot 4x}{\frac{\sin 2x}{2x} \cdot 2x} \right]$$
  2. Simplify the algebraic terms outside the trigonometric functions: $$\lim_{x \to 0} \left[ \frac{\sin 4x}{4x} \cdot \frac{2x}{\sin 2x} \cdot \frac{4x}{2x} \right] = 2 \cdot \lim_{x \to 0} \left[ \frac{\frac{\sin 4x}{4x}}{\frac{\sin 2x}{2x}} \right]$$
  3. Apply the quotient rule of limits. Note that as $x \to 0$, both $4x \to 0$ and $2x \to 0$: $$\text{Limit} = 2 \cdot \frac{\lim_{4x \to 0} \frac{\sin 4x}{4x}}{\lim_{2x \to 0} \frac{\sin 2x}{2x}}$$
  4. Substitute the standard limit values: $$\text{Limit} = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{1} = 2$$

Final Evaluation Output: $2$

4. Derivatives

The derivative of a function represents its instantaneous rate of change with respect to the independent variable. Geometrically, it is the slope of the tangent line to the curve at a given point.

Definition and the First Principle of Derivative
  • Derivative at a Point: Let $f$ be a real-valued function and $a$ be a point in its domain. The derivative of $f$ at $a$, denoted by $f'(a)$, is defined as: $$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$$ provided this limit exists.
  • First Principle of Derivative: The derivative function $f'(x)$ at any general point $x$ is defined as: $$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}$$
Algebra of Derivatives

Theorem 5: Let $f$ and $g$ be two functions whose derivatives are defined in a common domain. Let $u = f(x)$ and $v = g(x)$. Then:

  1. Sum Rule: $$\frac{d}{dx}[f(x) + g(x)] = \frac{d}{dx}f(x) + \frac{d}{dx}g(x)$$
  2. Difference Rule: $$\frac{d}{dx}[f(x) - g(x)] = \frac{d}{dx}f(x) - \frac{d}{dx}g(x)$$
  3. Product Rule (Leibnitz Rule): $$\frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \cdot g(x)] = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x) \quad \text{or} \quad (uv)' = u'v + uv'$$
  4. Quotient Rule: $$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)g(x) - f(x)g'(x)}{(g(x))^2} \quad \text{or} \quad \left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$$

Algorithmic Solved Examples

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the derivative of $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ using the First Principle.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Set up the limit using the First Principle definition: $$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{x+h} - \frac{1}{x}}{h}$$
  2. Simplify the numerator by finding a common denominator: $$\frac{1}{x+h} - \frac{1}{x} = \frac{x - (x+h)}{x(x+h)} = \frac{-h}{x(x+h)}$$
  3. Substitute this back into the limit expression: $$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{-h}{x(x+h)}}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \frac{-h}{h \cdot x(x+h)} \right]$$
  4. Cancel the common factor $h$ (valid since $h \neq 0$): $$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-1}{x(x+h)}$$
  5. Evaluate the limit by direct substitution of $h = 0$: $$f'(x) = \frac{-1}{x(x+0)} = -\frac{1}{x^2}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $-\frac{1}{x^2}$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the derivative of $f(x) = \cot x$ using the Quotient Rule.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Express $\cot x$ as a quotient of cosine and sine: $$f(x) = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}$$
  2. Identify the numerator $u = \cos x$ and denominator $v = \sin x$. Compute their individual derivatives: $$u' = -\sin x$$ $$v' = \cos x$$
  3. Apply the Quotient Rule formula $\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$: $$f'(x) = \frac{(-\sin x)(\sin x) - (\cos x)(\cos x)}{(\sin x)^2}$$
  4. Simplify the numerator using the fundamental Pythagorean identity $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$: $$\text{Numerator} = -\sin^2 x - \cos^2 x = -(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x) = -1$$
  5. Write the final simplified expression: $$f'(x) = \frac{-1}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc^2 x$$

Final Evaluation Output: $-\csc^2 x$

Historical Chronicle: The Invention of Calculus

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Calculus was independently developed in the late 17th century by two intellectual giants: Sir Isaac Newton (1642–1727) of England and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) of Germany. Newton approached calculus from a physical perspective (fluxions and rates of motion), while Leibniz approached it from a geometric and philosophical perspective (sums of infinitesimals).
  • Theoretical Foundations: Leibniz introduced the highly intuitive and enduring notation $\frac{dy}{dx}$ and the integral symbol $\int$. In the 19th century, mathematicians like Augustin-Louis Cauchy, Joseph-Louis Lagrange, and Karl Weierstrass placed calculus on a rigorous logical foundation using the formal concept of limits, resolving the logical ambiguities surrounding infinitesimals.
