A.2.1 & A.2.2 Introduction to Mathematical Modelling

Mathematical modelling is the process of converting a real-life physical situation into mathematical terms using suitable conditions, parameters, and variables. It is an activity where mathematical models are constructed to describe, analyze, and predict the behavior of real-world phenomena using words, drawings, computer programs, or mathematical formulae.

Why Do We Need Mathematical Modelling?

Many real-world problems cannot be solved directly by physical measurement or simple observation due to physical, safety, or financial constraints. Mathematical modelling provides a systematic technique to solve such problems by introducing physical laws and mathematical symbols. Examples of such problems include:

  • Finding the width of a wide river without crossing it.
  • Determining the optimal angle for a shot-put throw by analyzing variables like release height, air resistance, and gravity.
  • Estimating the height of an inaccessible tower.
  • Calculating the temperature at the surface of the Sun.
  • Understanding why heart patients are restricted from using lifts without invasive physiological testing.
  • Estimating the total mass of the Earth.
  • Predicting crop yields or blood volume without destructive harvesting or complete bleeding.
  • Estimating future population sizes (e.g., India in 2020) without waiting for the actual year.
A.2.3 Principles & Steps of Mathematical Modelling

Mathematical modelling is a principled, structured activity. The process follows a logical sequence of steps to ensure the model is consistent, realistic, and useful.

Core Principles of Modelling

The formulation of any mathematical model is guided by the following fundamental principles:

Principle Step Description & Key Questions Systemic Role
Identify the Need Determine what we are looking for and define the objective clearly. Establishes the model's goal.
List Parameters/Variables Identify all essential quantities required to describe the system. Defines the mathematical domain.
Identify Relevant Data Gather known values, constants, and experimental data. Provides boundary conditions.
Formulate Assumptions Simplify the physical situation by identifying applicable circumstances. Reduces complexity to a solvable level.
Identify Governing Laws Apply physical, chemical, or economic principles (e.g., Newton's laws, conservation laws). Provides the mathematical framework.
Formulate & Solve Equations Set up equations, perform calculations, and derive the mathematical solution. Generates the raw mathematical output.
Test & Validate Check the consistency and utility of the model against real-world observations. Ensures accuracy and reliability.
Refine & Improve Adjust parameter values or assumptions to improve the model's fit. Enables iterative optimization.
The Five-Step Algorithmic Process
  1. Step 1: Identify the physical situation. Define the real-world problem clearly.
  2. Step 2: Convert to a mathematical model. Introduce parameters, variables, physical laws, and symbols to translate the physical situation into mathematical equations.
  3. Step 3: Find the mathematical solution. Solve the equations using algebraic, matrix, calculus, or programming techniques.
  4. Step 4: Interpret and compare. Translate the mathematical solution back into the context of the original physical problem and compare it with experimental observations.
  5. Step 5: Validate or modify. If the results are in good agreement, accept the model. Otherwise, modify the hypotheses/assumptions and return to Step 2.
Static Algebraic & Matrix Models

When a system involves multiple interrelated variables with linear relationships, matrices provide an elegant framework for formulation and solution.

Example 1: Height of a Tower (Trigonometric Model)

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the height of a given tower using mathematical modelling.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Step 1 (Identify): The physical situation is to find the height of a tower $AB$ of height $H$.
  2. Step 2 (Formulate): Let an observer $PQ$ of height $h$ stand at a distance $l = PC = QB$ from the tower. Let $\alpha$ be the angle of elevation from the observer's eye at $P$ to the top of the tower $A$.
  3. Step 3 (Solve): From the right-angled triangle $ACP$, we have:
    $$\tan \alpha = \frac{AC}{PC} = \frac{H - h}{l}$$
    Rearranging for $H$, we get:
    $$H = h + l \tan \alpha$$
  4. Step 4 (Interpret): If the foot of the tower is inaccessible (so $l$ is unknown), let $\beta$ be the angle of depression from $P$ to the foot $B$. From $\Delta PQB$, we have:
    $$\tan \beta = \frac{PQ}{QB} = \frac{h}{l} \implies l = h \cot \beta$$
    Substituting $l$ back into the height equation yields:
    $$H = h + h \cot \beta \tan \alpha$$

Final Evaluation Output: $H = h(1 + \cot \beta \tan \alpha)$

Example 2 & 3: Matrix Model for Production Planning

Mathematical Problem Statement: A business firm produces three products $P_1, P_2, P_3$ using raw materials $R_1, R_2, R_3$. The firm has purchase orders from two clients $F_1, F_2$. Model the raw material requirements if the order matrix $A$ and raw material requirement matrix $B$ are given by:
$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 15 & 6 \\ 10 & 20 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 & 0 \\ 7 & 9 & 3 \\ 5 & 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$$
Determine if the orders can be met with available raw materials: 330 units of $R_1$, 455 units of $R_2$, and 140 units of $R_3$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Step 1 (Formulate): Let $A$ represent the purchase orders ($2 \times 3$ matrix, clients $\times$ products) and $B$ represent the raw material requirements per unit of product ($3 \times 3$ matrix, products $\times$ raw materials).
  2. Step 2 (Compute): The total raw material requirement matrix is given by the product $AB$:
    $$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 15 & 6 \\ 10 & 20 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 & 0 \\ 7 & 9 & 3 \\ 5 & 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$$
    $$AB = \begin{bmatrix} (10 \times 3 + 15 \times 7 + 6 \times 5) & (10 \times 4 + 15 \times 9 + 6 \times 12) & (10 \times 0 + 15 \times 3 + 6 \times 7) \\ (10 \times 3 + 20 \times 7 + 0 \times 5) & (10 \times 4 + 20 \times 9 + 0 \times 12) & (10 \times 0 + 20 \times 3 + 0 \times 7) \end{bmatrix}$$
    $$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 165 & 247 & 87 \\ 170 & 220 & 60 \end{bmatrix}$$
  3. Step 3 (Interpret): The total raw materials required are:
    - For $R_1$: $165 + 170 = 335$ units (Available: 330)
    - For $R_2$: $247 + 220 = 467$ units (Available: 455)
    - For $R_3$: $87 + 60 = 147$ units (Available: 140)
    Since the required amounts exceed the available stock, the orders cannot be fulfilled. The firm must either request more raw materials or ask clients to reduce their orders.

Final Evaluation Output: Total required: $[335, 467, 147]$, which exceeds available stock $[330, 455, 140]$.

Example 4: Resource Allocation via Systems of Linear Equations

Mathematical Problem Statement: Determine the exact number of units of $P_1, P_2, P_3$ (denoted by $x, y, z$) that must be produced to utilize the available raw materials ($330$ of $R_1$, $455$ of $R_2$, $140$ of $R_3$) completely, using the requirement matrix $B$ from Example 2.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Step 1 (Formulate): Set up the system of linear equations based on raw material consumption:
    - For $R_1$: $3x + 7y + 5z = 330$
    - For $R_2$: $4x + 9y + 12z = 455$
    - For $R_3$: $0x + 3y + 7z = 140$
  2. Step 2 (Matrix Representation): Express the system as $MX = D$:
    $$\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 7 & 5 \\ 4 & 9 & 12 \\ 0 & 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 330 \\ 455 \\ 140 \end{bmatrix}$$
  3. Step 3 (Solve): Apply elementary row operations to find the reduced row echelon form:
    $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 20 \\ 35 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $x = 20$ units of $P_1$, $y = 35$ units of $P_2$, and $z = 5$ units of $P_3$.

Optimization Models (Linear Programming)

When a system has constraints on resources and a linear objective function to maximize or minimize, Linear Programming (LP) is used.

Example 5: Medicine Production Scheduling

Mathematical Problem Statement: A manufacturer of medicines $M_1$ and $M_2$ has sufficient raw materials to make 20,000 bottles of $M_1$ and 40,000 bottles of $M_2$. Only 45,000 bottles are available in total. It takes 3 hours to prepare material for 1,000 bottles of $M_1$ and 1 hour for 1,000 bottles of $M_2$, with 66 hours available. Profit is Rs 8 per bottle of $M_1$ and Rs 7 per bottle of $M_2$. Maximize the profit.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Step 1 (Formulate): Let $x$ and $y$ be the number of bottles of $M_1$ and $M_2$ produced, respectively.
  2. Step 2 (Objective Function): Maximize profit $Z = 8x + 7y$.
  3. Step 3 (Constraints):
    - Production limits: $x \le 20000$, $y \le 40000$
    - Bottle limit: $x + y \le 45000$
    - Time limit: $\frac{3}{1000}x + \frac{1}{1000}y \le 66 \implies 3x + y \le 66000$
    - Non-negativity: $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$
  4. Step 4 (Solve): Identify the vertices of the feasible region $OPQRST$:
    - $O(0, 0) \implies Z = 0$
    - $P(20000, 0) \implies Z = 160,000$
    - $Q(20000, 6000) \implies Z = 202,000$
    - $R(10500, 34500) \implies Z = 325,500$
    - $S(5000, 40000) \implies Z = 320,000$
    - $T(0, 40000) \implies Z = 280,000$

Final Evaluation Output: Maximum profit of Rs 325,500 is achieved by producing $10,500$ bottles of $M_1$ and $34,500$ bottles of $M_2$.

Dynamic & Differential Equation Models

When variables change continuously over time, differential equations are used to model the rate of change of the system.

Example 6: Profitability & Break-Even Analysis

Let $x$ be the number of units produced and sold, $C$ be the total cost, $I$ be the income, and $P$ be the profit.

Variable Type Symbol Mathematical Definition / Formula
Independent Variable $x$ Number of units produced and sold
Dependent Variables $C, I, P$ $C = a + bx$ (where $a$ is fixed cost, $b$ is variable cost per unit)
$I = sx$ (where $s$ is selling price per unit)
$P = I - C = (s - b)x - a$
Parameters $a, b, s$ Constants determined by market and production conditions

The rate of change of profit with respect to production is given by:
$$\frac{dP}{dx} = s - b$$
The break-even point (where $P = 0$) occurs at:
$$x = \frac{a}{s - b}$$

Example 7: Mixing Tank Brine Problem

Mathematical Problem Statement: A tank contains 1,000 liters of brine with 250 g of salt per liter. Brine containing 200 g of salt per liter flows in at 25 L/min, and the well-stirred mixture flows out at the same rate. Find the amount of salt $y(t)$ in the tank at any time $t$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Step 1 (Initial Condition): At $t = 0$, the initial salt content is:
    $$y(0) = 250 \text{ g/L} \times 1000 \text{ L} = 250,000 \text{ g} = 250 \text{ kg}$$
  2. Step 2 (Formulate Differential Equation):
    - Inflow rate of salt: $25 \text{ L/min} \times 0.2 \text{ kg/L} = 5 \text{ kg/min}$
    - Outflow rate of salt: $25 \text{ L/min} \times \frac{y}{1000} \text{ kg/L} = \frac{y}{40} \text{ kg/min}$
    - Net rate of change:
    $$\frac{dy}{dt} = 5 - \frac{y}{40} \implies \frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{1}{40}y = 5$$
  3. Step 3 (Solve): This is a first-order linear differential equation. The integrating factor is $I.F. = e^{\int \frac{1}{40} dt} = e^{t/40}$. Multiplying and integrating:
    $$y e^{t/40} = \int 5 e^{t/40} dt = 200 e^{t/40} + C \implies y(t) = 200 + C e^{-t/40}$$
    Using $y(0) = 250$:
    $$250 = 200 + C \implies C = 50$$
    Thus, the solution is:
    $$y(t) = 200 + 50 e^{-t/40}$$
  4. Step 4 (Interpret): Since $e^{-t/40}$ is always positive, $y(t) > 200$ at all \times. As $t \to \infty$, $y(t) \to 200$ kg. The minimum salt content in the tank asymptotically approaches 200 kg.

Final Evaluation Output: $y(t) = 200 + 50 e^{-t/40}$ kg, with time $t = 40 \log_e \left( \frac{50}{y - 200} \right)$ minutes.

Limitations of Mathematical Modelling

While mathematical models are incredibly powerful, they have inherent limitations:

  • Complexity: Many real-world situations are too complex to be captured by simple equations, requiring too many variables or parameters.
  • Intractability: The resulting mathematical models may be mathematically impossible or extremely difficult to solve analytically.
  • Parameter Estimation: There are often no clear guidelines for choosing or accurately estimating the parameters and variables used in a model.
  • Overfitting: It is possible to fit any data perfectly by choosing five or six parameters, but this reduces the model's predictive utility. A minimal number of parameters is preferred for robust estimation.

Historical Chronicle / Epigraph: John von Neumann & Mathematical Modelling

  • Historical Context & Milestones: John von Neumann (1903–1957) was a pioneer in applying mathematical models to economics (Game Theory), meteorology (numerical weather prediction), and computation.
  • Theoretical Foundations: He famously remarked, "The sciences do not try to explain, they hardly even try to interpret, they mainly make models. By a model is meant a mathematical construct which, with the addition of certain verbal interpretations, describes observed phenomena."
