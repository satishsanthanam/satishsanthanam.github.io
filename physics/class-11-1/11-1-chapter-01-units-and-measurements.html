Physics: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 1: Units and Measurement

1.1 Introduction to Units and Measurement

Measurement of any physical quantity involves comparison with a certain basic, arbitrarily chosen, internationally accepted reference standard called unit . The result of a measurement of a physical quantity is expressed by a number (or numerical measure) accompanied by a unit.

Physical quantities are broadly classified into two categories:

  • Fundamental or Base Quantities: These are independent physical quantities that cannot be expressed in terms of other quantities. Their units are called fundamental or base units .
  • Derived Quantities: All other physical quantities whose units can be expressed as combinations of the base units. Their units are called derived units .

A complete set of these units, both the base units and derived units, is known as the system of units .

1.2 The International System of Units (SI)

Historically, three main systems of units were extensively used in different countries:

  • CGS System: Centimetre, gram, and second for length, mass, and time respectively.
  • FPS System: Foot, pound, and second (British system) for length, mass, and time respectively.
  • MKS System: Metre, kilogram, and second for length, mass, and time respectively.

The system of units which is at present internationally accepted for measurement is the Système International d’Unités (French for International System of Units), abbreviated as SI . It was developed by the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures (BIPM) in 1971 and recently revised by the General Conference on Weights and Measures in November 2018.

SI Base Quantities and Units

The SI is founded on seven base quantities, defined as follows:

Base Quantity Unit Name Symbol Definition and Constants
Length metre m Defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the speed of light in vacuum $c$ to be **299792458** when expressed in the unit **m s⁻¹**, where the second is defined in terms of the caesium frequency $\Delta \nu_{Cs}$.
Mass kilogram kg Defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the Planck constant $h$ to be **6.62607015** $\times$ **10⁻³⁴** when expressed in the unit **J s**, which is equal to **kg m² s⁻¹**, where the metre and second are defined in terms of $c$ and $\Delta \nu_{Cs}$.
Time second s Defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the caesium frequency $\Delta \nu_{Cs}$, the unperturbed ground-state hyperfine transition frequency of the caesium-133 atom, to be **9192631770** when expressed in the unit **Hz**, which is equal to **s⁻¹**.
Electric Current ampere A Defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the elementary charge $e$ to be **1.602176634** $\times$ **10⁻¹⁹** when expressed in the unit **C**, which is equal to **A s**, where the second is defined in terms of $\Delta \nu_{Cs}$.
Thermodynamic Temperature kelvin K Defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the Boltzmann constant $k$ to be **1.380649** $\times$ **10⁻²³** when expressed in the unit **J K⁻¹**, which is equal to **kg m² s⁻² K⁻¹**, where the kilogram, metre and second are defined in terms of $h$, $c$ and $\Delta \nu_{Cs}$.
Amount of Substance mole mol One mole contains exactly **6.02214076** $\times$ **10²³** elementary entities. This number is the fixed numerical value of the Avogadro constant, $N_A$, when expressed in the unit **mol⁻¹** and is called the Avogadro number.
Luminous Intensity candela cd Defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the luminous efficacy of monochromatic radiation of frequency **540** $\times$ **10¹² Hz**, $K_{cd}$, to be **683** when expressed in the unit **lm W⁻¹**, which is equal to **cd sr W⁻¹**, or **cd sr kg⁻¹ m⁻² s³**, where the kilogram, metre and second are defined in terms of $h$, $c$ and $\Delta \nu_{Cs}$.
Supplementary Units: Plane Angle and Solid Angle

Besides the seven base units, there are two supplementary units defined for dimensionless geometric quantities:

  • Plane Angle ($d\theta$): Defined as the ratio of length of arc $ds$ to the radius $r$. The unit is radian (symbol: rad ). $$d\theta = \frac{ds}{r}$$
  • Solid Angle ($d\Omega$): Defined as the ratio of the intercepted area $dA$ of the spherical surface, described about the apex $O$ as the centre, to the square of its radius $r$. The unit is steradian (symbol: sr ). $$d\Omega = \frac{dA}{r^2}$$

1.3 Significant Figures

Every measurement involves errors. The reported result of a measurement is a number that includes all digits in the number that are known reliably plus the first digit that is uncertain. These reliable digits plus the first uncertain digit are known as significant figures or significant digits.

Rules for Determining Significant Figures
  1. All non-zero digits are significant. (e.g., **287.5 cm** has four significant figures).
  2. All zeros between two non-zero digits are significant , no matter where the decimal point is, if at all. (e.g., **2.308 cm** has four significant figures).
  3. If the number is less than 1, the zero(s) on the right of decimal point but to the left of the first non-zero digit are not significant. (e.g., in **0.002308**, the underlined zeroes are not significant, leaving four significant figures).
  4. The terminal or trailing zero(s) in a number without a decimal point are not significant. (e.g., **123 m = 12300 cm = 123000 mm** has only three significant figures).
  5. The trailing zero(s) in a number with a decimal point are significant. (e.g., **3.500** or **0.06900** have four significant figures).
  6. Scientific Notation: To remove ambiguities, every measurement should be reported in scientific notation as $a \times 10^b$, where $a$ is a number between 1 and 10, and $b$ is any positive or negative exponent of 10. The power of 10 is irrelevant to significant figures, and all zeroes in $a$ are significant. (e.g., **4.700** $\times$ **10³ mm** has four significant figures).
1.3.1 Rules for Arithmetic Operations with Significant Figures
  • Multiplication or Division: The final result should retain as many significant figures as are there in the original number with the least significant figures.

    Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the density of an object of mass **4.237 g** and volume **2.51 cm³** to appropriate significant figures.

    Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

    1. Identify the significant figures in the given values: Mass = **4.237 g** (4 significant figures), Volume = **2.51 cm³** (3 significant figures).
    2. The least number of significant figures is 3 (from the volume).
    3. Calculate the raw density: $$\text{Density} = \frac{4.237\text{ g}}{2.51\text{ cm}^3} \approx 1.6880478\text{ g cm}^{-3}$$
    4. Round off the result to 3 significant figures.

    Final Evaluation Output: $1.69\text{ g cm}^{-3}$

  • Addition or Subtraction: The final result should retain as many decimal places as are there in the number with the least decimal places.

    Mathematical Problem Statement: Sum the numbers **436.32 g**, **227.2 g**, and **0.301 g**.

    Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

    1. Identify the decimal places: **436.32 g** (2 decimal places), **227.2 g** (1 decimal place), **0.301 g** (3 decimal places).
    2. The least precise measurement has 1 decimal place (**227.2 g**).
    3. Calculate the raw sum: $$436.32\text{ g} + 227.2\text{ g} + 0.301\text{ g} = 663.821\text{ g}$$
    4. Round off the sum to 1 decimal place.

    Final Evaluation Output: $663.8\text{ g}$

1.3.2 Rounding off the Uncertain Digits

The rules for rounding off numbers to appropriate significant figures are:

  1. If the insignificant digit to be dropped is more than 5, the preceding digit is raised by 1. (e.g., **2.746** rounded to three significant figures is **2.75**).
  2. If the insignificant digit to be dropped is less than 5, the preceding digit is left unchanged. (e.g., **1.743** rounded to three significant figures is **1.74**).
  3. If the insignificant digit to be dropped is exactly 5:
    • If the preceding digit is even, the insignificant digit is simply dropped. (e.g., **2.745** becomes **2.74**).
    • If the preceding digit is odd, the preceding digit is raised by 1. (e.g., **2.735** becomes **2.74**).
1.3.3 Rules for Determining the Uncertainty in Arithmetic Calculations

When combining measurements with uncertainties, the following principles apply:

  • If a set of experimental data is specified to $n$ significant figures, a result obtained by combining the data will also be valid to $n$ significant figures.
  • When subtracting data, the number of significant figures can be reduced because uncertainties combine in a different fashion (based on decimal places).
  • Intermediate results in a multi-step computation should be calculated to one more significant figure than the number of digits in the least precise measurement to avoid accumulation of rounding errors.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Each side of a cube is measured to be **7.203 m**. Find the total surface area and the volume of the cube to appropriate significant figures.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. The measured length has 4 significant figures (**7.203 m**).
  2. Calculate the surface area: $$\text{Surface Area} = 6 \times (7.203\text{ m})^2 = 6 \times 51.883209\text{ m}^2 = 311.299254\text{ m}^2$$
  3. Round off the surface area to 4 significant figures: **311.3 m²**.
  4. Calculate the volume: $$\text{Volume} = (7.203\text{ m})^3 = 373.714754\text{ m}^3$$
  5. Round off the volume to 4 significant figures: **373.7 m³**.

Final Evaluation Output: Surface Area = $311.3\text{ m}^2$, Volume = $373.7\text{ m}^3$

1.4 Dimensions of Physical Quantities

The nature of a physical quantity is described by its dimensions . All physical quantities represented by derived units can be expressed in terms of some combination of seven fundamental or base quantities, denoted with square brackets $[ ]$:

  • Length: $[L]$
  • Mass: $[M]$
  • Time: $[T]$
  • Electric Current: $[A]$
  • Thermodynamic Temperature: $[K]$
  • Luminous Intensity: $[cd]$
  • Amount of Substance: $[mol]$

The dimensions of a physical quantity are the powers (or exponents) to which the base quantities are raised to represent that quantity.

For example, the dimensions of volume are $[L^3]$, which can be written as $[M^0 L^3 T^0]$. The dimensions of force are $[M L T^{-2}]$, meaning force has 1 dimension in mass, 1 dimension in length, and -2 dimensions in time.

1.5 Dimensional Formulae and Dimensional Equations

The expression which shows how and which of the base quantities represent the dimensions of a physical quantity is called the dimensional formula of the given physical quantity.

An equation obtained by equating a physical quantity with its dimensional formula is called the dimensional equation of the physical quantity.

Physical Quantity Dimensional Formula Dimensional Equation
Volume ($V$) $[M^0 L^3 T^0]$ $[V] = [M^0 L^3 T^0]$
Speed or Velocity ($v$) $[M^0 L T^{-1}]$ $[v] = [M^0 L T^{-1}]$
Acceleration ($a$) $[M^0 L T^{-2}]$ $[a] = [M^0 L T^{-2}]$
Force ($F$) $[M L T^{-2}]$ $[F] = [M L T^{-2}]$
Mass Density (${\rho}$) $[M L^{-3} T^0]$ $[\rho] = [M L^{-3} T^0]$
1.6 Dimensional Analysis and its Applications

Dimensional analysis is a powerful tool used to check the correctness of mathematical expressions and to deduce relations among physical quantities. It is based on the principle of homogeneity of dimensions , which states that physical quantities can be added or subtracted only if they have the same dimensions.

1.6.1 Checking the Dimensional Consistency of Equations

If the dimensions of all terms on both sides of an equation are not the same, the equation is wrong. However, dimensional consistency does not guarantee that an equation is physically correct, as it cannot account for dimensionless constants or functions (like trigonometric, logarithmic, or exponential functions, which must be dimensionless).

Mathematical Problem Statement: Check the dimensional consistency of the equation: $$x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2$$ where $x$ and $x_0$ are distances, $v_0$ is velocity, $a$ is acceleration, and $t$ is time.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Write the dimensions of each term:
    • $[x] = [L]$
    • $[x_0] = [L]$
    • $[v_0 t] = [L T^{-1}] [T] = [L]$
    • $[\frac{1}{2} a t^2] = [L T^{-2}] [T^2] = [L]$
  2. Since each term on both sides of the equation has the same dimension of length $[L]$, the equation is dimensionally correct.

Final Evaluation Output: The equation is dimensionally consistent.

1.6.2 Deducing Relations among Physical Quantities

By knowing the dependence of a physical quantity on other quantities, we can derive its mathematical relation using the method of dimensions (up to three independent variables).

Mathematical Problem Statement: Derive the expression for the time period $T$ of a simple pendulum, assuming it depends on the length of the string $l$, mass of the bob $m$, and acceleration due to gravity $g$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Express the dependence as a product: $$T = k l^x g^y m^z$$ where $k$ is a dimensionless constant.
  2. Substitute the dimensions of each quantity: $$[M^0 L^0 T^1] = [L]^x [L T^{-2}]^y [M]^z = [M^z L^{x+y} T^{-2y}]$$
  3. Equate the exponents of $[M]$, $[L]$, and $[T]$ on both sides:
    • For $[M]$: $z = 0$
    • For $[T]$: $-2y = 1 \implies y = -\frac{1}{2}$
    • For $[L]$: $x + y = 0 \implies x = -y = \frac{1}{2}$
  4. Substitute the values of $x$, $y$, and $z$ back into the equation: $$T = k l^{1/2} g^{-1/2} m^0 = k \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$$
  5. Using experimental analysis, the constant $k$ is found to be $2\pi$.

Final Evaluation Output: $T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$
