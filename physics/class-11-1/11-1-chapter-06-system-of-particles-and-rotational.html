Physics: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 6: Systems of Particles and Rotational Motion

6.1 Introduction & The Concept of a Rigid Body

In classical mechanics, real-world objects are often modeled as systems of particles. While a single particle is idealized as a point mass with no spatial dimensions, real extended bodies have finite sizes and complex internal structures. To analyze their motion, we introduce the idealized concept of a rigid body .

Definition and Physical Reality
  • An ideal rigid body is defined as a system of particles in which the distance between any two constituent particles remains perfectly constant and unchanging over time, regardless of the external forces applied to it.
  • In reality, no macroscopic body is perfectly rigid, as all materials undergo some degree of deformation under external loads. However, for many practical engineering and physical systems (such as wheels, steel beams, planets, and spinning tops), these deformations are negligible, allowing us to treat them as rigid.
Types of Motion of a Rigid Body
  1. Pure Translational Motion: At any given instant of time, every particle of the body moves with the exact same velocity vector. An example is a rectangular block sliding down a frictionless inclined plane without rotating.
  2. Pure Rotational Motion: The body is constrained such that it rotates about a fixed line called the axis of rotation . Every constituent particle moves in a circular path that lies in a plane perpendicular to this axis, with the center of each circle lying on the axis itself. Particles located directly on the axis remain stationary.
  3. Combined Translational and Rotational Motion: The body simultaneously translates and rotates. A classic example is a cylinder rolling down an inclined plane; while the center of mass translates down the slope, the individual particles exhibit varying velocities due to simultaneous rotation.
6.2 Centre of Mass (COM)

The centre of mass is a unique point associated with a system of particles that behaves as if the entire mass of the system were concentrated there, and all external forces were applied directly at this point.

Mathematical Formulation for Discrete Systems

For a system of $n$ discrete particles, the position of the centre of mass is calculated as a mass-weighted average of the individual particle positions:

  • Two-Particle System along the x-axis: $$X = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$
  • Multi-Particle System in Three Dimensions: $$X = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i x_i}{M}, \quad Y = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i y_i}{M}, \quad Z = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i z_i}{M}$$where $M = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i$ is the total mass of the system.
  • Vector Notation: $$\vec{R} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i \vec{r}_i}{M}$$
Continuous Mass Distributions

For continuous rigid bodies, the discrete summations are replaced by integrals over the volume of the body:

$$\vec{R} = \frac{1}{M} \int \vec{r} \\, dm$$

By utilizing geometric symmetry, we can locate the centre of mass of homogeneous bodies (bodies with uniform mass density):

  • For a homogeneous thin rod, the centre of mass lies at its geometric midpoint.
  • For homogeneous rings, discs, spheres, and cylinders, the centre of mass coincides with their respective geometric centers.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the centre of mass of three particles located at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side length 0.5 m . The masses of the particles are 100 g , 150 g , and 200 g respectively.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Set the origin $(0,0)$ at the position of the 100 g mass ($m_1$).
  2. The 150 g mass ($m_2$) lies on the x-axis at $(0.5, 0)$.
  3. The 200 g mass ($m_3$) lies at the third vertex. Its coordinates are calculated using trigonometry:$$x_3 = 0.5 \times \cos(60^\circ) = 0.25 \text{ m}$$$$y_3 = 0.5 \times \sin(60^\circ) = 0.25\sqrt{3} \text{ m}$$
  4. Calculate the total mass: $M = 100 + 150 + 200 = 450 \text{ g}$.
  5. Compute the x-coordinate of the COM ($X$):$$X = \frac{100(0) + 150(0.5) + 200(0.25)}{450} = \frac{75 + 50}{450} = \frac{125}{450} = \frac{5}{18} \text{ m} \approx 0.28 \text{ m}$$
  6. Compute the y-coordinate of the COM ($Y$):$$Y = \frac{100(0) + 150(0) + 200(0.25\sqrt{3})}{450} = \frac{50\sqrt{3}}{450} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}} \text{ m} \approx 0.19 \text{ m}$$

Final Evaluation Output: The coordinates of the centre of mass are $(\frac{5}{18} \text{ m}, \frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}} \text{ m})$.

6.3 Motion of the Centre of Mass & Linear Momentum

The physical significance of the centre of mass becomes clear when we analyze its kinematics and dynamics under the influence of external forces.

Velocity and Acceleration of the COM

Differentiating the position vector of the centre of mass with respect to time yields:

  1. Velocity of the Centre of Mass: $$\vec{V} = \frac{d\vec{R}}{dt} = \frac{\sum m_i \vec{v}_i}{M}$$
  2. Acceleration of the Centre of Mass: $$\vec{A} = \frac{d\vec{V}}{dt} = \frac{\sum m_i \vec{a}_i}{M}$$
Newton's Second Law for a System of Particles

Multiplying the acceleration equation by the total mass $M$ gives:

$$M\vec{A} = \sum m_i \vec{a}_i = \sum \vec{F}_i$$

According to Newton's third law, internal forces between the particles of the system occur in equal and opposite pairs and cancel out in the summation. Thus, only external forces contribute to the acceleration of the centre of mass:

$$M\vec{A} = \vec{F}_{ext}$$

This fundamental result states that the centre of mass of a system of particles moves as if the entire mass of the system were concentrated at that point, and all external forces were applied directly to it.

Linear Momentum and its Conservation

The total linear momentum $\vec{P}$ of a system of particles is the vector sum of the individual momenta of its constituent particles:

$$\vec{P} = \sum m_i \vec{v}_i = M\vec{V}$$

Differentiating this with respect to time yields the system-wide extension of Newton's second law:

$$\frac{d\vec{P}}{dt} = \vec{F}_{ext}$$

If the net external force acting on the system is zero ($\vec{F}_{ext} = 0$), the total linear momentum remains constant:

$$\vec{P} = \text{constant} \implies \vec{V} = \text{constant}$$

This is the law of conservation of linear momentum for a system of particles. It \implies that in the absence of external forces, the velocity of the centre of mass remains completely unaffected by internal interactions (such as radioactive decay or internal explosions).

6.4 Vector Product of Two Vectors (Cross Product)

Rotational dynamics relies heavily on the vector product to define directional rotational quantities such as torque and angular momentum.

Definition and Properties

The vector product (or cross product ) of two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ is a vector $\vec{c}$ defined as:

$$\vec{c} = \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = (ab \sin\theta) \hat{n}$$where $\theta$ is the angle between the two vectors ($0 \le \theta \le 180^\circ$) and $\hat{n}$ is a unit vector perpendicular to the plane containing $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, with its direction determined by the right-hand rule.
  • Non-commutativity: $\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = -(\vec{b} \times \vec{a})$
  • Distributive Property: $\vec{a} \times (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) = \vec{a} \times \vec{b} + \vec{a} \times \vec{c}$
  • Self-Product: $\vec{a} \times \vec{a} = 0$ (since $\sin 0^\circ = 0$)
Orthogonal Unit Vectors

For the standard Cartesian unit vectors $\hat{i}$, $\hat{j}$, and $\hat{k}$:

$$\hat{i} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k} \times \hat{k} = 0$$$$\hat{i} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k}, \quad \hat{j} \times \hat{k} = \hat{i}, \quad \hat{k} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j}$$$$\hat{j} \times \hat{i} = -\hat{k}, \quad \hat{k} \times \hat{j} = -\hat{i}, \quad \hat{i} \times \hat{k} = -\hat{j}$$
Determinant Representation

The cross product can be computed systematically using a $3 \times 3$ determinant:

$$\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_x & a_y & a_z \\ b_x & b_y & b_z \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i}(a_y b_z - a_z b_y) - \hat{j}(a_x b_z - a_z b_x) + \hat{k}(a_x b_y - a_y b_x)$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the vector product $\vec{a} \times \vec{b}$ of the vectors $\vec{a} = 3\hat{i} - 4\hat{j} + 5\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = -2\hat{i} + \hat{j} - 3\hat{k}$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Set up the determinant:$$\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 3 & -4 & 5 \\ -2 & 1 & -3 \end{vmatrix}$$
  2. Expand along the first row:$$\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \hat{i}[(-4)(-3) - (5)(1)] - \hat{j}[(3)(-3) - (5)(-2)] + \hat{k}[(3)(1) - (-4)(-2)]$$
  3. Simplify the components:$$\text{x-component: } 12 - 5 = 7$$$$\text{y-component: } -[-9 - (-10)] = -[-9 + 10] = -1$$$$\text{z-component: } 3 - 8 = -5$$

Final Evaluation Output: $\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = 7\hat{i} - \hat{j} - 5\hat{k}$.

6.5 Angular Velocity and its Relation with Linear Velocity

To describe the rotational kinematics of a rigid body, we define angular variables that apply to the body as a whole.

Angular Velocity ($\vec{\omega}$)
  • As a rigid body rotates about a fixed axis, any particle $P$ describes a circular path of radius $r_\perp$ perpendicular to the axis.
  • The rate of change of the angular displacement $\theta$ is the angular velocity :$$\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}$$
  • For a rigid body, $\omega$ is identical for all constituent particles at any given instant. Thus, we refer to $\omega$ as the angular velocity of the entire body.
  • Vectorially, $\vec{\omega}$ lies along the axis of rotation, with its direction given by the right-hand screw rule.
Vector Relation: $\vec{v} = \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}$

The linear velocity $\vec{v}$ of a particle at position vector $\vec{r}$ relative to an origin on the axis of rotation is given by the cross product:

$$\vec{v} = \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}$$

This vector is perpendicular to both $\vec{\omega}$ and $\vec{r}$, pointing along the tangent to the circular path of the particle.

Angular Acceleration ($\vec{\alpha}$)

The rate of change of angular velocity is defined as the angular acceleration :

$$\vec{\alpha} = \frac{d\vec{\omega}}{dt}$$

For rotation about a fixed axis, the direction of $\vec{\omega}$ is constant, so the vector equation simplifies to the scalar relation:

$$\alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt}$$
6.6 Torque and Angular Momentum

These two vector quantities form the dynamic foundation of rotational mechanics, acting as the rotational analogues of force and linear momentum.

Torque (Moment of Force)

The rotational effectiveness of a force $\vec{F}$ acting on a particle at position $\vec{r}$ relative to an origin is defined as the torque $\vec{\tau}$:

$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F}$$
  • Magnitude: $\tau = r F \sin\theta = r_\perp F = r F_\perp$ (where $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from the origin to the line of action of the force).
  • SI Unit: Newton-meter ( N m ).
  • Dimensional Formula: $[M L^2 T^{-2}]$ (same as work/energy, but torque is a vector while work is a scalar).
Angular Momentum of a Particle

The rotational analogue of linear momentum for a particle with momentum $\vec{p}$ at position $\vec{r}$ is the angular momentum $\vec{l}$:

$$\vec{l} = \vec{r} \times \vec{p}$$
  • Magnitude: $l = r p \sin\theta = r_\perp p$
  • Relation to Torque: Differentiating $\vec{l}$ with respect to time yields:$$\frac{d\vec{l}}{dt} = \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} \times \vec{p} + \vec{r} \times \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} = \vec{v} \times (m\vec{v}) + \vec{r} \times \vec{F} = 0 + \vec{\tau} = \vec{\tau}$$
  • Thus, the time rate of change of angular momentum is equal to the torque acting on the particle:$$\vec{\tau} = \frac{d\vec{l}}{dt}$$
System of Particles and Conservation of Angular Momentum

For a system of particles, the total angular momentum is $\vec{L} = \sum \vec{l}_i$. Summing the individual torques and canceling internal torque contributions (assuming central internal forces) yields:

$$\vec{\tau}_{ext} = \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt}$$

If the net external torque acting on the system is zero ($\vec{\tau}_{ext} = 0$), the total angular momentum is conserved:

$$\vec{L} = \text{constant}$$
6.7 Equilibrium of a Rigid Body

A rigid body is in mechanical equilibrium if its state of motion does not change over time.

Conditions for Mechanical Equilibrium

A rigid body is in complete mechanical equilibrium if it satisfies two independent conditions:

  1. Translational Equilibrium: The vector sum of all external forces acting on the body must be zero:$$\sum \vec{F}_i = 0$$
  2. Rotational Equilibrium: The vector sum of all external torques acting on the body about any arbitrary origin must be zero:$$\sum \vec{\tau}_i = 0$$
The Concept of a Couple
  • A couple is defined as a pair of equal and opposite forces acting on a body along different parallel lines of action.
  • A couple produces pure rotational motion without any translational motion, as the net force is zero ($\sum \vec{F} = 0$), but the net torque is non-zero ($\sum \vec{\tau} \neq 0$).
  • The moment of a couple is independent of the choice of origin and is equal to the product of the magnitude of one of the forces and the perpendicular distance between their lines of action.
Principle of Moments (The Lever)

An ideal lever is a light rod pivoted at a point called the fulcrum . When a load $F_1$ is placed at a distance $d_1$ (load arm) and an effort $F_2$ is applied at a distance $d_2$ (effort arm), rotational equilibrium about the fulcrum requires:

$$F_1 d_1 = F_2 d_2$$

The mechanical advantage (M.A.) is defined as:

$$\text{M.A.} = \frac{F_1}{F_2} = \frac{d_2}{d_1}$$
Centre of Gravity (CG)
  • The centre of gravity of a body is the point where the net gravitational torque on the body is zero.
  • In a uniform gravitational field, the centre of gravity coincides exactly with the centre of mass of the body.
6.8 Moment of Inertia & Radius of Gyration

The rotational analogue of mass is the moment of inertia, which measures a body's resistance to rotational acceleration.

Moment of Inertia ($I$)

For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, the total kinetic energy of rotation is the sum of the kinetic energies of its individual particles:

$$K = \sum \frac{1}{2} m_i v_i^2 = \sum \frac{1}{2} m_i (r_i \omega)^2 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \sum m_i r_i^2 \right) \omega^2$$

We define the moment of inertia $I$ as:

$$I = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i r_i^2$$

This allows us to write the rotational kinetic energy in a form analogous to translational kinetic energy ($K = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$):

$$K = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2$$
Radius of Gyration ($k$)

The radius of gyration $k$ of a body about a given axis is the radial distance from the axis to a point where the entire mass of the body could be concentrated to yield the same moment of inertia:

$$I = M k^2 \implies k = \sqrt{\frac{I}{M}}$$

6.9 Kinematics and Dynamics of Rotational Motion

Rotational motion about a fixed axis can be described using equations analogous to those of linear motion.

Kinematic Equations for Constant Angular Acceleration
Linear Motion (Constant $a$) Rotational Motion (Constant $\alpha$)
$v = v_0 + at$ $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$
$x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} at^2$ $\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2} \alpha t^2$
$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)$ $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha(\theta - \theta_0)$
Dynamics of Rotational Motion
  • Work Done by a Torque: $dW = \tau \\, d\theta$
  • Rotational Power: $P = \tau \omega$
  • Newton's Second Law for Rotation: $\tau = I \alpha$
  • Angular Momentum about a Fixed Axis: $L = I \omega$

Mathematical Problem Statement: A flywheel of mass 20 kg and radius 20 cm is mounted on a frictionless horizontal axle. A steady pull of 25 N is applied to a cord wound around its rim. Find the angular acceleration of the flywheel.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Convert the radius to standard SI units: $R = 20 \text{ cm} = 0.20 \text{ m}$.
  2. Calculate the torque ($\tau$) exerted by the pull:$$\tau = F \times R = 25 \text{ N} \times 0.20 \text{ m} = 5.0 \text{ N m}$$
  3. Model the flywheel as a solid disc to find its moment of inertia ($I$):$$I = \frac{1}{2} M R^2 = \frac{1}{2} (20 \text{ kg}) (0.20 \text{ m})^2 = 10 \times 0.04 = 0.4 \text{ kg m}^2$$
  4. Apply Newton's second law for rotation to find the angular acceleration ($\alpha$):$$\alpha = \frac{\tau}{I} = \frac{5.0 \text{ N m}}{0.4 \text{ kg m}^2} = 12.5 \text{ rad/s}^2$$

Final Evaluation Output: The angular acceleration of the flywheel is 12.5 rad/s² .

Historical Chronicle: Leonhard Euler and Rigid Body Dynamics

  • Historical Context & Milestones: While Isaac Newton formulated the laws of motion for point masses, it was Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) who systematically extended these laws to rigid bodies. In 1750, Euler introduced the concept of resolving a rigid body's motion into the translation of its center of mass and rotation about that center.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Euler formulated the general equations of rotational motion (Euler's equations) and introduced the concept of principal moments of inertia, establishing the mathematical framework used today in aerospace engineering and robotics.
