Introduction & Historical Models of Planetary Motion

The study of gravitation began with the observation of everyday phenomena, such as objects falling towards the Earth and the motion of celestial bodies. Historically, different models were proposed to explain these observations.

Historical Chronicle: Geocentric vs. Heliocentric Models

  • Geocentric Model: Proposed by Ptolemy nearly 2000 years ago, this model placed the Earth at the center of the universe, with all celestial bodies revolving around it in complex circular paths called epicycles.
  • Heliocentric Model: A more elegant model placing the Sun at the center was mentioned by Indian astronomer Aryabhatta in the 5th century A.D. and later developed by Nicolas Copernicus. Galileo Galilei supported this model, facing prosecution for his beliefs.
  • Tycho Brahe & Johannes Kepler: Tycho Brahe spent his lifetime recording precise planetary observations with the naked eye. His assistant, Johannes Kepler, analyzed this data to formulate three elegant laws of planetary motion.
Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

Johannes Kepler formulated three laws that describe the motion of planets around the Sun. These laws laid the foundation for Newton's law of universal gravitation.

1. Law of Orbits

All planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun situated at one of the two foci of the ellipse. An ellipse is a closed curve where the sum of the distances from any point on the curve to the two foci is constant. The closest point to the Sun is called the perihelion , and the farthest point is the aphelion .

2. Law of Areas

The line joining any planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time. This \implies that the areal velocity of a planet is constant, meaning planets move faster when they are closer to the Sun and slower when farther away.

Derivation from Conservation of Angular Momentum:

The area swept out by a planet of mass $m$ in a small time interval $\Delta t$ is given by:

$$\Delta A = \frac{1}{2} |\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{v} \Delta t|$$

Dividing by $\Delta t$ and substituting linear momentum $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}$:

$$\frac{\Delta A}{\Delta t} = \frac{1}{2m} |\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}| = \frac{L}{2m}$$

Since gravity is a central force, the torque acting on the planet is zero, meaning its angular momentum ($L$) is conserved. Thus, the areal velocity $\frac{\Delta A}{\Delta t}$ is constant.

3. Law of Periods

The square of the time period of revolution ($T$) of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis ($a$) of its elliptical orbit:

$$T^2 \propto a^3$$

This law is confirmed by observational data for all planets in the solar system, where the ratio $T^2/a^3$ remains remarkably constant.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Let the speed of a planet at the perihelion be $v_P$ and the Sun-planet distance be $r_P$. Relate these to the corresponding quantities $v_A$ and $r_A$ at the aphelion. Will the planet take equal \times to traverse the two halves of the ellipse divided by the minor axis?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Apply the conservation of angular momentum at the perihelion and aphelion, where the velocity vectors are perpendicular to the position vectors: $$L_P = m r_P v_P \quad \text{and} \quad L_A = m r_A v_A$$
  2. Equate the angular momenta: $$m r_P v_P = m r_A v_A \implies \frac{v_P}{v_A} = \frac{r_A}{r_P}$$
  3. Since $r_A > r_P$, it follows that $v_P > v_A$.
  4. The area of the half containing the aphelion is larger than the half containing the perihelion. Since equal areas are swept in equal \times, the planet takes longer to traverse the half containing the aphelion.

Final Evaluation Output: $\frac{v_P}{v_A} = \frac{r_A}{r_P}$ and the planet takes longer to traverse the aphelion half.

Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation

Newton's law states that every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

Mathematical Formulation

The magnitude of the gravitational force $F$ between two point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ separated by a distance $r$ is:

$$F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

In vector form, the force $\mathbf{F}_{12}$ exerted on body 1 by body 2 is:

$$\mathbf{F}_{12} = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12}$$

where $G$ is the universal gravitational constant , and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{12}$ is the unit vector pointing from body 1 to body 2.

Principle of Superposition

For a system of multiple point masses, the net gravitational force on any single mass is the vector sum of the individual gravitational forces exerted on it by all other masses:

$$\mathbf{F}_1 = \sum_{i=2}^{n} \mathbf{F}_{1i} = G m_1 \sum_{i=2}^{n} \frac{m_i}{r_{i1}^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{i1}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Three equal masses of $m$ kg each are fixed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle ABC. What is the force acting on a mass $2m$ placed at the centroid G of the triangle? What is the force if the mass at vertex A is doubled? (Take $AG = BG = CG = 1$ m).

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. By symmetry, the gravitational forces exerted by the three equal masses at the vertices on the central mass $2m$ are equal in magnitude: $$F_{GA} = F_{GB} = F_{GC} = \frac{G(2m)(m)}{1^2} = 2Gm^2$$
  2. Resolve the forces into components. The forces along the x-axis cancel out, and the forces along the y-axis also sum to zero due to symmetric angular separation of 120° : $$\mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_{GA} + \mathbf{F}_{GB} + \mathbf{F}_{GC} = 0$$
  3. If the mass at vertex A is doubled to $2m$, the force $\mathbf{F}_{GA}$ doubles to $4Gm^2 \hat{\mathbf{j}}$. The other forces remain unchanged.
  4. The new resultant force is: $$\mathbf{F}_R' = 2Gm^2 \hat{\mathbf{j}}$$

Final Evaluation Output: Net force at centroid is $0$ initially, and becomes $2Gm^2 \hat{\mathbf{j}}$ when mass at A is doubled.

The Gravitational Constant (G)

The value of the universal gravitational constant $G$ was first determined experimentally by Henry Cavendish in 1798.

Cavendish's Experiment

The apparatus consisted of a light rigid bar with two small lead spheres of mass $m$ at its ends, suspended by a fine wire. Two large lead spheres of mass $M$ were brought close to the small spheres on opposite sides. The gravitational attraction produced a torque that twisted the suspension wire through an angle $\theta$.

At equilibrium, the gravitational torque equals the restoring torque of the wire:

$$G \frac{M m}{d^2} L = \tau \theta$$

where $L$ is the length of the bar, $d$ is the separation between the spheres, and $\tau$ is the restoring couple per unit angle of twist. The currently accepted value of $G$ is:

$$G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \text{ N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$$

Acceleration due to Gravity (g)

The acceleration experienced by a body due to the Earth's gravitational pull is denoted by $g$. It varies depending on the position relative to the Earth's surface.

Acceleration due to Gravity on Earth's Surface

Assuming the Earth is a uniform sphere of mass $M_E$ and radius $R_E$, the gravitational force on a mass $m$ on its surface is:

$$F = G \frac{M_E m}{R_E^2}$$

By Newton's second law, $F = mg$, which gives:

$$g = \frac{G M_E}{R_E^2}$$

Variation of g with Altitude (Height)

At a height $h$ above the Earth's surface, the acceleration due to gravity $g(h)$ is:

$$g(h) = \frac{G M_E}{(R_E + h)^2} = g \left(1 + \frac{h}{R_E}\right)^{-2}$$

For $h \ll R_E$, using the binomial approximation:

$$g(h) \approx g \left(1 - \frac{2h}{R_E}\right)$$

Variation of g with Depth

At a depth $d$ below the Earth's surface, only the inner sphere of radius $(R_E - d)$ contributes to the gravitational force. The acceleration due to gravity $g(d)$ is:

$$g(d) = g \left(1 - \frac{d}{R_E}\right)$$

Thus, $g$ is maximum at the surface of the Earth and decreases whether one goes upwards or downwards, becoming zero at the center of the Earth.

Gravitational Potential Energy

The gravitational potential energy of a system is the energy stored in it due to its configuration under the influence of gravitational forces.

Derivation of Potential Energy Function

The work done in lifting a mass $m$ from a distance $r_1$ to $r_2$ from the center of the Earth is:

$$W_{12} = \int_{r_1}^{r_2} \frac{G M_E m}{r^2} dr = -G M_E m \left(\frac{1}{r_2} - \frac{1}{r_1}\right)$$

We define the potential energy $W(r)$ at a distance $r$ by setting the potential energy at infinity to zero ($W(\infty) = 0$):

$$W(r) = -\frac{G M_E m}{r}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the potential energy of a system of four particles, each of mass $m$, placed at the vertices of a square of side $l$. Also obtain the potential at the center of the square.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify all pairs of particles. For 4 particles, there are $\frac{4 \times 3}{2} = 6$ pairs.
  2. Four pairs are along the sides of the square (distance $l$), and two pairs are along the diagonals (distance $\sqrt{2}l$).
  3. Sum the potential energies of all pairs: $$W(r) = -4 \frac{G m^2}{l} - 2 \frac{G m^2}{\sqrt{2}l} = -\frac{G m^2}{l} \left(4 + \sqrt{2}\right) \approx -5.41 \frac{G m^2}{l}$$
  4. The gravitational potential $U(r)$ at the center of the square (distance $r = \frac{l}{\sqrt{2}}$ from each vertex) is: $$U(r) = -4 \frac{G m}{l / \sqrt{2}} = -4\sqrt{2} \frac{G m}{l}$$

Final Evaluation Output: Total potential energy is $-5.41 \frac{G m^2}{l}$ and potential at the center is $-4\sqrt{2} \frac{G m}{l}$.

Escape Speed

The escape speed is the minimum speed required for a projectile to escape the gravitational influence of a celestial body and reach infinity.

Derivation of Escape Velocity

Using the principle of conservation of mechanical energy, the total energy of a projectile of mass $m$ projected with speed $v_i$ from the surface of the Earth must be greater than or equal to its energy at infinity (where potential energy is zero and kinetic energy is non-negative):

$$\frac{1}{2} m v_i^2 - \frac{G M_E m}{R_E} \ge 0$$

The minimum speed required corresponds to the equality:

$$v_e = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M_E}{R_E}} = \sqrt{2 g R_E}$$

For Earth, substituting $g = 9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$ and $R_E = 6400$ km gives:

$$v_e \approx 11.2 \text{ km/s}$$

For the Moon, the escape speed is only about 2.3 km/s , which is why gas molecules easily escape and the Moon has no atmosphere.

Earth Satellites & Orbital Mechanics

Satellites are natural or artificial bodies revolving around a planet. Their motion is governed by gravitational attraction acting as the necessary centripetal force.

Orbital Speed and Time Period

For a satellite of mass $m$ in a circular orbit at a height $h$ above the Earth's surface:

$$\frac{m V^2}{R_E + h} = \frac{G M_E m}{(R_E + h)^2} \implies V = \sqrt{\frac{G M_E}{R_E + h}}$$

The time period $T$ for one complete revolution is:

$$T = \frac{2\pi(R_E + h)}{V} = \frac{2\pi(R_E + h)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{G M_E}}$$

Squaring both sides yields Kepler's third law for satellites: $T^2 = k(R_E + h)^3$. For a satellite close to the Earth's surface ($h \approx 0$):

$$T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R_E}{g}} \approx 85 \text{ minutes}$$

Energy of an Orbiting Satellite

The total mechanical energy $E$ of a satellite is the sum of its kinetic and potential energies:

  • Kinetic Energy: $K.E. = \frac{1}{2} m V^2 = \frac{G M_E m}{2(R_E + h)}$
  • Potential Energy: $P.E. = -\frac{G M_E m}{R_E + h}$
  • Total Energy: $E = K.E. + P.E. = -\frac{G M_E m}{2(R_E + h)}$

The total energy is negative, indicating that the satellite is in a bound state. To escape, it must be supplied with energy equal to $+|E|$.
