14.1 Introduction to Waves

A wave is a physical disturbance that propagates through a medium or space without the actual physical transfer of matter as a whole. Waves transport both energy and information from one point to another.

Historical Chronicle: Christiaan Huygens, Robert Hooke, and Isaac Newton

  • Historical Context & Milestones: The scientific study of wave motion began in the 17th century. Christiaan Huygens proposed the wave theory of light, while Isaac Newton investigated the speed of sound in air.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Newton formulated the first theoretical expression for the speed of sound based on isothermal pressure variations, which was later corrected by Pierre-Simon Laplace using adiabatic assumptions.
Classification of Waves
  • Mechanical Waves: Require a material medium for propagation (e.g., sound waves, water waves, waves on a string). They depend on the elastic and inertial properties of the medium.
  • Electromagnetic Waves: Do not require a material medium and can propagate through a vacuum (e.g., light, X-rays, radio waves). In a vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light $c = 299,792,458\text{ m s}^{-1}$.
  • Matter Waves: Associated with moving fundamental particles like electrons, protons, and neutrons, governed by quantum mechanics.
14.2 Transverse and Longitudinal Waves

Waves are classified based on the direction of oscillation of the medium particles relative to the direction of wave propagation.

Transverse Waves

In a transverse wave , the particles of the medium oscillate perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. These waves can only propagate in media that can sustain shearing stress, such as solids.

Longitudinal Waves

In a longitudinal wave , the particles of the medium oscillate parallel to the direction of wave propagation. These waves can propagate in all elastic media (solids, liquids, and gases) because all states of matter can sustain compressive strain.

Water Waves: A Hybrid Phenomenon

Waves on the surface of water are a combination of both longitudinal and transverse motions. They are categorized into:

  • Capillary Waves: Short-wavelength ripples (less than a few centimeters) where the restoring force is dominated by surface tension.
  • Gravity Waves: Longer-wavelength waves where the restoring force is dominated by gravity.
14.3 Displacement Relation in a Progressive Wave

A progressive wave is a wave that travels continuously through a medium. Mathematically, a sinusoidal progressive wave travelling along the positive x-axis is represented by:

$$y(x, t) = a \sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)$$

Where:

  • $y(x, t)$ is the displacement of the particle at position $x$ and time $t$.
  • $a$ is the amplitude , representing the maximum displacement.
  • $kx - \omega t + \phi$ is the phase of the wave.
  • $\phi$ is the initial phase angle.
Wavelength and Wave Number

The wavelength ($\lambda$) is the minimum distance between two points having the same phase. The angular wave number ($k$) is defined as:

$$k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$$
Period, Frequency, and Angular Frequency

The period ($T$) is the time taken for a particle to complete one full oscillation. The frequency ($\nu$) and angular frequency ($\omega$) are given by:

$$\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi\nu$$
14.4 The Speed of a Travelling Wave

The speed of propagation of a wave is determined by the properties of the medium through which it travels.

Speed of a Transverse Wave on a Stretched String

The speed ($v$) depends on the tension ($T$) in the string and its linear mass density ($\mu$):

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: A steel wire 0.72 m long has a mass of 5.0 &× 10 -3 kg . If the wire is under a tension of 60 N , what is the speed of transverse waves on the wire?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Calculate the linear mass density ($\mu$):$$\mu = \frac{m}{L} = \frac{5.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}}{0.72\text{ m}} \approx 6.9 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg m}^{-1}$$
  2. Substitute $T = 60\text{ N}$ and $\mu$ into the speed formula:$$v = \sqrt{\frac{60}{6.9 \times 10^{-3}}} \approx 93\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $v = 93\text{ m s}^{-1}$

Speed of a Longitudinal Wave (Speed of Sound)

The speed of a longitudinal wave in a medium depends on its bulk modulus ($B$) and mass density ($\rho$):

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}}$$

For a solid bar, the bulk modulus is replaced by Young's modulus ($Y$):

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{Y}{\rho}}$$

For an ideal gas, Newton assumed the process to be isothermal ($B = P$), leading to Newton's formula:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{P}{\rho}}$$

Laplace corrected this by noting that pressure variations occur too rapidly for heat exchange, making the process adiabatic ($B = \gamma P$):

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Estimate the speed of sound in air at standard temperature and pressure (STP) using both Newton's formula and Laplace's correction. (Mass of 1 mole of air = 29.0 &× 10 -3 kg , $\gamma = 1.4$).

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Calculate the density of air ($\rho$) at STP:$$\rho = \frac{\text{mass of 1 mole}}{\text{volume of 1 mole}} = \frac{29.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}}{22.4 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^3} = 1.29\text{ kg m}^{-3}$$
  2. Calculate speed using Newton's formula ($P = 1.01 \times 10^5\text{ N m}^{-2}$):$$v = \sqrt{\frac{1.01 \times 10^5}{1.29}} \approx 280\text{ m s}^{-1}$$
  3. Calculate speed using Laplace's correction:$$v = \sqrt{\frac{1.4 \times 1.01 \times 10^5}{1.29}} \approx 331.3\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

Final Evaluation Output: Newton's Speed = $280\text{ m s}^{-1}$, Laplace's Speed = $331.3\text{ m s}^{-1}$

14.5 The Principle of Superposition of Waves

The principle of superposition states that when two or more wave disturbances overlap in a medium, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the displacements of the individual waves:

$$y(x, t) = y_1(x, t) + y_2(x, t)$$
Interference of Waves

When two sinusoidal waves of equal amplitude and frequency but differing in phase by $\phi$ overlap, the resultant wave is:

$$y(x, t) = \left[2a \cos\left(\frac{\phi}{2}\right)\right] \sin\left(kx - \omega t + \frac{\phi}{2} ight)$$
  • Constructive Interference: Occurs when $\phi = 0, 2\pi, 4\pi, \dots$, resulting in maximum amplitude $2a$.
  • Destructive Interference: Occurs when $\phi = \pi, 3\pi, 5\pi, \dots$, resulting in zero amplitude.
14.6 Reflection of Waves and Standing Waves

When a wave encounters a boundary, it undergoes reflection.

  • Rigid Boundary: The reflected wave suffers a phase change of $\pi$ (or $180^\circ$).
  • Open Boundary: The reflected wave undergoes no phase change.
Standing Waves

The superposition of two identical waves travelling in opposite directions produces a standing wave :

$$y(x, t) = [2a \sin(kx)] \cos(\omega t)$$
  • Nodes: Points of zero amplitude, occurring where $x = n\frac{\lambda}{2}$ ($n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$).
  • Antinodes: Points of maximum amplitude, occurring where $x = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\lambda}{2}$ ($n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$).
Normal Modes of a Stretched String

For a string of length $L$ fixed at both ends, the boundary conditions require nodes at both ends, leading to quantized wavelengths and frequencies:

$$\lambda = \frac{2L}{n}, \quad \nu = \frac{nv}{2L} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$
Normal Modes of Air Columns
  • Closed Pipe (One end closed, one open): Generates only odd harmonics:$$\nu = \left(n + \frac{1}{2} ight)\frac{v}{2L} \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots)$$
  • Open Pipe (Both ends open): Generates all harmonics:$$\nu = \frac{nv}{2L} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$
14.7 Beats

The phenomenon of beats occurs when two harmonic sound waves of nearly equal frequencies $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$ propagate in the same direction, causing a periodic waxing and waning of sound intensity.

Beat Frequency

The number of beats heard per second is equal to the difference between the two frequencies:

$$\nu_{\text{beat}} = |\nu_1 - \nu_2|$$
