Physics: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 1: ELECTRIC CHARGES AND FIELDS

1.1 Introduction & Historical Foundations

The study of electric charges at rest, their forces, fields, and potentials is known as electrostatics . Everyday phenomena such as hearing a crackle when taking off synthetic clothes, seeing lightning during a thunderstorm, or feeling a mild shock when opening a car door are all manifestations of static electricity discharge through the human body.

Historical Chronicle: Discovery of Charge

  • Thales of Miletus (600 BC): Discovered that amber rubbed with wool or silk cloth attracts light objects. The word "electricity" is derived from the Greek word elektron , meaning amber.
  • Benjamin Franklin: Introduced the convention of naming the two types of charges as positive and negative . By convention, the charge on a glass rod rubbed with silk is positive, while that on a plastic rod rubbed with cat's fur is negative.
Detection of Charge: The Gold-Leaf Electroscope

The gold-leaf electroscope is a simple apparatus used to detect the presence and estimate the magnitude of electric charge on a body. It consists of a vertical metal rod housed in a protective box, with two extremely thin gold leaves attached to its bottom end. When a charged object touches the metal knob at the top, charge flows down to the leaves, causing them to diverge due to mutual electrostatic repulsion. The degree of divergence serves as a qualitative indicator of the amount of charge.

1.2 Conductors, Insulators, and Grounding

Substances are classified based on their ability to allow the passage of electric charge through them:

  • Conductors: Materials that readily allow electricity to pass through them because they possess mobile charge carriers (free electrons). Examples include metals, the human body, animal bodies, and the earth.
  • Insulators: Materials that offer high resistance to the passage of electricity because their electrons are tightly bound to their parent atoms. Examples include glass, porcelain, plastic, nylon, and wood.
  • Semiconductors: A third category of materials whose electrical resistance lies intermediate between that of conductors and insulators.

When a charge is transferred to a conductor, it immediately distributes itself over the entire outer surface of the conductor. In contrast, if a charge is placed on an insulator, it remains localized at the point of contact.

The process of sharing excess electric charge with the earth is called grounding or earthing . It acts as a safety measure in electrical wiring to prevent shocks by diverting leakage currents safely into the ground.

1.3 Basic Properties of Electric Charge

Electric charge is a fundamental scalar property of matter. It exhibits three primary physical properties:

1. Additivity of Charges

If a system contains $n$ point charges $q_1, q_2, q_3, \dots, q_n$, the total charge of the system is obtained by their algebraic sum:

$$q_{\text{total}} = q_1 + q_2 + q_3 + \dots + q_n$$

Unlike mass, which is always positive, electric charges must be added with their proper positive or negative signs. For example, a system containing charges of +1 , +2 , -3 , +4 , and -5 units has a net charge of:

$$(+1) + (+2) + (-3) + (+4) + (-5) = -1 \text{ unit}$$
2. Conservation of Charge

Within an isolated system, charges can be transferred from one body to another, but the net charge of the system remains constant. Charges cannot be created or destroyed; they can only be redistributed. In high-energy physics, when a neutron decays into a proton and an electron, the net charge remains zero before and after the process, demonstrating the conservation of charge .

3. Quantisation of Charge

All free charges in nature exist as integral multiples of a basic, indivisible unit of charge denoted by $e$. This property is called the quantisation of charge :

$$q = n e$$

Where $n$ is any integer (positive or negative) and $e$ is the elementary charge carried by an electron or proton:

$$e \approx 1.602192 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$$

At the macroscopic level, where charges are of the order of microcoulombs ($\mu\text{C}$), the discrete, grainy nature of charge is imperceptible, and charge appears continuous. However, at the microscopic level, quantisation cannot be ignored.

Mathematical Problem Statement: If $10^9$ electrons move out of a body to another body every second, how much time is required to get a total charge of 1 C on the other body?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Calculate the charge transferred per second: $$\frac{dq}{dt} = 10^9 \times e = 10^9 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}) = 1.6 \times 10^{-10} \text{ C/s}$$
  2. Determine the total time $t$ required to accumulate 1 C : $$t = \frac{q}{dq/dt} = \frac{1 \text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-10} \text{ C/s}} = 6.25 \times 10^9 \text{ s}$$
  3. Convert the time from seconds to years: $$t = \frac{6.25 \times 10^9}{365 \times 24 \times 3600} \approx 198 \text{ years}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $t \approx \mathbf{200 \text{ years}}$

Mathematical Problem Statement: How much positive and negative charge is there in a cup of water (assuming the mass of one cup of water is 250 g )?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Find the molecular mass of water ($H_2O$): $$M = 18 \text{ g/mol}$$
  2. Calculate the number of molecules in 250 g of water using Avogadro's number ($N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}$): $$N = \frac{250}{18} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \approx 8.36 \times 10^{24} \text{ molecules}$$
  3. Determine the number of electrons/protons per molecule of $H_2O$: $$\text{Electrons} = 2(1) + 8 = 10$$
  4. Compute the total positive (or negative) charge: $$Q = N \times 10 \times e = (8.36 \times 10^{24}) \times 10 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}) \approx 1.34 \times 10^7 \text{ C}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $Q = \mathbf{1.34 \times 10^7 \text{ C}}$

1.4 Coulomb's Law

Coulomb's Law states that the electrostatic force of attraction or repulsion between two stationary point charges is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The force acts along the line joining the two charges.

$$F = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$$

Where $k$ is the electrostatic constant. In SI units, $k$ is expressed in terms of the permittivity of free space ($\epsilon_0$):

$$k = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \approx 8.987 \times 10^9 \text{ N m}^2\text{C}^{-2} \approx 9 \times 10^9 \text{ N m}^2\text{C}^{-2}$$ $$\epsilon_0 \approx 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \text{ C}^2\text{N}^{-1}\text{m}^{-2}$$
Vector Form of Coulomb's Law

Let $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$ be the position vectors of charges $q_1$ and $q_2$. The vector leading from $q_1$ to $q_2$ is $\vec{r}_{21} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1$, with magnitude $r_{21}$ and unit vector $\hat{r}_{21} = \vec{r}_{21}/r_{21}$. The force $\vec{F}_{21}$ exerted on $q_2$ by $q_1$ is:

$$\vec{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{21}^2} \hat{r}_{21}$$

Similarly, the force $\vec{F}_{12}$ exerted on $q_1$ by $q_2$ is:

$$\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}^2} \hat{r}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}$$

This confirms that Coulomb's Law is fully consistent with Newton's Third Law of Motion.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Compare the electrostatic force $F_e$ and gravitational force $F_g$ between an electron and a proton separated by distance $r$. ($m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}$, $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}$, $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \text{ N m}^2\text{kg}^{-2}$)

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Write the expressions for both forces: $$F_e = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{r^2}, \quad F_g = G \frac{m_p m_e}{r^2}$$
  2. Take the ratio of the magnitudes: $$\frac{F_e}{F_g} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 G m_p m_e}$$
  3. Substitute the physical constants: $$\frac{F_e}{F_g} = \frac{(9 \times 10^9) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{(6.67 \times 10^{-11}) \times (1.67 \times 10^{-27}) \times (9.11 \times 10^{-31})} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{39}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $\frac{F_e}{F_g} \approx \mathbf{2.4 \times 10^{39}}$ (Electrostatic forces are vastly stronger than gravitational forces).

1.5 Forces Between Multiple Charges: Superposition Principle

For a system of $n$ stationary charges $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n$, the net force on any single charge is calculated using the superposition principle . It states that the total force on a given charge due to a number of other charges is the vector sum of the individual forces exerted on it by each of the other charges, taken one at a time. The individual forces between any two charges remain unaffected by the presence of other charges.

$$\vec{F}_1 = \vec{F}_{12} + \vec{F}_{13} + \dots + \vec{F}_{1n}$$ $$\vec{F}_1 = \frac{q_1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \sum_{i=2}^{n} \frac{q_i}{r_{1i}^2} \hat{r}_{1i}$$
1.6 The Electric Field

An electric charge $Q$ modifies the surrounding space by creating an electric field $\vec{E}$. When a test charge $q$ is placed at a point in this field, it experiences an electrostatic force $\vec{F}$:

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \lim_{q \to 0} \frac{\vec{F}}{q}$$

The test charge $q$ is kept infinitesimally small so that its presence does not alter the spatial distribution of the source charge $Q$.

Electric Field of a Point Charge

The electric field $\vec{E}$ at a distance $r$ from a single point charge $Q$ is given by:

$$\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{r}$$

The field is directed radially outwards if $Q > 0$, and radially inwards if $Q < 0$. It exhibits spherical symmetry.

Electric Field of a System of Charges

By the superposition principle, the net electric field $\vec{E}$ at a point $\vec{r}$ due to a system of $n$ point charges is the vector sum of the fields produced by each charge individually:

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{q_i}{r_{iP}^2} \hat{r}_{iP}$$
1.7 Electric Field Lines

An electric field line is a spatial curve drawn such that the tangent to it at any point indicates the direction of the net electric field vector $\vec{E}$ at that point. The relative density of these lines in space is directly proportional to the local strength of the electric field.

Fundamental Properties of Electric Field Lines:

  1. Field lines originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges. For a single isolated charge, they start or end at infinity.
  2. In a charge-free region, electric field lines are continuous curves without any sudden breaks.
  3. Two field lines can never cross each other. If they did, the electric field at the point of intersection would point in two different directions simultaneously, which is physically impossible.
  4. Electrostatic field lines do not form closed loops. This is a direct consequence of the conservative nature of the electrostatic field.
1.8 Electric Flux

Electric flux ($\Delta\Phi$) is a mathematical measure of the number of electric field lines passing through a given surface area element $\Delta\vec{S}$:

$$\Delta\Phi = \vec{E} \cdot \Delta\vec{S} = E \Delta S \cos\theta$$

Where $\theta$ is the angle between the electric field vector $\vec{E}$ and the outward-pointing normal vector of the area element $\Delta\vec{S}$. The SI unit of electric flux is N m² C⁻¹ (or V m ).

1.9 The Electric Dipole

An electric dipole consists of a pair of equal and opposite point charges, $+q$ and $-q$, separated by a small distance $2a$. The dipole moment vector $\vec{p}$ is defined as:

$$\vec{p} = q \times (2a) \hat{p}$$

By convention, the vector $\vec{p}$ points along the dipole axis from the negative charge $-q$ to the positive charge $+q$.

Electric Field of a Dipole at Large Distances ($r \gg a$)
  • On the Axial Line: At a point on the axis of the dipole at a distance $r$ from its center: $$\vec{E}_{\text{axial}} \approx \frac{2\vec{p}}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}$$
  • On the Equatorial Plane: At a point on the equatorial plane of the dipole at a distance $r$ from its center: $$\vec{E}_{\text{equatorial}} \approx -\frac{\vec{p}}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}$$

At large distances, the dipole field falls off as $1/r^3$, which is faster than the $1/r^2$ dependence of a single point charge.

Dipole in a Uniform External Electric Field

When an electric dipole is placed in a uniform external electric field $\vec{E}$:

  1. The net translational force on the dipole is zero because the forces on $+q$ and $-q$ are equal and opposite ($q\vec{E}$ and $-q\vec{E}$): $$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = 0$$
  2. Since the forces act at different points, they produce a net torque $\vec{\tau}$: $$\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$$ $$\tau = p E \sin\theta$$

This torque acts to align the dipole moment vector $\vec{p}$ parallel to the external electric field $\vec{E}$. In a non-uniform electric field, the dipole experiences both a net torque and a net translational force.

1.10 Continuous Charge Distributions

When dealing with macroscopic systems containing a massive number of charges, it is practical to treat the charge distribution as continuous rather than discrete. We define three types of charge densities:

Distribution Type Density Symbol & Definition SI Unit Differential Charge Element ($dq$)
Linear (along a wire) $\lambda = \frac{dq}{dl}$ C m⁻¹ $dq = \lambda dl$
Surface (over a sheet) $\sigma = \frac{dq}{dS}$ C m⁻² $dq = \sigma dS$
Volume (inside a sphere) $\rho = \frac{dq}{dV}$ C m⁻³ $dq = \rho dV$
1.11 Gauss's Law

Gauss's Law states that the total electric flux $\Phi$ through any closed surface $S$ (referred to as a Gaussian surface ) is equal to $1/\epsilon_0$ \times the total net charge $q_{\text{enclosed}}$ enclosed within that surface:

$$\Phi = \oint_{S} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{q_{\text{enclosed}}}{\epsilon_0}$$

Key Insights Regarding Gauss's Law:

  • The law is valid for any closed surface, regardless of its shape or size.
  • The term $q$ on the right-hand side includes only the charges enclosed inside the Gaussian surface.
  • The electric field $\vec{E}$ on the left-hand side is the vector sum of fields produced by all charges, both inside and outside the surface.
  • It is highly useful for calculating the electric field of highly symmetric charge distributions.

Mathematical Problem Statement: An electric field is uniform and given by $\vec{E} = 200 \hat{i} \text{ N/C}$ for $x > 0$ and $\vec{E} = -200 \hat{i} \text{ N/C}$ for $x < 0$. A cylinder of length 20 cm and radius 5 cm has its center at the origin and its axis along the x-axis. Find the net outward flux through the cylinder and the net charge inside.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Calculate the cross-sectional area of the flat faces: $$A = \pi r^2 = \pi \times (0.05 \text{ m})^2 \approx 7.85 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}^2$$
  2. Determine the flux through the left flat face ($x = -10 \text{ cm}$): $$\Phi_L = \vec{E}_L \cdot \vec{A}_L = (-200 \hat{i}) \cdot (-A \hat{i}) = 200 \times 7.85 \times 10^{-3} \approx +1.57 \text{ N m}^2\text{C}^{-1}$$
  3. Determine the flux through the right flat face ($x = +10 \text{ cm}$): $$\Phi_R = \vec{E}_R \cdot \vec{A}_R = (200 \hat{i}) \cdot (A \hat{i}) = 200 \times 7.85 \times 10^{-3} \approx +1.57 \text{ N m}^2\text{C}^{-1}$$
  4. The flux through the curved side of the cylinder is zero because $\vec{E}$ is perpendicular to the area vector at all points: $$\Phi_{\text{curved}} = 0$$
  5. Calculate the total net outward flux: $$\Phi_{\text{total}} = \Phi_L + \Phi_R + \Phi_{\text{curved}} = 1.57 + 1.57 + 0 = 3.14 \text{ N m}^2\text{C}^{-1}$$
  6. Use Gauss's Law to find the enclosed charge: $$q = \epsilon_0 \Phi_{\text{total}} = (8.854 \times 10^{-12} \text{ C}^2\text{N}^{-1}\text{m}^{-2}) \times 3.14 \text{ N m}^2\text{C}^{-1} \approx 2.78 \times 10^{-11} \text{ C}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $\Phi_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{3.14 \text{ N m}^2\text{C}^{-1}}$, $q = \mathbf{2.78 \times 10^{-11} \text{ C}}$

1.12 Applications of Gauss's Law

Gauss's Law simplifies the derivation of electric fields for symmetric charge distributions:

1. Field Due to an Infinitely Long Straight Uniformly Charged Wire

For a thin, infinitely long straight wire with uniform linear charge density $\lambda$, we choose a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ and length $l$ coaxial with the wire. The electric field is radial and its magnitude is:

$$E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$

Vectorially, $\vec{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r} \hat{n}$, where $\hat{n}$ is the radial unit vector in the plane normal to the wire.

2. Field Due to a Uniformly Charged Infinite Plane Sheet

For an infinite plane sheet with uniform surface charge density $\sigma$, we choose a cylindrical Gaussian surface of cross-sectional area $A$ perpendicular to the sheet. The electric field is uniform, directed normal to the sheet, and independent of the distance from the sheet:

$$E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$$

Vectorially, $\vec{E} = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} \hat{n}$, where $\hat{n}$ is the unit vector normal to the plane pointing away from it.

3. Field Due to a Uniformly Charged Thin Spherical Shell

For a thin spherical shell of radius $R$ carrying a total charge $q$ with uniform surface charge density $\sigma$:

  • Outside the Shell ($r \ge R$): The electric field at any external point is calculated by choosing a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$: $$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}$$

    This is identical to the field of a point charge $q$ concentrated at the center of the shell.

  • Inside the Shell ($r < R$): A spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r < R$ encloses no charge ($q_{\text{enclosed}} = 0$): $$E = 0$$
