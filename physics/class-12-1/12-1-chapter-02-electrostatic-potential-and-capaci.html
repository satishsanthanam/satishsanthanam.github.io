Physics: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 2: Electrostatic Potential and Capacitance

2.1 Introduction & Electrostatic Potential Energy

When an external force does work in taking a body from one point to another against a conservative force (like gravitational force or spring force), that work gets stored as the potential energy of the body. The Coulomb force between two stationary charges is also a conservative force , allowing us to define electrostatic potential energy .

Mathematical Formulation of Potential Energy Difference

The work done $W_{RP}$ by an external force $\mathbf{F}_{ext}$ in bringing a charge $q$ from point $R$ to point $P$ against the repulsive electric force $\mathbf{F}_E$ (where $\mathbf{F}_{ext} = -\mathbf{F}_E$) is given by:

$$W_{RP} = \int_{R}^{P} \mathbf{F}_{ext} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = -\int_{R}^{P} \mathbf{F}_E \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$

This work done increases the potential energy of the charge by:

$$\Delta U = U_P - U_R = W_{RP}$$
Key Characteristics of Electrostatic Potential Energy
  • The work done by an electrostatic field depends only on the initial and final positions of the charge, and is completely path-independent .
  • The actual value of potential energy is not physically significant; only the difference $\Delta U$ is physically meaningful.
  • By convention, the potential energy at infinity is chosen to be zero ($U_{\infty} = 0$). Thus, the potential energy of a charge $q$ at any point $P$ is the work done by an external force in bringing it from infinity to that point: $W_{\infty P} = U_P$.
2.2 Electrostatic Potential

To obtain a quantity independent of the test charge $q$, we define the electrostatic potential ($V$) as the potential energy per unit charge.

Definition and Equation

The potential difference between two points $P$ and $R$ is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing it from $R$ to $P$:

$$V_P - V_R = \frac{U_P - U_R}{q} = \frac{W_{RP}}{q}$$

Taking the potential at infinity to be zero ($V_{\infty} = 0$), the electrostatic potential at any point $P$ is defined as:

The work done by an external force in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point without acceleration.

Historical Chronicle: Count Alessandro Volta (1745 – 1827)

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Italian physicist and professor at Pavia. Volta established that the "animal electricity" observed by Luigi Galvani in frog muscles was actually generated by contact with dissimilar metals.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Developed the first voltaic pile (battery), consisting of a stack of moist cardboard disks (electrolyte) sandwiched between metal disks (electrodes).
2.3 Potential due to a Point Charge

Consider a point charge $Q$ placed at the origin. We wish to determine the potential at any point $P$ at a distance $r$ from the origin.

Derivation of the Potential Formula
  1. The electrostatic force on a unit positive charge at an intermediate point $P'$ at distance $r'$ is: $$F = \frac{Q \times 1}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r'^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}'$$
  2. The work done against this force for a small displacement $dr'$ is: $$dW = -\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r'^2} dr'$$
  3. Integrating from infinity ($r' = \infty$) to the point $P$ ($r' = r$): $$W = -\int_{\infty}^{r} \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r'^2} dr' = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$
  4. By definition, this work done is the potential $V(r)$ at distance $r$: $$V(r) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: (a) Calculate the potential at a point P due to a charge of 4 &× 10^{-7} C located 9 cm away. (b) Hence obtain the work done in bringing a charge of 2 &× 10^{-9} C from infinity to the point P. Does the answer depend on the path?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify given values: $Q = 4 \times 10^{-7}$ C , $r = 9$ cm $= 0.09$ m , $q = 2 \times 10^{-9}$ C .
  2. Calculate potential $V$ at P using $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r}$:
    $V = 9 \times 10^9 \times \frac{4 \times 10^{-7}}{0.09} = 4 \times 10^4$ V .
  3. Calculate work done $W = qV$:
    $W = 2 \times 10^{-9} \times 4 \times 10^4 = 8 \times 10^{-5}$ J .
  4. Path independence: The work done is independent of the path because the electrostatic force is a conservative force.

Final Evaluation Output: Potential $V = 4 \times 10^4$ V , Work Done $W = 8 \times 10^{-5}$ J (Path Independent)

2.4 Potential due to an Electric Dipole

An electric dipole consists of two charges $q$ and $-q$ separated by a small distance $2a$. The dipole moment is $\mathbf{p}$ with magnitude $p = q \times 2a$.

Derivation of Dipole Potential

The potential $V$ at a point $P$ at distance $r$ from the center of the dipole is the sum of potentials due to $+q$ and $-q$:

$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left( \frac{q}{r_1} - \frac{q}{r_2} \right)$$

Using geometry and the approximation $r \gg a$:

$$r_1^2 = r^2 + a^2 - 2ar\cos\theta \implies \frac{1}{r_1} \approx \frac{1}{r} \left( 1 + \frac{a\cos\theta}{r} \right)$$$$r_2^2 = r^2 + a^2 + 2ar\cos\theta \implies \frac{1}{r_2} \approx \frac{1}{r} \left( 1 - \frac{a\cos\theta}{r} \right)$$$$V = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2a\cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} = \frac{\mathbf{p} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}$$
Special Cases
  • On the dipole axis ($\theta = 0$ or $\theta = \pi$): $$V = \pm \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}$$
  • On the equatorial plane ($\theta = \pi/2$): $$V = 0$$
2.5 Potential due to a System of Charges

By the superposition principle , the potential $V$ at a point $P$ due to a system of charges $q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n$ is the algebraic sum of potentials due to individual charges:

$$V = V_1 + V_2 + \dots + V_n = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{q_i}{r_{iP}}$$
Potential of a Uniformly Charged Spherical Shell
  • Outside the shell ($r \ge R$): The potential is as if the entire charge $q$ is concentrated at the center: $$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r}$$
  • Inside the shell ($r < R$): The electric field inside is zero, meaning the potential is constant and equal to its value at the surface: $$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{R}$$
2.6 Equipotential Surfaces

An equipotential surface is a surface with a constant value of potential at all points on the surface.

Key Properties
  • No work is done in moving a test charge between any two points on an equipotential surface.
  • The electric field $\mathbf{E}$ at any point is always normal to the equipotential surface passing through that point.
  • For a uniform electric field along the $x$-axis, the equipotential surfaces are planes parallel to the $y$-$z$ plane.
Relation between Field and Potential

The relation between the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ and potential gradient is given by:

$$|\mathbf{E}| = -\frac{\delta V}{\delta l} \quad \text{or} \quad E = \frac{|\delta V|}{\delta l}$$

This \implies:

  1. The electric field is in the direction in which the potential decreases steepest.
  2. Its magnitude is given by the change in potential per unit displacement normal to the equipotential surface.
2.7 Potential Energy of a System of Charges
System of Two Charges (No External Field)

The potential energy $U$ of a system of two charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by distance $r_{12}$ is the work done in assembling them from infinity:

$$U = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$$
System of Three Charges

For three charges $q_1, q_2, q_3$ located at $\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \mathbf{r}_3$:

$$U = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left( \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1 q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2 q_3}{r_{23}} \right)$$
Potential Energy in an External Field
  • Single charge $q$ in external potential $V(\mathbf{r})$: $$U = qV(\mathbf{r})$$
  • Two charges $q_1, q_2$ in external potential $V(\mathbf{r})$: $$U = q_1 V(\mathbf{r}_1) + q_2 V(\mathbf{r}_2) + \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$$
  • Dipole in a uniform external field $\mathbf{E}$: The potential energy $U(\theta)$ of a dipole with moment $\mathbf{p}$ at angle $\theta$ is: $$U(\theta) = -\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{E} = -pE \cos\theta$$
2.8 Electrostatics of Conductors

Conductors contain mobile charge carriers (free electrons) that redistribute themselves in the presence of an electric field to establish electrostatic equilibrium.

6 Key Electrostatic Properties of Conductors
  1. Inside a conductor, the electrostatic field is zero.
  2. At the surface of a charged conductor, the electrostatic field must be normal to the surface at every point.
  3. The interior of a conductor can have no excess charge in the static situation. (All excess charge resides on the outer surface).
  4. Electrostatic potential is constant throughout the volume of the conductor and has the same value on its surface.
  5. Electric field at the surface of a charged conductor is: $$\mathbf{E} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} \hat{\mathbf{n}}$$ where $\sigma$ is the local surface charge density.
  6. Electrostatic shielding: The electric field inside a cavity of any conductor is always zero, shielding the interior from external electrical influences.
2.9 Dielectrics and Polarisation

Dielectrics are non-conducting substances that do not have free charge carriers but develop a net dipole moment in an external electric field.

Polar vs Non-Polar Molecules
  • Non-polar molecules: The centers of positive and negative charges coincide, resulting in zero intrinsic dipole moment (e.g., $O_2, H_2$).
  • Polar molecules: The centers of positive and negative charges are separated, giving them a permanent dipole moment (e.g., $HCl, H_2O$).
Polarisation Vector

The dipole moment per unit volume is called polarisation ($\mathbf{P}$). For linear isotropic dielectrics:

$$\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \mathbf{E}$$

where $\chi_e$ is the electric susceptibility of the dielectric medium.

2.10 Capacitors and Capacitance

A capacitor is a system of two conductors separated by an insulator. It is used to store electrical charge and energy.

Capacitance Definition

The charge $Q$ on a capacitor is directly proportional to the potential difference $V$ between the conductors:

$$Q = C V \implies C = \frac{Q}{V}$$

The constant $C$ is the capacitance . Its SI unit is the Farad (F) ($1\text{ F} = 1\text{ C V}^{-1}$).

The Parallel Plate Capacitor

For two parallel plates of area $A$ separated by a small distance $d$ in vacuum:

$$C_0 = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}$$
Effect of Dielectric on Capacitance

When the space between the plates is completely filled with a dielectric of dielectric constant $K$:

$$C = K C_0 = \frac{K \epsilon_0 A}{d} = \frac{\epsilon A}{d}$$

where $\epsilon = K \epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of the medium.

2.11 Combination of Capacitors & Energy Stored
Capacitors in Series

For $n$ capacitors connected in series, the charge $Q$ is the same on each, and the total potential drop is the sum of individual drops:

$$\frac{1}{C_{eq}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{C_n}$$
Capacitors in Parallel

For $n$ capacitors connected in parallel, the potential difference $V$ is the same across each, and the total charge is the sum of individual charges:

$$C_{eq} = C_1 + C_2 + \dots + C_n$$
Energy Stored in a Capacitor

The work done in charging a capacitor is stored as electrostatic potential energy $U$:

$$U = \frac{1}{2} Q V = \frac{1}{2} C V^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C}$$
Energy Density

The energy stored per unit volume in the electric field between the plates is the energy density ($u$):

$$u = \frac{1}{2} \epsilon_0 E^2$$
