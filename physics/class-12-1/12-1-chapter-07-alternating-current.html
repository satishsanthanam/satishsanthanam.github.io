Physics: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 7: Alternating Current

7.1 Introduction to Alternating Current

Most of the electrical energy generated and distributed today is in the form of alternating current (ac). Unlike direct current (dc) which flows continuously in one direction, alternating current periodically reverses its direction and varies continuously in magnitude with time.

Advantages of AC over DC
  • Efficient Voltage Transformation: Alternating voltages can be easily and efficiently stepped up or stepped down using transformers .
  • Economical Transmission: By stepping up the voltage, electrical energy can be transmitted over long distances at high voltage and low current, significantly reducing $I^2 R$ power losses in transmission lines.
  • Widespread Device Compatibility: Most modern industrial and household appliances are designed to operate directly on ac voltage.

Historical Chronicle: Nicola Tesla (1856 – 1943)

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Serbian-American scientist, inventor, and genius who conceived the rotating magnetic field, which is the foundation of modern alternating current machinery.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Developed the polyphase system of ac power, the induction motor, and the high-frequency induction coil (Tesla coil). The SI unit of magnetic field is named the Tesla ($T$) in his honour.

Historical Chronicle: George Westinghouse (1846 – 1914)

  • Historical Context & Milestones: A pioneer and leading proponent of alternating current technology over direct current, putting him in direct conflict with Thomas Edison.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Founded the Westinghouse Electric Company, championing the commercialization of ac power systems and hiring Nicola Tesla to develop ac motors and transmission systems.
7.2 AC Voltage Applied to a Resistor

Consider a pure resistor of resistance $R$ connected to an ac source that produces a sinusoidally varying potential difference across its terminals.

Mathematical Derivation of Current

The instantaneous ac voltage is given by:

$$v = v_m \sin \omega t$$

where $v_m$ is the amplitude of the oscillating potential difference and $\omega$ is its angular frequency.

Applying Kirchhoff's loop rule ($\sum \epsilon(t) = 0$) to the circuit:

$$v_m \sin \omega t = i R$$

$$i = \frac{v_m}{R} \sin \omega t$$

Since $R$ is constant, we can write:

$$i = i_m \sin \omega t$$

where the current amplitude $i_m$ is given by Ohm's law :

$$i_m = \frac{v_m}{R}$$

Phase Relationship and Power Dissipation
  • Phase: Comparing the equations for $v$ and $i$, we see that both reach their zero, minimum, and maximum values at the same time. Therefore, the voltage and current in a pure resistor are in phase with each other.
  • Average Current: The average value of current over one complete cycle is zero because the positive and negative half-cycles cancel out.
  • Joule Heating: Although the average current is zero, the average power dissipated is not zero because heating depends on $i^2$, which is always positive.
  • Instantaneous Power: $$p = i^2 R = i_m^2 R \sin^2 \omega t$$
  • Average Power: Since the average value of $\sin^2 \omega t$ over a complete cycle is $\frac{1}{2}$, the average power is:
  • $$P = \bar{p} = \frac{1}{2} i_m^2 R$$
Root Mean Square (RMS) Values

To express ac power in the same mathematical form as dc power ($P = I^2 R$), we define the root mean square current ($I$ or $I_{rms}$):

$$I = \sqrt{\bar{i^2}} = \frac{i_m}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 i_m$$

Similarly, the rms voltage ($V$ or $V_{rms}$) is defined as:

$$V = \frac{v_m}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 v_m$$

Using these rms values, the average power can be written as:

$$P = I^2 R = V I = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: A light bulb is rated at 100 W for a 220 V supply. Find (a) the resistance of the bulb; (b) the peak voltage of the source; and (c) the rms current through the bulb.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify given values: $P = 100\text{ W}$, $V = 220\text{ V}$ (rms voltage).
  2. Calculate resistance $R$ using $R = \frac{V^2}{P}$:$$R = \frac{(220)^2}{100} = \frac{48400}{100} = 484\\ \Omega$$
  3. Calculate peak voltage $v_m$ using $v_m = \sqrt{2} V$:$$v_m = 1.414 \times 220 = 311\text{ V}$$
  4. Calculate rms current $I$ using $I = \frac{P}{V}$:$$I = \frac{100}{220} = 0.454\text{ A}$$

Final Evaluation Output: Resistance $R = 484\\ \Omega$, Peak Voltage $v_m = 311\text{ V}$, RMS Current $I = 0.454\text{ A}$

7.3 Representation of AC Current and Voltage by Rotating Vectors — Phasors

To analyze phase relationships between voltage and current in ac circuits containing inductors, capacitors, or their combinations, we use the concept of phasors .

  • A phasor is a vector that rotates about the origin in a counter-clockwise direction with an angular speed $\omega$.
  • The length of the phasor represents the amplitude (peak value) of the quantity ($v_m$ or $i_m$).
  • The projection of the phasor on the vertical axis represents the instantaneous value of the quantity ($v_m \sin \omega t$ or $i_m \sin \omega t$).
  • The angle between the phasor and the horizontal axis represents its phase at that instant.
7.4 AC Voltage Applied to an Inductor

Consider a pure inductor of self-inductance $L$ and negligible resistance connected to an ac source.

Mathematical Derivation of Current

Let the applied voltage be $v = v_m \sin \omega t$. Applying Kirchhoff's loop rule:

$$v - L \frac{di}{dt} = 0 \implies \frac{di}{dt} = \frac{v_m}{L} \sin \omega t$$

Integrating both sides with respect to time $t$:

$$\int \frac{di}{dt} dt = \frac{v_m}{L} \int \sin(\omega t) dt$$

$$i = -\frac{v_m}{\omega L} \cos(\omega t) + \text{constant}$$

Since the source voltage oscillates symmetrically about zero, the current must also oscillate symmetrically about zero, meaning the integration constant is zero.

Using the trigonometric identity $-\cos(\omega t) = \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$:

$$i = i_m \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$

where the current amplitude $i_m$ is:

$$i_m = \frac{v_m}{\omega L}$$

Inductive Reactance and Phase Relationship
  • Inductive Reactance ($X_L$): The quantity $\omega L$ acts as the effective resistance of the inductor and is called inductive reactance :$$X_L = \omega L = 2\pi \nu L$$Its SI unit is the ohm ($\Omega$). It is directly proportional to both the inductance and the frequency of the ac source.
  • Phase Relationship: Comparing $v = v_m \sin \omega t$ and $i = i_m \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, we see that the current lags the voltage by $\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians (or $90^\circ$, which is one-quarter of a period).
Power Dissipation in an Inductor

The instantaneous power supplied to the inductor is:

$$p_L = i v = \left[i_m \sin\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right] \times [v_m \sin \omega t] = -i_m v_m \cos(\omega t) \sin(\omega t) = -\frac{i_m v_m}{2} \sin(2\omega t)$$

The average power over a complete cycle is:

$$P_L = -\frac{i_m v_m}{2} \langle\sin(2\omega t)\rangle = 0$$

Thus, the average power supplied to an inductor over a complete cycle is zero . No electrical energy is dissipated as heat in a pure inductor.

Mathematical Problem Statement: A pure inductor of 25.0 mH is connected to a source of 220 V . Find the inductive reactance and rms current in the circuit if the frequency of the source is 50 Hz .

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify given values: $L = 25.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ H}$, $V = 220\text{ V}$, $\nu = 50\text{ Hz}$.
  2. Calculate inductive reactance $X_L = 2\pi \nu L$:$$X_L = 2 \times 3.14 \times 50 \times 25.0 \times 10^{-3} = 7.85\\ \Omega$$
  3. Calculate rms current $I = \frac{V}{X_L}$:$$I = \frac{220}{7.85} \approx 28\text{ A}$$

Final Evaluation Output: Inductive Reactance $X_L = 7.85\\ \Omega$, RMS Current $I = 28\text{ A}$

7.5 AC Voltage Applied to a Capacitor

Consider a capacitor of capacitance $C$ connected to an ac source.

Mathematical Derivation of Current

Let the applied voltage be $v = v_m \sin \omega t$. The instantaneous charge $q$ on the capacitor is:

$$q = C v = C v_m \sin \omega t$$

The current $i$ in the circuit is the rate of flow of charge:

$$i = \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(C v_m \sin \omega t) = \omega C v_m \cos(\omega t)$$

Using the trigonometric identity $\cos(\omega t) = \sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$:

$$i = i_m \sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$

where the current amplitude $i_m$ is:

$$i_m = \omega C v_m = \frac{v_m}{1/(\omega C)}$$

Capacitive Reactance and Phase Relationship
  • Capacitive Reactance ($X_C$): The quantity $\frac{1}{\omega C}$ acts as the effective resistance of the capacitor and is called capacitive reactance :$$X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{2\pi \nu C}$$Its SI unit is the ohm ($\Omega$). It is inversely proportional to both the frequency and the capacitance.
  • Phase Relationship: Comparing $v = v_m \sin \omega t$ and $i = i_m \sin\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, we see that the current leads the voltage by $\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians (or $90^\circ$, which is one-quarter of a period).
Power Dissipation in a Capacitor

The instantaneous power supplied to the capacitor is:

$$p_C = i v = \left[i_m \cos(\omega t)\right] \times [v_m \sin \omega t] = \frac{i_m v_m}{2} \sin(2\omega t)$$

The average power over a complete cycle is:

$$P_C = \frac{i_m v_m}{2} \langle\sin(2\omega t)\rangle = 0$$

Thus, the average power supplied to a capacitor over a complete cycle is zero .

Mathematical Problem Statement: A 15.0 \mu F capacitor is connected to a 220 V , 50 Hz source. Find the capacitive reactance and the current (rms and peak) in the circuit. If the frequency is doubled, what happens to the capacitive reactance and the current?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify given values: $C = 15.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ F}$, $V = 220\text{ V}$, $\nu = 50\text{ Hz}$.
  2. Calculate capacitive reactance $X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi \nu C}$:$$X_C = \frac{1}{2 \times 3.14 \times 50 \times 15.0 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{1}{0.00471} \approx 212\\ \Omega$$
  3. Calculate rms current $I = \frac{V}{X_C}$:$$I = \frac{220}{212} = 1.04\text{ A}$$
  4. Calculate peak current $i_m = \sqrt{2} I$:$$i_m = 1.414 \times 1.04 = 1.47\text{ A}$$
  5. If frequency is doubled, $X_C$ is halved ($106\\ \Omega$) and the current is doubled ($I = 2.08\text{ A}$, $i_m = 2.94\text{ A}$).

Final Evaluation Output: $X_C = 212\\ \Omega$, $I = 1.04\text{ A}$, $i_m = 1.47\text{ A}$. Doubling frequency halves $X_C$ and doubles current.

7.6 AC Voltage Applied to a Series LCR Circuit

Consider a circuit containing a resistor $R$, an inductor $L$, and a capacitor $C$ connected in series across an ac source of voltage $v = v_m \sin \omega t$.

Phasor-Diagram Solution

Since the elements are in series, the instantaneous current $i$ is the same at any point in the circuit. Let it be:

$$i = i_m \sin(\omega t + \phi)$$

where $\phi$ is the phase difference between the voltage of the source and the current in the circuit.

Let $\mathbf{V}_R$, $\mathbf{V}_L$, $\mathbf{V}_C$, and $\mathbf{V}$ be the phasors representing the voltages across the resistor, inductor, capacitor, and source respectively. Their amplitudes are:

  • $v_{Rm} = i_m R$ (in phase with current phasor $\mathbf{I}$)
  • $v_{Lm} = i_m X_L$ (leads $\mathbf{I}$ by $\frac{\pi}{2}$)
  • $v_{Cm} = i_m X_C$ (lags $\mathbf{I}$ by $\frac{\pi}{2}$)

The phasor relation is:

$$\mathbf{V}_L + \mathbf{V}_R + \mathbf{V}_C = \mathbf{V}$$

Since $\mathbf{V}_C$ and $\mathbf{V}_L$ are along the same line but in opposite directions, they combine to form a single phasor of magnitude $|v_{Cm} - v_{Lm}|$. Using the Pythagorean theorem on the right-triangle formed by $\mathbf{V}_R$, $(\mathbf{V}_C + \mathbf{V}_L)$, and $\mathbf{V}$:

$$v_m^2 = v_{Rm}^2 + (v_{Cm} - v_{Lm})^2$$

$$v_m^2 = (i_m R)^2 + (i_m X_C - i_m X_L)^2 = i_m^2 [R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2]$$

$$i_m = \frac{v_m}{\sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2}} = \frac{v_m}{Z}$$

where $Z$ is the impedance of the circuit:

$$Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_C - X_L)^2}$$

The phase angle $\phi$ is given by:

$$\tan \phi = \frac{v_{Cm} - v_{Lm}}{v_{Rm}} = \frac{X_C - X_L}{R}$$

Resonance in Series LCR Circuits

An interesting characteristic of a series $RLC$ circuit is the phenomenon of resonance , where the current amplitude is maximized at a specific frequency.

  • Resonance Condition: The current amplitude $i_m = \frac{v_m}{Z}$ is maximum when the impedance $Z$ is minimum. This occurs when:$$X_C = X_L \implies \frac{1}{\omega_0 C} = \omega_0 L$$
  • Resonant Frequency ($\omega_0$): $$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$
  • Impedance at Resonance: At $\omega_0$, $Z = R$, which is purely resistive. The current amplitude is at its maximum value:$$i_{m,\text{max}} = \frac{v_m}{R}$$
  • Requirement for Resonance: Resonance can only occur if both $L$ and $C$ are present in the circuit. In $RL$ or $RC$ circuits, the voltages across the reactive elements cannot cancel each other out, so resonance is impossible.

Mathematical Problem Statement: A resistor of $200\\ \Omega$ and a capacitor of $15.0\\ \mu\text{F}$ are connected in series to a $220\text{ V}$, $50\text{ Hz}$ ac source. (a) Calculate the current in the circuit; (b) Calculate the voltage (rms) across the resistor and the capacitor. Is the algebraic sum of these voltages more than the source voltage? If yes, resolve the paradox.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify given values: $R = 200\\ \Omega$, $C = 15.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ F}$, $V = 220\text{ V}$, $\nu = 50\text{ Hz}$.
  2. Calculate capacitive reactance $X_C$:$$X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi \nu C} = \frac{1}{2 \times 3.14 \times 50 \times 15.0 \times 10^{-6}} \approx 212.3\\ \Omega$$
  3. Calculate impedance $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + X_C^2}$:$$Z = \sqrt{200^2 + 212.3^2} = \sqrt{40000 + 45071} = \sqrt{85071} \approx 291.7\\ \Omega$$
  4. Calculate current $I = \frac{V}{Z}$:$$I = \frac{220}{291.7} \approx 0.755\text{ A}$$
  5. Calculate rms voltage across resistor $V_R = I R$:$$V_R = 0.755 \times 200 = 151\text{ V}$$
  6. Calculate rms voltage across capacitor $V_C = I X_C$:$$V_C = 0.755 \times 212.3 \approx 160.3\text{ V}$$
  7. The algebraic sum of $V_R$ and $V_C$ is $151 + 160.3 = 311.3\text{ V}$, which is greater than the source voltage of $220\text{ V}$. This is because the two voltages are not in phase (they are $90^\circ$ out of phase). The total voltage must be calculated using the Pythagorean theorem:$$V = \sqrt{V_R^2 + V_C^2} = \sqrt{151^2 + 160.3^2} \approx 220\text{ V}$$

Final Evaluation Output: Current $I = 0.755\text{ A}$, $V_R = 151\text{ V}$, $V_C = 160.3\text{ V}$. The paradox is resolved by vector addition of out-of-phase voltages.

7.7 Power in AC Circuit: The Power Factor

The average power $P$ over a complete cycle is:

$$P = V I \cos \phi$$

where $\cos \phi$ is called the power factor of the circuit.

Analysis of Power Factor in Different Circuits
Circuit Type Phase Angle ($\phi$) Power Factor ($\cos \phi$) Average Power ($P$)
Purely Resistive $\phi = 0$ $\cos \phi = 1$ $P = V I$ (Maximum power dissipation)
Purely Inductive / Capacitive $\phi = \pm \frac{\pi}{2}$ $\cos \phi = 0$ $P = 0$ (Zero power dissipation, wattless current )
Series LCR Circuit $\tan \phi = \frac{X_C - X_L}{R}$ $\cos \phi = \frac{R}{Z}$ $P = V I \cos \phi$ (Power dissipated only in resistor)
LCR Circuit at Resonance $\phi = 0$ $\cos \phi = 1$ $P = I^2 R$ (Maximum power dissipation)
7.8 Transformers

A transformer is a static electrical device used to increase or decrease alternating voltage based on the principle of mutual induction .

Construction and Working Principle
  • Coils: It consists of two coils wound on a laminated soft-iron core:
    • Primary Coil: Connected to the input ac voltage source, having $N_p$ turns.
    • Secondary Coil: Connected to the output load, having $N_s$ turns.
  • Working: An alternating current in the primary coil creates a continuously changing magnetic flux in the iron core, which passes through the secondary coil and induces an alternating electromotive force (emf) in it.
Mathematical Equations

Assuming an ideal transformer with zero flux leakage and negligible coil resistance, the induced emfs are:

$$\epsilon_s = -N_s \frac{d\phi}{dt}$$

$$\epsilon_p = -N_p \frac{d\phi}{dt}$$

Since $\epsilon_s = v_s$ and $\epsilon_p = v_p$ (where $v_s$ and $v_p$ are terminal voltages):

$$\frac{v_s}{v_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}$$

For an ideal 100% efficient transformer, input power equals output power ($i_p v_p = i_s v_s$):

$$\frac{i_p}{i_s} = \frac{v_s}{v_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}$$

Types of Transformers
  • Step-up Transformer: Increases voltage ($V_s > V_p$) by having more turns in the secondary coil ($N_s > N_p$). Consequently, the secondary current is lower than the primary current ($I_s < I_p$).
  • Step-down Transformer: Decreases voltage ($V_s < V_p$) by having fewer turns in the secondary coil ($N_s < N_p$). Consequently, the secondary current is higher than the primary current ($I_s > I_p$).
Energy Losses in Real Transformers
  1. Flux Leakage: Not all magnetic flux from the primary coil passes through the secondary coil. Minimized by winding the primary and secondary coils over one another.
  2. Copper Loss (Resistance of Windings): Heat produced ($I^2 R$) due to the resistance of the copper wire. Minimized by using thick wires for high-current windings.
  3. Iron Loss (Eddy Currents): Alternating magnetic flux induces circulating currents (eddy currents) in the iron core, causing heating. Minimized by using a laminated iron core.
  4. Hysteresis Loss: Energy lost as heat during the continuous magnetization and demagnetization of the core. Minimized by using a core material with low hysteresis loss, such as soft iron.
