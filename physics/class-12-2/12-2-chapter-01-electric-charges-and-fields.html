9.1 Introduction to Ray Optics

Light is electromagnetic radiation belonging to the visible region of the spectrum, with wavelengths ranging from approximately 400 nm to 750 nm . It is the primary medium through which we perceive and interpret the physical world.

Core Properties of Light
  • Rectilinear Propagation : Light travels in a straight line in a homogeneous medium. This is an approximation valid when the wavelength of light is extremely small compared to the dimensions of ordinary obstacles or apertures.
  • Speed of Light : In a vacuum, light travels at its maximum speed, denoted by $c = 2.99792458 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$, which is commonly approximated as $3 \times 10^8 \text{ m s}^{-1}$ for practical calculations.
  • Ray and Beam : The straight-line path of light is called a ray of light , and a bundle of such rays constitutes a beam of light .
9.2 Reflection of Light by Spherical Mirrors

Reflection is the phenomenon where light rays strike a boundary separating two different media and return to the medium from which they originated, obeying specific geometric laws.

Laws of Reflection
  1. The angle of incidence ($i$) equals the angle of reflection ($r$).
  2. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.

These laws are universally valid at every point on any reflecting surface, whether plane or curved.

Cartesian Sign Convention

To derive consistent formulas for spherical mirrors and lenses, the Cartesian sign convention is adopted:

  • All distances are measured from the pole ($P$) of the mirror or the optical centre of the lens.
  • Distances measured in the direction of the incident light are taken as positive ($+$).
  • Distances measured in the direction opposite to the incident light are taken as negative ($-$).
  • Heights measured upwards, perpendicular to the principal axis, are positive ($+$).
  • Heights measured downwards, perpendicular to the principal axis, are negative ($-$).
Focal Length of Spherical Mirrors

For paraxial rays (rays incident close to the pole making small angles with the principal axis), the relation between the focal length ($f$) and the radius of curvature ($R$) of a spherical mirror is given by:

$$f = \frac{R}{2}$$

Derivation Trace: Let a ray parallel to the principal axis strike a concave mirror at $M$. The normal $CM$ passes through the centre of curvature $C$. The angle of incidence is $\theta$, and the angle of reflection is also $\theta$. The reflected ray intersects the principal axis at the focus $F$. From geometry, $\angle MCP = \theta$ and $\angle MFP = 2\theta$. For small angles, $\tan \theta \approx \theta = \frac{MD}{CD}$ and $\tan 2\theta \approx 2\theta = \frac{MD}{FD}$. Thus, $2\left(\frac{MD}{CD}\right) = \frac{MD}{FD}$, which simplifies to $FD = \frac{CD}{2}$. Since $D$ is very close to $P$ for paraxial rays, $FD \approx f$ and $CD \approx R$, yielding $f = \frac{R}{2}$.

The Mirror Equation and Magnification

The relation between the object distance ($u$), image distance ($v$), and focal length ($f$) of a spherical mirror is known as the mirror equation :

$$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$

The linear magnification ($m$) is defined as the ratio of the height of the image ($h'$) to the height of the object ($h$):

$$m = \frac{h'}{h} = -\frac{v}{u}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: An object is placed at (i) 10 cm , (ii) 5 cm in front of a concave mirror of radius of curvature 15 cm . Find the position, nature, and magnification of the image in each case.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify the given parameters: Radius of curvature $R = -15 \text{ cm}$, so focal length $f = \frac{R}{2} = -7.5 \text{ cm}$.
  2. Case (i): Object distance $u = -10 \text{ cm}$. Use the mirror equation: $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{-10} = \frac{1}{-7.5} \implies \frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{7.5} + \frac{1}{10} = \frac{-4 + 3}{30} = -\frac{1}{30} \implies v = -30 \text{ cm}$. Magnification $m = -\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{-30}{-10} = -3$. The image is real, inverted, and magnified.
  3. Case (ii): Object distance $u = -5 \text{ cm}$. Use the mirror equation: $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{-5} = \frac{1}{-7.5} \implies \frac{1}{v} = -\frac{1}{7.5} + \frac{1}{5} = \frac{-2 + 3}{15} = \frac{1}{15} \implies v = +15 \text{ cm}$. Magnification $m = -\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{15}{-5} = +3$. The image is virtual, erect, and magnified.

Final Evaluation Output: Case (i): $v = -30 \text{ cm}$, $m = -3$ (Real, Inverted); Case (ii): $v = +15 \text{ cm}$, $m = +3$ (Virtual, Erect).

9.3 Refraction of Light

Refraction is the change in direction of propagation of a light ray when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium to another due to a change in its speed.

Laws of Refraction (Snell's Law)
  1. The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal to the interface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence ($i$) to the sine of the angle of refraction ($r$) is constant for a given pair of media and a given wavelength of light:
    3. $$\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = n_{21}$$

where $n_{21}$ is the refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1. If $n_{21} > 1$, the refracted ray bends towards the normal (medium 2 is optically denser). If $n_{21} < 1$, the ray bends away from the normal (medium 2 is optically rarer).

Optical Density vs. Mass Density

Optical density is a measure of the speed of light in a medium (higher optical density means lower speed of light), whereas mass density is mass per unit volume. They are not directly proportional. For example, turpentine has a lower mass density than water but a higher optical density, meaning light travels slower in turpentine than in water.

Apparent Depth and Lateral Shift
  • Lateral Shift : When light passes through a parallel-sided glass slab, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but suffers a lateral displacement.
  • Apparent Depth : Due to refraction, the bottom of a container filled with a liquid appears raised. For viewing near the normal direction, the relation is:
    • $$\text{Apparent Depth } (h_1) = \frac{\text{Real Depth } (h_2)}{n}$$
9.4 Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

When a ray of light travels from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium, and the angle of incidence exceeds a specific threshold, the light is completely reflected back into the denser medium. This phenomenon is called total internal reflection .

Critical Angle

The angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is $90^\circ$ is called the critical angle ($i_c$). From Snell's law:

$$\sin i_c = n_{21} = \frac{1}{n_{12}}$$

Substance Medium Refractive Index ($n$) Critical Angle ($i_c$)
Water 1.33 $48.75^\circ$
Crown Glass 1.52 $41.14^\circ$
Dense Flint Glass 1.62 $37.31^\circ$
Diamond 2.42 $24.41^\circ$
Applications of Total Internal Reflection
  • Prisms : Right-angled isosceles prisms can bend light by $90^\circ$ or $180^\circ$, or invert images without changing their size, by utilizing total internal reflection. The critical angle of the glass must be less than $45^\circ$.
  • Optical Fibres : Extremely thin strands of high-quality composite glass or quartz. Each fibre consists of a high refractive index core surrounded by a lower refractive index cladding . Light entering one end at a suitable angle undergoes repeated total internal reflections along the length of the fibre, transmitting signals over long distances with minimal intensity loss.
9.5 Refraction at Spherical Surfaces and Lenses

Refraction at curved boundaries forms the basis of image formation by lenses.

Refraction at a Single Spherical Surface

For a spherical interface of radius of curvature $R$ separating two media of refractive indices $n_1$ and $n_2$, the relation between object distance $u$ and image distance $v$ is:

$$\frac{n_2}{v} - \frac{n_1}{u} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R}$$
The Lens Maker's Formula and Thin Lens Formula

By applying the single spherical surface refraction formula successively to the two surfaces of a thin lens, we obtain the Lens Maker's Formula :

$$\frac{1}{f} = \left(\frac{n_2}{n_1} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)$$

where $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the radii of curvature of the two surfaces, and $f$ is the focal length. The Thin Lens Formula relates $u$, $v$, and $f$ as:

$$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$

The linear magnification ($m$) produced by a lens is:

$$m = \frac{h'}{h} = \frac{v}{u}$$
Power of a Lens

The power of a lens ($P$) is a measure of its ability to converge or diverge light, defined as the reciprocal of its focal length (in metres):

$$P = \frac{1}{f}$$

The SI unit of power is the dioptre ($D$), where $1 \text{ D} = 1 \text{ m}^{-1}$. Power is positive for converging (convex) lenses and negative for diverging (concave) lenses.

Combination of Thin Lenses in Contact

When several thin lenses of focal lengths $f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots$ are placed in contact, the effective focal length ($f$) of the combination is given by:

$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} + \frac{1}{f_3} + \dots$$

In terms of power, the net power ($P$) is the algebraic sum of individual powers:

$$P = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + \dots$$

The total magnification ($m$) is the product of individual magnifications:

$$m = m_1 \times m_2 \times m_3 \times \dots$$
9.6 Refraction through a Prism

A prism is a wedge-shaped transparent medium bounded by two refracting surfaces inclined at an angle called the angle of the prism ($A$).

Prism Formula and Minimum Deviation

When a ray of light passes through a triangular prism, it suffers deviation. The total angle of deviation ($\delta$) is given by:

$$\delta = i + e - A$$

where $i$ is the angle of incidence, $e$ is the angle of emergence, and $A$ is the angle of the prism. At the position of minimum deviation ($\delta = D_m$), we have $i = e$ and $r_1 = r_2 = A/2$. The refractive index ($n_{21}$) of the prism material is given by:

$$n_{21} = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A + D_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)}$$

For a thin prism (small angle $A$), the minimum deviation is simplified to:

$$D_m = (n_{21} - 1)A$$
9.7 Optical Instruments

Optical instruments utilize the principles of reflection and refraction to enhance human vision or capture images.

The Simple Microscope

A simple microscope is a converging lens of short focal length. The magnifying power ($m$) is:

  • When the image is formed at the near point ($D \approx 25 \text{ cm}$):
    • $$m = 1 + \frac{D}{f}$$
  • When the image is formed at infinity (relaxed eye):
    • $$m = \frac{D}{f}$$
The Compound Microscope

To achieve much higher magnification, a compound microscope uses two converging lenses: the objective (near the object, short focal length $f_o$) and the eyepiece (near the eye, short focal length $f_e$). The total magnification ($m$) when the final image is at infinity is:

$$m = m_o \times m_e \approx \left(\frac{L}{f_o}\right)\left(\frac{D}{f_e}\right)$$

where $L$ is the tube length (distance between the second focal point of the objective and the first focal point of the eyepiece).

Telescopes

Telescopes are used to provide angular magnification of distant objects.

  • Refracting Telescope : Consists of an objective lens of large focal length ($f_o$) and large aperture, and an eyepiece of short focal length ($f_e$). The magnifying power ($m$) is:
    • $$m = \frac{f_o}{f_e}$$

    The length of the telescope tube is $f_o + f_e$.

  • Reflecting Telescope : Uses a concave mirror as the objective instead of a lens. This eliminates chromatic aberration and reduces spherical aberration. The Cassegrain telescope configuration uses a primary concave mirror with a hole at its centre and a secondary convex mirror to focus light to the eyepiece.
