12.1 Introduction & Early Atomic Models

By the nineteenth century, substantial evidence supported the atomic hypothesis of matter. In 1897, J. J. Thomson discovered the electron , proving that atoms contain identical negatively charged constituents. Since atoms are electrically neutral, they must also contain a neutralizing positive charge.

Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1898)

Thomson proposed that the positive charge of the atom is uniformly distributed throughout its volume, with negatively charged electrons embedded in it like seeds in a watermelon. This model is historically known as the plum pudding model .

  • Key Feature: Uniform electrostatic sphere of positive charge.
  • Limitation: Failed to explain the large-angle scattering of \alpha-particles observed in subsequent experiments.
12.2 Alpha-Particle Scattering & Rutherford's Nuclear Model

In 1911, H. Geiger and E. Marsden, under the direction of Ernst Rutherford, performed a landmark scattering experiment using \alpha-particles directed at a thin gold foil.

Historical Chronicle: Ernst Rutherford (1871 – 1937)

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Conducted pioneering work on radioactivity, discovered \alpha and \beta rays, and proposed the nuclear model of the atom by scattering \alpha-particles from thin metal foils.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Established the existence of the atomic nucleus and estimated its approximate size to be around 10 -15 m to 10 -14 m.
The Geiger-Marsden Experiment

A collimated beam of 5.5 MeV \alpha-particles from a bismuth-214 source was directed at a thin gold foil of thickness 2.1 &× 10 -7 m . The scattered particles were detected using a rotatable zinc sulphide screen and microscope.

  • Observations: Most \alpha-particles passed through the foil undeflected. Only about 0.14% scattered by more than 1°, and about 1 in 8000 deflected by more than 90°.
  • Deduction: To experience such a massive repulsive force, the entire positive charge and most of the mass of the atom must be concentrated in an extremely small volume at the center, called the nucleus .
Alpha-Particle Trajectory & Impact Parameter

The trajectory of a scattered \alpha-particle depends on its impact parameter ($b$), which is the perpendicular distance of the initial velocity vector of the \alpha-particle from the center of the nucleus.

  • For a small impact parameter ($b \approx 0$), a head-on collision occurs, and the \alpha-particle rebounds back ($\theta \approx \pi$).
  • For a large impact parameter, the electrostatic force is weak, and the particle suffers negligible deflection ($\theta \approx 0$).

The electrostatic repulsive force is governed by Coulomb's Law:

$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{(2e)(Ze)}{r^2}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: In a Geiger-Marsden experiment, calculate the distance of closest approach to the nucleus of a 7.7 MeV \alpha-particle before it momentarily comes to rest and reverses its direction (for Gold, $Z = 79$).

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify conservation of mechanical energy: Initial kinetic energy ($K$) equals final potential energy ($U$) at the distance of closest approach ($d$).$$K = 7.7 \text{ MeV} = 1.2 \times 10^{-12} \text{ J}$$
  2. Set up the potential energy equation:$$K = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2Ze^2}{d}$$
  3. Isolate the distance of closest approach ($d$):$$d = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 K}$$
  4. Substitute the values ($e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$, $Z = 79$, $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 9.0 \times 10^9 \text{ N m}^2/\text{C}^2$):$$d = \frac{2 \times (9.0 \times 10^9) \times 79 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{1.2 \times 10^{-12}} \approx 3.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m} = 30 \text{ fm}$$

Final Evaluation Output: $d \approx 3.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ m}$

12.3 Electron Orbits & Classical Instability

Rutherford's nuclear model pictured the atom as a dynamically stable system where electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular orbits, balanced by electrostatic attraction.

Orbital Mechanics & Energy Relations

The electrostatic force of attraction provides the necessary centripetal force for a stable orbit of radius $r$ and velocity $v$:

$$F_e = F_c \implies \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}$$

This yields the relation for the orbit radius:

$$r = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 mv^2}$$

The total mechanical energy ($E$) of the electron is the sum of its kinetic energy ($K$) and potential energy ($U$):

$$K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$$$U = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$$$E = K + U = -\frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 r}$$

The negative sign of the total energy indicates that the electron is bound to the nucleus.

The Classical Collapse Dilemma

According to classical electromagnetic theory, an accelerating charged particle (such as a revolving electron undergoing centripetal acceleration) must continuously emit electromagnetic radiation.

  • Instability: As the electron radiates energy, its orbital radius would continuously decrease, causing it to spiral inward and collapse into the nucleus.
  • Continuous Spectrum Contradiction: The frequency of emitted light would change continuously, producing a continuous spectrum rather than the observed discrete line spectra.
12.4 Bohr Model of the Hydrogen Atom

Niels Bohr combined classical mechanics with early quantum concepts to resolve the instability of Rutherford's model.

Historical Chronicle: Niels Henrik David Bohr (1885 – 1962)

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Developed the quantum model of the hydrogen atom in 1913, explaining its discrete spectral lines. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Introduced the concept of quantized stationary states and the quantization of orbital angular momentum.
Bohr's Three Postulates
  1. First Postulate: An electron can revolve in certain stable, non-radiating orbits called stationary states without emitting radiant energy.
  2. Second Postulate: The electron revolves only in orbits where its orbital angular momentum ($L$) is an integral multiple of $\frac{h}{2\pi}$:$$L = m v r = \frac{nh}{2\pi} \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$where $h$ is Planck's constant ($6.6 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}$).
  3. Third Postulate: An electron can transition from a higher energy state ($E_i$) to a lower energy state ($E_f$) by emitting a single photon of frequency $\nu$ given by:$$h\nu = E_i - E_f$$
Mathematical Derivations for Hydrogen ($Z=1$)

By combining the quantization condition with orbital mechanics, we derive the radius of the $n$-th orbit ($r_n$):

$$r_n = \left(\frac{n^2}{m}\right) \left(\frac{h}{2\pi}\right)^2 \frac{4\pi\epsilon_0}{e^2} = \frac{n^2 h^2 \epsilon_0}{\pi m e^2}$$

The total energy of the electron in the $n$-th stationary state is:

$$E_n = -\frac{m e^4}{8 \epsilon_0^2 n^2 h^2}$$$$E_n = -\frac{2.18 \times 10^{-18}}{n^2} \text{ J} = -\frac{13.6}{n^2} \text{ eV}$$

Energy Levels & Spectral Transitions
  • Ground State ($n=1$): The lowest energy state of the hydrogen atom, where $E_1 = -13.6 \text{ eV}$.
  • Excited States ($n > 1$): States of higher energy ($E_2 = -3.40 \text{ eV}$, $E_3 = -1.51 \text{ eV}$, etc.).
  • Ionization Energy: The minimum energy required to free an electron from its ground state ($13.6 \text{ eV}$ for hydrogen).
12.5 De Broglie's Explanation of Bohr's Second Postulate

In 1923, Louis de Broglie explained Bohr's quantization of angular momentum by introducing the wave nature of electrons.

Standing Waves on a Circular Orbit

An electron in a circular orbit behaves as a particle wave. For the wave to persist without destructive interference, it must form a standing wave. This requires the circumference of the orbit to be an integral multiple of the de Broglie wavelength ($\lambda$):

$$2\pi r_n = n\lambda \quad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$

Substituting the de Broglie relation $\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{m v_n}$:

$$2\pi r_n = n \frac{h}{m v_n}$$$$m v_n r_n = \frac{nh}{2\pi}$$

This elegantly derives Bohr's second postulate from the wave-particle duality of matter.

12.6 Limitations of the Bohr Model

Despite its success in explaining the hydrogen spectrum, the Bohr model has several critical limitations:

  • Applicability: It is restricted to hydrogenic atoms (single-electron systems like H, He + , Li 2+ ) and fails for multi-electron atoms due to electron-electron interactions.
  • Spectral Intensities: It cannot explain why some spectral lines are brighter (more intense) than others.
  • Theoretical Inconsistency: It uses a semi-classical approach, mixing classical circular orbits with quantum conditions, violating the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle.
