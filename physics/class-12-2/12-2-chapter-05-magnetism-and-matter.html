Physics: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 5: Nuclei

13.1 & 13.2 Introduction and Atomic Composition

In every atom, the positive charge and mass are densely concentrated at the center, forming the nucleus . The volume of a nucleus is about 10 -12 \times the volume of the atom, meaning an atom is almost empty space. Nevertheless, the nucleus contains more than 99.9% of the mass of an atom.

Atomic Mass Unit (u)

The mass of an atom is extremely small. To measure atomic masses, the atomic mass unit ($u$) is defined as 1/12th of the mass of a carbon-12 ($^{12}\text{C}$) atom.

$$1\text{ u} = \frac{\text{mass of one }^{12}\text{C atom}}{12} = \frac{1.992647 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}}{12} \approx 1.660539 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$$

Nuclear Constituents: Protons and Neutrons
  • Proton : Carries one unit of fundamental positive charge ($+e$). It is stable, with mass $m_p = 1.00727\text{ u} = 1.67262 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$.
  • Neutron : A neutral particle discovered by James Chadwick in 1932. Its mass is $m_n = 1.00866\text{ u} = 1.6749 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg}$. A free neutron is unstable and decays into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino with a mean life of about 1000 s . It is stable inside the nucleus.

Historical Chronicle: Discovery of the Neutron

  • Historical Context & Milestones: In 1932, James Chadwick observed the emission of highly penetrating neutral radiation when beryllium nuclei were bombarded with \alpha particles. This radiation could knock out protons from light nuclei (helium, carbon, nitrogen).
  • Theoretical Foundations: By applying the conservation of energy and momentum, Chadwick proved that this neutral radiation consisted of a new neutral particle, the neutron , with a mass nearly equal to that of a proton. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1935.
Nuclear Nomenclature and Classification

A nuclear species or nuclide is represented as $_Z^A\text{X}$, where $X$ is the chemical symbol, $Z$ is the atomic number (number of protons), and $A$ is the mass number (total number of protons and neutrons, also called nucleons ). The neutron number is $N = A - Z$.

Classification Type Definition & Properties Examples
Isotopes Nuclides with the same atomic number $Z$ but different mass numbers $A$ (same proton count, different neutron count). They exhibit identical chemical properties. $_1^1\text{H}$, $_1^2\text{H}$ (Deuterium), $_1^3\text{H}$ (Tritium); $_{17}^{35}\text{Cl}$, $_{17}^{37}\text{Cl}$
Isobars Nuclides with the same mass number $A$ but different atomic numbers $Z$. $_1^3\text{H}$ and $_2^3\text{He}$
Isotones Nuclides with the same neutron number $N = A - Z$ but different atomic numbers $Z$. $_{80}^{198}\text{Hg}$ and $_{79}^{197}\text{Au}$ ($N = 118$)
13.3 Size and Density of the Nucleus

By performing scattering experiments using fast electrons as projectiles, the sizes of nuclei of various elements have been accurately measured.

Nuclear Radius Formula

The radius $R$ of a nucleus of mass number $A$ is given by the empirical relation:

$$R = R_0 A^{1/3}$$

where $R_0 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m} = 1.2\text{ fm}$ (femtometer).

Constant Nuclear Density

Since the volume of a sphere is $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$, substituting the radius formula gives:

$$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi (R_0 A^{1/3})^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A$$

This shows that the volume $V$ is directly proportional to the mass number $A$. Consequently, the nuclear density ($\rho$), which is mass divided by volume, is constant and independent of $A$ for all nuclei:

$$\rho = \frac{A \times m_u}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A} = \frac{3 m_u}{4 \pi R_0^3} \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}\text{ kg m}^{-3}$$

This density is extremely large compared to ordinary matter (e.g., water is 10 3 kg m -3 ) and is comparable to the density of matter in neutron stars.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Given the mass of an iron nucleus as 55.85 u and $A = 56$, find the nuclear density.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify given values: Mass $m = 55.85\text{ u} = 55.85 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg} \approx 9.27 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}$, and $A = 56$.
  2. Calculate nuclear volume: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi (R_0 A^{1/3})^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi (1.2 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m})^3 \times 56 \approx 4.05 \times 10^{-43} ext{ m}^3$.
  3. Compute density: $\rho = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}} = \frac{9.27 \times 10^{-26}\text{ kg}}{4.05 \times 10^{-43}\text{ m}^3} \approx 2.29 \times 10^{17}\text{ kg m}^{-3}$.

Final Evaluation Output: $\rho \approx 2.29 \times 10^{17}\text{ kg m}^{-3}$

13.4 Mass-Energy and Nuclear Binding Energy
Einstein's Mass-Energy Equivalence

Einstein showed that mass is another form of energy, related by the famous equivalence relation:

$$E = m c^2$$

where $c \approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$ is the velocity of light in vacuum.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Calculate the energy equivalent of 1 g of substance.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Convert mass to SI units: $m = 1\text{ g} = 10^{-3}\text{ kg}$.
  2. Apply Einstein's relation: $E = m c^2 = 10^{-3}\text{ kg} \times (3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1})^2$.
  3. Perform arithmetic: $E = 10^{-3} \times 9 \times 10^{16} = 9 \times 10^{13}\text{ J}$.

Final Evaluation Output: $E = 9 \times 10^{13}\text{ J}$

Mass Defect and Binding Energy

The mass of a stable nucleus $M$ is always less than the sum of the individual masses of its constituent protons and neutrons. This difference is called the mass defect ($\Delta M$):

$$\Delta M = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M$$

The energy equivalent of this mass defect is the nuclear binding energy ($E_b$), which is the energy required to separate a nucleus into its individual nucleons:

$$E_b = \Delta M c^2$$

A more useful measure of nuclear stability is the binding energy per nucleon ($E_{bn}$):

$$E_{bn} = \frac{E_b}{A}$$

The Binding Energy Curve and Its Features

The plot of $E_{bn}$ versus mass number $A$ reveals critical features of nuclear forces:

  • Constancy in Middle Range: For $30 < A < 170$, $E_{bn}$ is practically constant at approximately 8.0 MeV . The maximum is about 8.75 MeV for $A = 56$ ($^{56}\text{Fe}$).
  • Lower Values at Extremes: $E_{bn}$ is lower for light nuclei ($A < 30$) and heavy nuclei ($A > 170$), reaching about 7.6 MeV for $A = 238$ ($^{238}\text{U}$).
  • Implications for Energy Release:
    • Fission : When a very heavy nucleus ($A \approx 240$) splits into two medium-sized nuclei, the nucleons become more tightly bound, releasing a large amount of energy.
    • Fusion : When two very light nuclei ($A \le 10$) join to form a heavier nucleus, the binding energy per nucleon increases, releasing energy.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Find the energy equivalent of 1 u in Joules and MeV, and express the mass defect of $^{16}_8\text{O}$ ($\Delta M = 0.13691\text{ u}$) in $\text{MeV/c}^2$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Convert $1\text{ u}$ to energy in Joules: $E = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\text{ kg} \times (2.9979 \times 10^8\text{ m/s})^2 \approx 1.4924 \times 10^{-10}\text{ J}$.
  2. Convert Joules to eV: $E = \frac{1.4924 \times 10^{-10}\text{ J}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J/eV}} \approx 9.315 \times 10^8\text{ eV} = 931.5\text{ MeV}$. Thus, $1\text{ u} = 931.5\text{ MeV/c}^2$.
  3. Calculate binding energy of $^{16}_8\text{O}$: $E_b = 0.13691\text{ u} \times 931.5\text{ MeV/u} \approx 127.5\text{ MeV}$.

Final Evaluation Output: $1\text{ u} = 931.5\text{ MeV/c}^2$, and $E_b(^{16}_8\text{O}) = 127.5\text{ MeV}$

13.5 The Nuclear Force

The nuclear force is the strong attractive force that binds protons and neutrons together inside the tiny volume of a nucleus, overcoming the strong electrostatic Coulomb repulsion between protons.

Key Properties of the Nuclear Force
  • Extremely Strong: Much stronger than the Coulomb force and gravitational force (dominates at short distances).
  • Short-Ranged: It is active only over distances of a few femtometers ($10^{-15}\text{ m}$) and falls rapidly to zero at larger distances.
  • Charge-Independent: The nuclear force between neutron-neutron, proton-neutron, and proton-proton is approximately the same.
  • Saturation Property: A nucleon influences only its immediate neighbors, leading to the constancy of binding energy per nucleon in medium-mass nuclei.
Potential Energy Curve of Nucleon-Nucleon Interaction

The potential energy between two nucleons as a function of their separation distance $r$ shows:

  • A minimum potential energy at a distance $r_0 \approx 0.8\text{ fm}$.
  • For $r > r_0$, the force is attractive.
  • For $r < r_0$, the force becomes strongly repulsive, preventing the nucleus from collapsing.
13.6 Radioactivity

Radioactivity is a nuclear phenomenon in which an unstable nucleus spontaneously undergoes decay to achieve stability.

Historical Chronicle: Discovery of Radioactivity

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Discovered accidentally by A. H. Becquerel in 1896 while studying the phosphorescence of uranium-potassium sulphate. He observed that the compound emitted a highly penetrating radiation that blackened photographic plates even when wrapped in black paper.
  • Theoretical Foundations: This established that radioactivity is a spontaneous nuclear emission process independent of external physical conditions.
Types of Radioactive Decay
  1. $\alpha$-decay: Emission of a helium nucleus ($^4_2\text{He}$).
  2. $\beta$-decay: Emission of electrons ($\beta^-$) or positrons ($\beta^+$).
  3. $\gamma$-decay: Emission of high-energy photons (gamma rays) with energies of hundreds of keV or more.
13.7 Nuclear Energy: Fission and Fusion

Nuclear reactions release energy on the order of millions of electron volts ($\text{MeV}$), which is about a million \times greater than conventional chemical reactions (which release energy on the order of $\text{eV}$).

13.7.1 Nuclear Fission

Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two lighter nuclei of intermediate mass when bombarded with neutrons.

Example reaction for Uranium-235:

$$_0^1\text{n} + _{92}^{235}\text{U} \rightarrow _{92}^{236}\text{U} \rightarrow _{56}^{144}\text{Ba} + _{36}^{89}\text{Kr} + 3 _0^1\text{n}$$

The average energy released per fission event is approximately 200 MeV , primarily appearing as the kinetic energy of the fragments and neutrons, which is subsequently converted into heat.

13.7.2 Nuclear Fusion & Stellar Energy

Nuclear fusion is the process where two or more light nuclei combine to form a single heavier nucleus, releasing enormous energy.

To overcome the electrostatic Coulomb barrier ($\approx 400\text{ keV}$ for two protons), the nuclei must have extremely high kinetic energy, requiring temperatures of about 3 &× 10 9 K (thermonuclear fusion).

In the Sun, fusion occurs via the multi-step proton-proton cycle at a core temperature of 1.5 &× 10 7 K due to high-energy tail distributions and quantum tunneling:

$$4 _1^1\text{H} + 2e^- \rightarrow _2^4\text{He} + 2\nu + 6\gamma + 26.7\text{ MeV}$$

13.7.3 Controlled Thermonuclear Fusion

Replicating stellar fusion on Earth requires confining a high-temperature mixture of positive ions and electrons ( plasma ) at temperatures around 10 8 K . Since no physical container can withstand such temperatures, magnetic confinement techniques (such as Tokamaks) are being developed globally.

Mathematical Problem Statement: If both the number of protons and neutrons are conserved in a nuclear reaction, how is mass converted into energy?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Note that the total number of protons and neutrons is conserved, meaning the sum of their individual rest masses remains constant.
  2. However, the total binding energy of the reactant nuclei is different from that of the product nuclei.
  3. The difference in total binding energy ($\Delta E_b$) corresponds to a difference in the actual nuclear masses (mass defect). This mass difference is released as kinetic energy or radiation according to $\Delta E = \Delta M c^2$.

Final Evaluation Output: Energy is released due to the difference in nuclear binding energies, which manifests as a change in the total rest mass of the system.
