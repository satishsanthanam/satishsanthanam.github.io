14.1 Introduction & Historical Context

The development of modern electronics began with devices that could control the flow of electrons. Before solid-state electronics, vacuum tubes (or valves) were used to control electron flow. These devices operated by heating a cathode to emit electrons, which then flowed through a vacuum to an anode under the influence of varying voltages between electrodes.

Historical Chronicle: From Vacuum Valves to Solid-State Electronics

  • Historical Context & Milestones: Vacuum tubes like the diode (two electrodes), triode (three electrodes), tetrode (four electrodes), and pentode (five electrodes) dominated early electronics. In 1948 , the invention of the transistor marked a revolutionary shift towards solid-state devices.
  • Theoretical Foundations: Vacuum tubes were bulky, consumed high power, operated at high voltages (~ 100 V ), and had limited life and low reliability. Solid-state semiconductors, discovered in the 1930s , allowed charge carrier control within the solid itself, eliminating the need for a vacuum or external heating.
14.2 Classification of Solids: Metals, Semiconductors, and Insulators
Classification Based on Electrical Conductivity and Resistivity

Solids are broadly classified into three categories based on their electrical resistivity ($_\rho$) and conductivity ($_\sigma = 1/_\rho$):

  • Metals : Possess very low resistivity and high conductivity. Typical ranges: $_\rho \sim 10^{-2}$ to $10^{-8}$ $_\Omega$ m, $_\sigma \sim 10^2$ to $10^8$ S m$^{-1}$.
  • Semiconductors : Possess intermediate resistivity and conductivity. Typical ranges: $_\rho \sim 10^{-5}$ to $10^6$ $_\Omega$ m, $_\sigma \sim 10^5$ to $10^{-6}$ S m$^{-1}$.
  • Insulators : Possess high resistivity and low conductivity. Typical ranges: $_\rho \sim 10^{11}$ to $10^{19}$ $_\Omega$ m, $_\sigma \sim 10^{-11}$ to $10^{-19}$ S m$^{-1}$.
Classification Based on Energy Bands

According to the band theory of solids, the energy levels of electrons split into continuous bands due to atomic interactions in a crystal lattice:

  • Valence Band : The energy band containing the valence electrons. At absolute zero ( 0 K ), this band is completely filled.
  • Conduction Band : The energy band above the valence band. Electrons in this band are free to move and contribute to electrical conduction.
  • Energy Band Gap ($E_g$): The energy difference between the top of the valence band ($E_V$) and the bottom of the conduction band ($E_C$).

The band configurations for the three classes of solids are as follows:

  1. Metals: The conduction band is either partially filled or overlaps with the valence band ($E_g \approx 0$). Electrons can easily move into the conduction band, resulting in high conductivity.
  2. Insulators: A large energy gap exists ($E_g > 3$ eV). Electrons cannot be excited from the valence band to the conduction band by thermal energy.
  3. Semiconductors: A small energy gap exists ($E_g < 3$ eV). At room temperature, some electrons gain enough thermal energy to cross the gap and enter the conduction band.
Types of Semiconductors

Semiconductors are classified into elemental and compound types:

  • Elemental Semiconductors: Silicon (Si) and Germanium (Ge).
  • Compound Semiconductors: Can be inorganic (e.g., CdS, GaAs, CdSe, InP), organic (e.g., anthracene, doped phthalocyanines), or organic polymers (e.g., polypyrrole, polyaniline, polythiophene).
14.3 Intrinsic Semiconductors

An intrinsic semiconductor is a pure semiconductor without any intentional impurities. Silicon (Si) and Germanium (Ge) have a tetravalent structure where each atom shares its four valence electrons with four neighboring atoms to form covalent bonds.

  • At low temperatures (near 0 K ), all covalent bonds are intact, and the material behaves as an insulator.
  • As temperature increases, thermal energy breaks some covalent bonds, creating free electrons and leaving behind vacant spaces called holes .
  • A hole behaves as an apparent free particle with an effective positive charge ($+q$).
  • In an intrinsic semiconductor, the concentration of free electrons ($n_e$) is exactly equal to the concentration of holes ($n_h$): $$n_e = n_h = n_i$$ where $n_i$ is the intrinsic carrier concentration.
  • The total current ($I$) flowing through an intrinsic semiconductor is the sum of the electron current ($I_e$) and the hole current ($I_h$): $$I = I_e + I_h$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Carbon (C), Silicon (Si), and Germanium (Ge) all have the same diamond-like lattice structure with four valence electrons. Why is Carbon an insulator, while Silicon and Germanium are semiconductors?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Identify the electronic configuration and orbit of the valence electrons: For Carbon, the valence electrons lie in the second orbit ($n = 2$). For Silicon, they lie in the third orbit ($n = 3$). For Germanium, they lie in the fourth orbit ($n = 4$).
  2. Analyze the ionization energy ($E_g$): Since the valence electrons of Carbon are closest to the nucleus, they are most tightly bound. The energy gap $E_g$ is highest for Carbon ( 5.4 eV ), intermediate for Silicon ( 1.1 eV ), and lowest for Germanium ( 0.7 eV ).
  3. Evaluate thermal excitation: At room temperature, thermal energy is insufficient to excite electrons across the large gap of Carbon, making it an insulator. Significant numbers of electrons can cross the smaller gaps of Silicon and Germanium, making them semiconductors.

Final Evaluation Output: Carbon is an insulator due to its large band gap ($E_g = 5.4$ eV), whereas Silicon ($E_g = 1.1$ eV) and Germanium ($E_g = 0.7$ eV) function as semiconductors.

14.4 Extrinsic Semiconductors

The conductivity of intrinsic semiconductors is very low at room temperature. To increase conductivity, a small amount of a suitable impurity (a few parts per million) is deliberately added. This process is called doping , and the added impurities are called dopants .

n-type Semiconductors

An n-type semiconductor is formed by doping a tetravalent semiconductor (Si or Ge) with a pentavalent impurity (valency 5) such as Arsenic (As), Antimony (Sb), or Phosphorus (P).

  • Four of the five valence electrons of the dopant atom form covalent bonds with neighboring Si or Ge atoms, while the fifth electron remains weakly bound and is easily donated to the conduction band.
  • The dopant atoms are called donor impurities .
  • Electrons become the majority carriers , and holes become the minority carriers : $$n_e \gg n_h$$
  • The donor energy level ($E_D$) lies slightly below the bottom of the conduction band ($E_C$).
p-type Semiconductors

A p-type semiconductor is formed by doping a tetravalent semiconductor with a trivalent impurity (valency 3) such as Indium (In), Boron (B), or Aluminium (Al).

  • The dopant atom has only three valence electrons, leaving a vacancy (hole) in the fourth covalent bond with neighboring Si or Ge atoms.
  • An electron from a neighboring bond can jump to fill this vacancy, effectively moving the hole to a new site.
  • The dopant atoms are called acceptor impurities .
  • Holes become the majority carriers , and electrons become the minority carriers : $$n_h \gg n_e$$
  • The acceptor energy level ($E_A$) lies slightly above the top of the valence band ($E_V$).
Mass Action Law and Charge Neutrality

In both n-type and p-type semiconductors, the product of electron and hole concentrations remains constant at a given temperature under thermal equilibrium:

$$n_e n_h = n_i^2$$

Despite the abundance of one type of charge carrier, the doped semiconductor crystal remains electrically neutral because the charge of the additional mobile carriers is exactly balanced by the charge of the ionized dopant cores.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Suppose a pure Silicon crystal has $5 \times 10^{28}$ atoms m$^{-3}$. It is doped with a 1 ppm concentration of pentavalent Arsenic. Calculate the number of electrons and holes, given that $n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16}$ m$^{-3}$.

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Calculate the donor concentration ($N_D$): Since doping is 1 ppm (1 part per million), the number of donor atoms per unit volume is: $$N_D = \frac{5 \times 10^{28}}{10^6} = 5 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^{-3}$$
  2. Determine the electron concentration ($n_e$): Since $N_D \gg n_i$, the concentration of electrons is approximately equal to the donor concentration: $$n_e \approx N_D = 5 \times 10^{22}\text{ m}^{-3}$$
  3. Calculate the hole concentration ($n_h$) using the mass action law: $$n_h = \frac{n_i^2}{n_e} = \frac{(1.5 \times 10^{16})^2}{5 \times 10^{22}} = \frac{2.25 \times 10^{32}}{5 \times 10^{22}} = 4.5 \times 10^9\text{ m}^{-3}$$

Final Evaluation Output: The electron concentration is $5 \times 10^{22}$ m$^{-3}$ and the hole concentration is $4.5 \times 10^9$ m$^{-3}$.

14.5 p-n Junction Formation & Physics

A p-n junction is formed by joining p-type and n-type semiconductor regions within a single crystal. Two primary physical processes occur during its formation:

Diffusion and Drift Processes
  1. Diffusion: Due to the concentration gradient, holes diffuse from the p-side to the n-side ($p \rightarrow n$) and electrons diffuse from the n-side to the p-side ($n \rightarrow p$). This movement produces a diffusion current .
  2. Depletion Region: As electrons diffuse from the n-side, they leave behind immobile positively charged donor ions. Similarly, holes diffusing from the p-side leave behind immobile negatively charged acceptor ions. This creates a space-charge region near the junction, depleted of mobile carriers, called the depletion region . Its thickness is typically of the order of 0.1 \mu m .
  3. Barrier Potential: The space-charge region establishes an electric field directed from the positive n-side to the negative p-side. This field opposes further diffusion of majority carriers and creates a potential difference across the junction called the barrier potential ($V_0$).
  4. Drift: The electric field sweeps minority carriers across the junction (electrons from p-side to n-side, holes from n-side to p-side), creating a drift current in the direction opposite to the diffusion current.
  5. Equilibrium: At equilibrium, the diffusion current equals the drift current, resulting in zero net current across the junction.

Mathematical Problem Statement: Can we physically join a slab of p-type semiconductor to another slab of n-type semiconductor to form a p-n junction?

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Analyze surface roughness: Any physically polished semiconductor slab has surface roughness on a scale much larger than the inter-atomic spacing (~ 2 to 3 Å ).
  2. Evaluate contact area: When two such slabs are pressed together, contact occurs only at a few microscopic points, leaving air gaps and discontinuities.
  3. Determine charge carrier flow: The lack of continuous atomic contact prevents the smooth diffusion and drift of charge carriers across the interface, making it behave as a discontinuity rather than a single junction.

Final Evaluation Output: No, a p-n junction cannot be formed by physical contact; it must be fabricated on a single continuous crystal wafer using specialized doping techniques.

14.6 Semiconductor Diode & Biasing

A semiconductor diode is a p-n junction with metallic contacts at both ends for external electrical connections. It is represented symbolically by an arrow pointing in the direction of conventional forward current flow.

Forward Biasing

When an external voltage $V$ is applied such that the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the p-side and the negative terminal to the n-side:

  • The applied voltage opposes the built-in barrier potential, reducing the effective barrier height to $(V_0 - V)$ and narrowing the depletion region.
  • Majority carriers are injected across the junction, leading to a significant forward current measured in milliamperes ( mA ).
  • The current increases exponentially once the applied voltage exceeds the threshold voltage (or cut-in voltage), which is approximately 0.2 V for Germanium and 0.7 V for Silicon.
Reverse Biasing

When the positive terminal of the battery is connected to the n-side and the negative terminal to the p-side:

  • The applied voltage aids the built-in barrier potential, increasing the effective barrier height to $(V_0 + V)$ and widening the depletion region.
  • This suppresses the flow of majority carriers. Only a very small reverse saturation current , due to the drift of minority carriers, flows across the junction. This current is measured in microamperes ( \mu A ).
  • At a critical high reverse voltage, known as the breakdown voltage ($V_{br}$), the reverse current increases sharply. Operating a general-purpose diode beyond this point can destroy it due to overheating.
Dynamic Resistance

The dynamic resistance ($r_d$) of a diode is defined as the ratio of a small change in voltage ($\Delta V$) to the resulting small change in current ($\Delta I$):

$$r_d = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}$$

Mathematical Problem Statement: Using the V-I characteristics of a Silicon diode, calculate its dynamic resistance at (a) a forward current of $I_D = 15$ mA (where at $I = 20$ mA, $V = 0.8$ V and at $I = 10$ mA, $V = 0.7$ V) and (b) a reverse voltage of $V_D = -10$ V (where $I = -1$ $\mu$A).

Step-by-Step Algorithmic Solution Trace:

  1. Calculate forward dynamic resistance ($r_{fb}$): Use the change in voltage and current around $15$ mA: $$\Delta V = 0.8\text{ V} - 0.7\text{ V} = 0.1\text{ V}$$ $$\Delta I = 20\text{ mA} - 10\text{ mA} = 10\text{ mA} = 10 \times 10^{-3}\text{ A}$$ $$r_{fb} = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I} = \frac{0.1\text{ V}}{10 \times 10^{-3}\text{ A}} = 10\text{ }\Omega$$
  2. Calculate reverse dynamic resistance ($r_{rb}$): At $V = -10$ V, the current is constant at $-1$ $\mu$A. The resistance is: $$r_{rb} = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{10\text{ V}}{1 \times 10^{-6}\text{ A}} = 1.0 \times 10^7\text{ }\Omega$$

Final Evaluation Output: The forward dynamic resistance is $10$ $\Omega$ and the reverse dynamic resistance is $1.0 \times 10^7$ $\Omega$.

14.7 Diode Applications: Rectifiers and Filters

Because a p-n junction diode conducts current primarily in one direction, it is widely used as a rectifier to convert alternating current (AC) into direct current (DC).

Half-Wave Rectifier

A half-wave rectifier uses a single diode connected in series with a load resistor ($R_L$):

  • During the positive half-cycle of the input AC voltage, the diode is forward-biased and conducts current, producing an output voltage across $R_L$.
  • During the negative half-cycle, the diode is reverse-biased and does not conduct, resulting in zero output voltage.
  • The output is a pulsating DC waveform containing only the positive half-cycles of the input AC.
Full-Wave Rectifier

A full-wave rectifier utilizes two diodes connected to a centre-tap transformer:

  • During the positive half-cycle, one diode is forward-biased and conducts while the other is reverse-biased.
  • During the negative half-cycle, the second diode becomes forward-biased and conducts while the first is reverse-biased.
  • This ensures that current flows through the load resistor in the same direction during both half-cycles, yielding a more efficient rectified output.
Filter Circuits

The output of a rectifier is pulsating and contains AC fluctuations (ripples). To obtain a steady DC voltage, a filter circuit is connected across the output:

  • A capacitor filter is connected in parallel with the load resistor ($R_L$).
  • When the rectified voltage rises, the capacitor charges to its peak value. When the voltage falls, the capacitor slowly discharges through the load, smoothing out the ripples.
  • The rate of discharge depends on the time constant $\tau = R_L C$. A larger capacitance $C$ provides a longer time constant and a smoother DC output.
