Political Science: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 2: Freedom

The Ideal of Freedom

Human history presents a continuous struggle between domination by powerful groups and the heroic quest for individual and collective freedom . At its core, freedom represents the desire of individuals and societies to control their own destinies, make autonomous choices, and express their creative potential.

Profile: Nelson Mandela (1918 – 2013)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the anti-apartheid movement, African National Congress (ANC) leader, and the first black President of democratic South Africa.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed the struggle against racial segregation, arguing that freedom is indivisible and must encompass the liberation of both the oppressed and the oppressor from unjust constraints.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Authored the iconic autobiography Long Walk to Freedom ; endured 27 years of imprisonment, frequently in solitary confinement, to dismantle the institutionalized apartheid regime.

Profile: Aung San Suu Kyi (1945 – Present)

  • Role & Affiliation: Burmese pro-democracy activist, leader of the National League for Democracy (NLD) in Myanmar.
  • Ideological Contributions: Conceptualized freedom as fundamentally linked to psychological liberation, asserting that true freedom cannot exist without overcoming fear.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Authored the celebrated essay collection Freedom from Fear ; spent years under house arrest, separated from her family, as a peaceful resistance against military rule, heavily inspired by Mahatma Gandhi's principles of non-violence.
What is Freedom?
The Dual Dimensions of Freedom

Political theory conceptualizes freedom through two distinct yet complementary dimensions:

  • Absence of External Constraints: A condition where individuals are not subject to external coercion, arbitrary controls, or physical force, allowing them to make independent decisions.
  • Expansion of Capabilities: The positive creation of social, economic, and political conditions that enable individuals to develop their potential, creativity, and talents.
Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Negative Liberty An inviolable, sacred sphere of non-interference where the individual is the absolute master. Natural rights, individual autonomy, and human dignity. Protects personal choices (e.g., dress, belief, private life) from state or societal coercion.
Positive Liberty The enabling conditions (education, employment, health) that allow individuals to realize their potential. Social justice, welfare state principles, and egalitarian philosophy. Enables meaningful participation in democratic decision-making and self-governance.
Swaraj in Indian Political Thought

The concept of Swaraj serves as the Indian analogue to freedom, incorporating deep philosophical and political dimensions:

  • Etymology: Derived from Swa (Self) and Raj (Rule), meaning both "rule of the self" and "rule over oneself".
  • Constitutional & National Demand: Utilized during the Indian national movement as a rallying cry for self-determination, famously declared by Lokmanya Tilak: "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it."
  • Gandhian Interpretation: Articulated in Mahatma Gandhi's seminal work Hind Swaraj (1909) as a state of moral self-rule, where individuals achieve liberation by redeeming their self-respect, self-responsibility, and capacity for self-realization from dehumanizing institutions.
Sources of Constraints and the Need for Rules
Where Do Constraints Come From?

Constraints on individual freedom can emerge from multiple structural and institutional sources:

  • Political Domination: External controls imposed by colonial rulers, military regimes, or authoritarian governments through force and coercive legislation.
  • Social Inequality: Systemic restrictions embedded in traditional hierarchies, such as the caste system , which deny basic liberties to marginalized groups.
  • Economic Disparity: Extreme inequalities in wealth distribution that restrict the poor from exercising meaningful choices or accessing opportunities.

Profile: Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose (1897 – 1945)

  • Role & Affiliation: Prominent leader of the Indian National Congress, founder of the Indian National Army (INA).
  • Ideological Contributions: Advocated for an all-round, multidimensional conception of freedom that transcends mere political independence.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: In his 1929 Lahore Presidential Address, defined freedom as encompassing equal distribution of wealth, the complete abolition of caste barriers, social iniquities, communalism, and religious intolerance.
Why Do We Need Constraints?

Living in a diverse society requires a framework of rules to prevent descent into social chaos and resolve inevitable conflicts over scarce resources, differing beliefs, and competing ambitions.

  • Conflict Resolution: Mechanisms are necessary to control violence and settle disputes arising from everyday friction (e.g., road rage, housing disputes, disagreements over media screenings).
  • Mutual Respect: Constraints ensure that powerful groups cannot coercively impose their views or lifestyles on minorities or dissenting individuals.
  • The Liberal Perspective: Liberalism prioritizes individual liberty but recognizes that a democratic state must enforce minimal rules to protect tolerance, reduce extreme inequalities, and safeguard the rights of all citizens.
The Harm Principle

To determine which social constraints are legitimate and which constitute unjustifiable interference, political theorists rely on the harm principle , formulated by John Stuart Mill in his classic essay On Liberty .

"...the sole end for which mankind are warranted, individually or collectively, in interfering with the liberty of action of any of their number, is self-protection. That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Mill establishes a clear threshold for state intervention. The state is only justified in using the coercive force of law to restrict an individual's freedom when their actions pose a direct, serious threat of harm to others. For minor inconveniences or moral disagreements, society should employ social disapproval rather than legal punishment.

Self-Regarding vs. Other-Regarding Actions

Mill divides human actions into two distinct categories to define the limits of state authority:

  1. Self-Regarding Actions: Activities whose consequences affect only the individual actor (e.g., personal lifestyle, dress, religious beliefs). In this domain, the state has no business to interfere.
  2. Other-Regarding Actions: Activities that have direct consequences for others and may cause them harm (e.g., public safety violations, hate speech, environmental destruction). These actions are subject to external regulation and state constraints.
Reasonable Restrictions

In democratic polities like India, the constitutional framework permits constraints on freedom only under the doctrine of reasonable restrictions . To be valid, restrictions must be:

  • Defensible by reason and logic.
  • Proportionate and not excessive.
  • Designed to protect public order, morality, or national sovereignty without destroying the core essence of freedom.
Freedom of Expression

The freedom to express one's thoughts, beliefs, and artistic creations is considered a core component of the minimum area of non-interference. However, it is frequently targeted by demands for censorship and bans.

J.S. Mill's Four Defences of Free Speech

Mill argued passionately against restricting freedom of expression, even for ideas that society deems false or offensive, offering four key reasons:

  1. No Idea is Completely False: Supposedly false ideas often contain a kernel of truth. Banning them deprives society of discovering that hidden element.
  2. Truth Emerges via Conflict: Truth does not exist in a vacuum; it can only be verified and kept alive through robust debate and clash with opposing viewpoints.
  3. Prevents Truth from Becoming a Cliché: Without challenge, even the most profound truths degenerate into unthinking, dogmatic clichés.
  4. Human Fallibility: We cannot be absolutely certain that what we currently believe to be true is indeed the absolute truth. Historically, ideas once suppressed as dangerous falsehoods (e.g., heliocentrism) turned out to be correct.
The Perils of Banning and Censorship

Demands to ban books, plays, or films (such as Deepa Mehta's film on Varanasi widows, Aubrey Menon's Ramayana Retold , or Salman Rushdie's The Satanic Verses ) represent short-term political expedients that carry severe long-term systemic costs.

  • Habit of Banning: Once a society begins to ban controversial works to appease specific groups, it develops a structural habit of censorship, gradually eroding the general condition of freedom.
  • Voluntary vs. Coercive Constraints: While individuals may voluntarily accept personal or contractual restrictions (e.g., royal household employment contracts), state-enforced or religiously coerced bans permanently damage democratic dialogue.
