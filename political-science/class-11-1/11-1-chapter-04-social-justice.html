Political Science: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 4: Social Justice

What is Justice?

Justice concerns the way in which public life is ordered and the principles according to which social goods and social duties are distributed among different members of society. It is of central importance for politics.

Historical and Philosophical Foundations
  • Ancient India : Justice was associated with dharma . Maintaining dharma or a just social order was considered the primary duty of kings.
  • China (Confucius) : Argued that kings should maintain justice by punishing wrongdoers and rewarding the virtuous.
  • Ancient Greece (Plato) : Discussed justice in The Republic . Through Socrates, Plato examined why we should be concerned about justice, arguing that justice involves the well-being of all people, not just doing good to friends and harm to enemies.

Glaucon argued to Socrates that being unjust is naturally good and suffering injustice is evil. He claimed people agree to laws and covenants only because they lack the power to commit injustice with impunity, making justice a compromise.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Socrates refuted this by showing that if everyone were unjust, no one would be secure. Obeying laws and maintaining justice is in our long-term collective self-interest because it ensures the well-being of all members of society, similar to how a doctor cares for all patients.

Giving Each Person Their Due

Our modern understanding of justice is closely linked to human rights and dignity.

Profile: Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)

  • Role & Affiliation: German philosopher of the Enlightenment.
  • Ideological Contributions: Argued that human beings possess intrinsic dignity .
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Asserted that because humans have dignity, they must be given the opportunity to develop their talents and pursue their chosen goals.
Three Principles of Justice

To decide how to give each person their due, modern societies balance three distinct principles of justice.

1. Equal Treatment for Equals
  • All individuals share certain characteristics as human beings and deserve equal rights and equal treatment.
  • Rights in Liberal Democracies : Include civil rights (life, liberty, property), political rights (right to vote), and social rights (equal opportunities).
  • Non-discrimination : People should not be discriminated against on grounds of class, caste, race, or gender. They must be judged on their actions and work, not their group identity.
2. Proportionate Justice
  • Treating everyone equally can sometimes be unjust (e.g., giving all students equal marks regardless of effort).
  • Justice requires rewarding people in proportion to the scale, quality, and effort of their work, provided everyone starts from the same baseline of equal rights.
  • Factors to consider: effort required, skills required, and possible dangers involved (e.g., miners, policemen, skilled craftsmen).
3. Recognition of Special Needs
  • Taking into account the special needs of people while distributing rewards or duties to promote social justice.
  • Extends the principle of equal treatment by recognizing that those who are unequal in certain respects (e.g., physical disabilities, age, lack of access to education/healthcare) should be treated differently.
  • Indian Constitution : Allows for reservations in government jobs and educational admissions for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes to counter historical caste-based discrimination.

Let's Think Scenarios: Suresh (visually impaired) gets extra time for an exam; Geeta (walks with a limp) is offered the same extra time; a teacher gives grace marks to weaker students; a professor distributes different papers based on capability; a proposal to reserve 33 percent of parliamentary seats for women.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: These scenarios highlight the tension between equal treatment, proportionality, and special needs. While Suresh's extra time is a justified recognition of a specific disability, giving Geeta the same extra time for a physical limp that does not affect writing is an incorrect application of the special needs principle. Reserving seats for women addresses systemic underrepresentation to ensure substantive political equality.

Just Distribution & John Rawls' Theory

Social justice requires the fair distribution of goods and services within a society to ensure a level playing field.

Just Distribution
  • If serious economic or social inequalities exist, governments must redistribute resources to ensure basic equality of life conditions and opportunities.
  • Indian Context : The Constitution abolished untouchability, and state governments instituted land reforms to redistribute land more fairly.
John Rawls' Theory of Justice

Profile: John Rawls (1921–2002)

  • Role & Affiliation: American political philosopher.
  • Ideological Contributions: Formulated the theory of justice based on rational choice rather than benevolence.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Introduced the concept of the veil of ignorance to determine fair principles of social organization.
  • Veil of Ignorance : A hypothetical scenario where individuals decide how to organize society without knowing their own future position, class, caste, gender, or talents.
  • Rational Choice : Under the veil of ignorance, rational individuals will choose rules that protect the worst-off, because they themselves might end up in that position.
  • This framework does not require heroic self-sacrifice; it relies on rational self-interest to achieve fair and impartial distribution.
Pursuing Social Justice: Free Markets vs. State Intervention

A just society must provide its members with the basic minimum conditions to live healthy, secure, and productive lives.

Basic Minimum Conditions
  • Includes basic nourishment, housing, clean drinking water, education, and a minimum wage.
  • Providing these is a primary responsibility of democratic governments, though it poses a heavy financial burden on developing nations like India.

Profile: B.R. Ambedkar (1891–1956)

  • Role & Affiliation: Chairman of the Drafting Committee of the Indian Constitution, social reformer.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed the annihilation of caste and social equality.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Defined a just society as one where the "ascending sense of reverence and descending sense of contempt is dissolved into the creation of a compassionate society."
The Debate: Free Markets vs. State Intervention
Perspective Core Arguments Potential Limitations
Free Market Supporters Individuals should be free to own property, contract, and compete. Markets reward merit and talent, are neutral to caste/religion, and offer consumers more choices. Private agencies may not provide essential services (like education or healthcare) in remote areas if unprofitable, or may provide substandard services.
State Intervention Supporters The state must step in to ensure basic facilities are available to all. Free markets tend to work in the interest of the strong, wealthy, and powerful, denying opportunities to the weak. Can place a heavy financial and administrative burden on the government, potentially leading to inefficiencies.
