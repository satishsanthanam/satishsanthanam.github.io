Political Science: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 6: Citizenship

Introduction to Citizenship

Citizenship represents full and equal membership of a political community, providing individuals with a collective political identity and a basket of rights and obligations. In the contemporary world, citizens look to their respective states for protection, rights, and facilitation of travel, while those without state membership face severe precarity.

Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Citizenship Full and equal membership of a political community. State constitution and democratic theory. Provides collective political identity, guarantees rights, and ensures equality of status.
Civil Rights Freedom of speech, belief, and expression. Constitutional guarantees and rule of law. Protects individual life, liberty, and property from arbitrary state action.
Political Rights Right to vote, contest elections, and participate in governance. Democratic franchise and constitutional provisions. Enables active participation in the democratic process and decision-making.
Socio-Economic Rights Right to a minimum wage, education, shelter, and livelihood. Welfare state policies and human rights frameworks. Ensures basic material conditions for a life of dignity and equal opportunity.

During the seventeenth to twentieth centuries, the white minority in South Africa established a system of racial segregation (Apartheid) over the black population. Whites enjoyed full political and civil rights, including the right to vote, contest elections, and purchase property. In contrast, Blacks were denied these rights, forced into separate colonies, required to carry 'passes' to work in white neighborhoods, and segregated in schools.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This historical case illustrates how the denial of full and equal membership creates a highly unequal, oppressive, and unstable political system. It highlights that citizenship is not merely a legal status but must embody the principle of equality to prevent systemic human rights violations and social conflict.

  • Historical Struggles : Rights enjoyed by modern citizens are the result of historic struggles, such as the French Revolution of 1789, anti-colonial movements in Asia and Africa, and ongoing struggles like the Dalit movement and women's movement in India.
  • Citizen-Citizen Relations : Citizenship extends beyond state-individual dynamics to include moral obligations to participate in, contribute to, and protect the shared life, culture, and natural resources of the community.
Full and Equal Membership

The concept of full and equal membership is frequently tested by real-world socio-economic dynamics, particularly when resources are scarce and divisions arise between 'insiders' and 'outsiders'.

Migration and the Freedom of Movement
  • Freedom of Movement : A fundamental right allowing citizens to seek employment, study, or live anywhere within the country. Unskilled and skilled labor migrate to economic hubs (e.g., IT workers to Bangalore, nurses from Kerala).
  • Local Resistance : Local populations may resist migration due to competition for scarce jobs, amenities, and resources, sometimes leading to nativist slogans like 'Mumbai for Mumbaikars' or organized violence.
  • Socio-Economic Discrepancy : Society often welcomes skilled, affluent migrants while marginalizing poor, unskilled laborers, raising critical questions about whether equal rights are truly universal across class lines.
The Right to Protest

In democratic societies, disputes regarding migration or resource allocation must be resolved through negotiation and discussion rather than force. The right to protest is a key aspect of the freedom of expression , allowing citizens to influence public opinion and state policy through peaceful demonstrations, media campaigns, and judicial appeals, provided they do not harm life or property.

Profile: Martin Luther King Jr. (1929-1968)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the American Civil Rights Movement in the 1950s and 1960s.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed non-violent resistance against racial segregation; argued that segregation inflicts deep psychological wounds ('social leprosy') and diminishes the quality of life for both black and white communities.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Led protests against segregation laws in the US South; advocated for the inherent dignity and equality of all human beings regardless of race.
Equal Rights and the Urban Poor

A major challenge to equal citizenship is the existence of extreme socio-economic inequalities, particularly visible in the plight of the urban poor and slum dwellers.

Profile: T. H. Marshall (1893-1981)

  • Role & Affiliation: Influential British sociologist and author of 'Citizenship and Social Class' (1950).
  • Ideological Contributions: Defined citizenship as a status of equality that counters the divisive effects of class hierarchy; conceptualized citizenship as involving civil, political, and social rights.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Established that social rights (access to education and employment) are essential for citizens to lead a life of dignity and achieve true integration in a community.

In the landmark case of Olga Tellis vs. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985), social activists filed a Public Interest Litigation on behalf of pavement and slum dwellers facing eviction. The Supreme Court ruled that Article 21 of the Constitution, which guarantees the right to life, includes the right to livelihood. Therefore, eviction without alternative accommodation violates the right to shelter.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This ruling demonstrates how judicial interpretation can expand the scope of constitutional rights to protect marginalized citizens. It establishes that formal political rights are meaningless without the socio-economic conditions (like shelter and livelihood) necessary to sustain life.

Marginalized Groups and Policy Challenges
  • Slum Dwellers : Contribute significantly to the urban economy as hawkers, domestic workers, and artisans, yet face shocking living conditions (lack of sanitation, clean water) and difficulties exercising political rights due to the lack of a fixed address.
  • Tribal and Forest Dwellers : Face threats to their traditional livelihoods and habitats from commercial mining, tourism, and development projects, requiring governments to balance environmental protection with national development.

In Zimbabwe, historical land distribution left 32% of fertile, irrigated agricultural land in the hands of a tiny white minority (0.06% of the population), while millions of black peasant families held less fertile, unirrigated land. In 1997, President Mugabe announced plans to reclaim 1500 white-owned farms.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This case highlights the tension between historical claims of native populations and the citizenship rights of long-term minority residents. It underscores the difficulty of resolving deep-seated historical injustices within a democratic framework of equal citizenship.

Citizen and Nation

The modern concept of the nation-state links political citizenship with a shared national identity, culture, and history, often creating tensions between assimilation and diversity.

Models of National Identity and Assimilation
  • France : Employs a secular, inclusive model where citizens are expected to assimilate into the national culture in the public sphere, while retaining personal beliefs in private. This has led to controversies, such as banning religious symbols (Sikh turbans, Muslim headscarves) in state schools.
  • Germany and Israel : Historically prioritized ethnic origin or religion as key criteria for granting citizenship, though these criteria remain subjects of intense domestic debate.
  • India : Defines itself as a secular, democratic nation-state. The Indian Constitution attempts to accommodate vast diversity (Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, linguistic and religious minorities) without forcing assimilation, providing equal rights while protecting distinct cultural practices.
Constitutional Provisions in India

Under Part II of the Indian Constitution and subsequent parliamentary laws, citizenship can be acquired through birth , descent , registration , naturalisation , or inclusion of territory . The state is constitutionally prohibited from discriminating against citizens based on religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth.

Universal and Global Citizenship

In an increasingly interconnected world, the limitations of national citizenship have prompted debates on universal human rights and global citizenship.

The Challenge of Statelessness
  • Refugees and Migrants : Millions of people are displaced by war, famine, or persecution (e.g., in Darfur, Palestine, Myanmar, Bangladesh). When no state is willing to grant them membership, they become stateless peoples , lacking basic rights, legal employment, or education.
  • State Exclusion : Despite humanitarian claims, states use sovereign power (walls, fences, legal barriers) to restrict entry and protect their own resources, leaving the UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) to manage the crisis.
The Concept of Global Citizenship

Global citizenship is supported by the growing interconnectedness of the modern world through digital communications (internet, television, cell phones). It offers a framework to address transnational challenges that national governments cannot solve alone.

Global Issue Transnational Impact Need for Global Cooperation
Natural Disasters Events like the 2004 Asian Tsunami affect multiple nations simultaneously. Requires immediate, coordinated international aid and sympathy across borders.
Pandemics & Diseases Spread of viral threats (e.g., bird flu) ignores national boundaries. Demands global monitoring and collaborative healthcare strategies by the UN and member states.
Statelessness & Migration Displaced populations seek refuge across international borders. Requires a global framework of human rights to guarantee basic dignity regardless of national status.
