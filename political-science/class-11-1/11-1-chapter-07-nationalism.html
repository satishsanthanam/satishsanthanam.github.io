Political Science: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 7: Nationalism

Introducing Nationalism

Nationalism is one of the most influential political creeds of the modern era, shaping global history by inspiring both intense loyalties and deep-seated conflicts.

  • Dual Nature : Acts as a powerful force for unity and liberation from colonial rule, but also serves as a source of division, separatist conflicts, and bitter wars.
  • Historical Phases : Led to the 19th-century unification of small kingdoms into nation-states (e.g., Germany, Italy) and the 20th-century dissolution of large empires (Austro-Hungarian, Russian, British, French, Dutch, Portuguese).
  • Modern Relevance : Despite globalization, nationalism remains highly relevant, visible in international sports, cultural connections, and ongoing separatist struggles (e.g., Quebecois in Canada, Tamils in Sri Lanka).
Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Nationalism A compelling political creed that shapes history, inspiring collective identity and political mobilization. Shared beliefs, historical identity, and collective aspirations of a group. Unifies diverse groups, mobilizes against oppressive rule, and establishes sovereign states.
Nation An "imagined community" held together by collective beliefs, aspirations, and mutual identification. Collective consciousness, shared history, territory, and political ideals. Provides a framework for democratic citizenship, mutual obligations, and self-governance.
National Self-Determination The right of a nation to govern itself and determine its future development. International recognition of a distinct political entity or statehood. Empowers communities to protect their culture and interests through sovereign statehood.
Nations and Nationalism: Core Assumptions

A nation is not a casual collection of people, nor is it defined solely by kinship or face-to-face relationships like families or tribes. Instead, it is an imagined community built on several key assumptions:

The Five Pillars of National Identity
  • Shared Beliefs : A nation exists when its members collectively believe they belong together and share a vision for their political future.
  • History : Embodying a sense of continuing historical identity, drawing on collective memories, legends, and historical records to link the past with the future.
  • Territory : Identification with a specific geographical homeland (motherland, fatherland, or holy land) which often becomes a source of collective identity and territorial conflict.
  • Shared Political Ideals : A collective commitment to a set of values and principles (such as democracy, secularism, and liberalism) that define the political identity of the nation.
  • Common Political Identity : Moving beyond cultural homogeneity to forge a shared political vision, binding citizens through mutual rights and obligations.
Cultural vs. Political Nationalism

Imposing a single cultural identity (language, religion, or descent) as a condition of national belonging poses a severe threat to democratic values:

  • Internal Diversity : Major religions and cultures are internally diverse; forcing a single interpretation leads to an oppressive and authoritarian society.
  • Exclusionary Nature : Culturally defined nations exclude minorities, restricting their liberty and violating the democratic ideal of equal treatment.
  • Political Solution : Democracies must define national identity in political terms, emphasizing loyalty to constitutional values rather than a specific religion or language.
National Self-Determination

The claim to national self-determination asserts that nations have the right to govern themselves and seek international recognition as distinct political entities.

  • One Culture - One State : A 19th-century European ideal that gained traction post-WWI (Treaty of Versailles). It proved highly disruptive, leading to mass migrations, displacement of millions, and communal violence.
  • The Paradox of Statehood : Newly formed nation-states, having achieved independence through struggle, often suppress minority groups within their own borders who demand their own self-determination.
  • Democratic Alternative : Rather than fragmenting into unviable micro-states, the solution lies in making existing states more democratic, equal, and inclusive of diverse cultural identities.

Case Study: Demand for National Self-Determination in Basque
Basque is a hilly, prosperous, and geographically distinct region in Spain. Recognized as an autonomous region, Basque nationalists seek full independence, citing a unique culture and language distinct from Spanish. Despite historical autonomy dating back to Roman \times, the region faced severe suppression under the dictator Franco, who banned the Basque language. Although repressive measures have been withdrawn, suspicion of the Spanish government remains high, and the movement has historically utilized both constitutional and violent means to press for statehood.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This case illustrates the limits of regional autonomy in satisfying nationalist aspirations when deep-seated cultural, linguistic, and historical differences persist. It highlights the challenge democratic states face in balancing territorial integrity with the self-determination demands of distinct cultural minorities.

Nationalism and Pluralism

Abandoning the rigid "one-culture-one-state" model requires democratic societies to adopt pluralism, recognizing and protecting the rights of cultural minorities.

  • Group Rights : Constitutional protections for minority languages, cultures, and religions, alongside representation in legislative bodies and state institutions.
  • Inclusive Identity : Defining national identity in a broad, inclusive manner that values the unique contributions of all cultural communities.
  • Multiple Identities : Recognizing that individuals possess multiple overlapping identities (gender, caste, religion, language, region). A healthy democracy allows these to coexist without forcing citizens to choose one over the others.

Profile: Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941)

  • Role & Affiliation: Renowned Indian poet, writer, and philosopher; active supporter of Indian independence but a sharp critic of aggressive nationalism.
  • Ideological Contributions: Distinguished between opposing Western imperialism and rejecting Western civilization. He argued that India should remain rooted in its own culture while freely learning from global advancements.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Critiqued narrow patriotism, warning that rejecting foreign influences (such as Christianity, Judaism, Zoroastrianism, and Islam) would lead to domestic intolerance and limit India's civilizational growth. He famously asserted: "Patriotism cannot be our final spiritual shelter; my refuge is humanity."
