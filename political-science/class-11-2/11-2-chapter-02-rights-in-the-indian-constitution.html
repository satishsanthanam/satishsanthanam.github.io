Political Science: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 2: Rights in the Indian Constitution

1. Introduction & The Importance of Rights

A constitution is not merely a document outlining the composition and relations of government organs; it sets limits on state power and guarantees a democratic framework where citizens enjoy fundamental liberties. In a democracy, individuals must possess certain rights that the government is bound to recognize and protect.

Case Studies on the Violation and Enforcement of Rights

In 1982, during the construction work for the Asian Games in Delhi, the government engaged private contractors who employed a large number of poor construction workers from various parts of India. These workers were kept in poor working conditions and paid less than the government-mandated minimum wages. A team of social scientists petitioned the Supreme Court, arguing that paying less than minimum wage constitutes begar or forced labour, violating the Fundamental Right against exploitation. The Supreme Court accepted the plea and directed the government to ensure the workers received their prescribed wages.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This case demonstrates that constitutional guarantees are not merely passive declarations but active legal instruments. The judiciary acted as a critical watchdog, expanding the definition of forced labour to include underpaid work, thereby holding both private contractors and the state executive accountable to the Right against Exploitation .

Machal Lalung, a resident of Chuburi village in Morigaon, Assam, was arrested at age 23 on charges of causing grievous injuries. Found mentally unstable to stand trial, he was sent to Lok Priya Gopinath Bordoloi Mental Hospital in Tejpur. Although doctors declared him fit to stand trial in 1967 and again in 1996, jail authorities ignored the notices. Machal remained in judicial custody until July 2005, when he was finally released at age 77 after the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) intervened. He spent 54 years in custody without his case ever coming up for a hearing.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Machal's case highlights the severe gap between constitutional theory and administrative practice. While the Constitution guarantees the Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Article 21), including the right to a fair and speedy trial, systemic bureaucratic apathy and institutional failure completely denied him these rights, illustrating how vulnerable citizens suffer when constitutional safeguards are not actively enforced.

The Concept of a Bill of Rights

A Bill of Rights is a list of rights mentioned and protected by the constitution of a country. It serves two primary functions:

  • Prohibits the government from acting against the rights of individuals.
  • Ensures a legal remedy in case of any violation of these rights.

It protects individuals from threats posed by other individuals, private organizations, and the organs of the state itself (legislature, executive, bureaucracy, and judiciary).

2. Fundamental Rights in the Indian Constitution

During the Indian freedom struggle, leaders realized the critical importance of rights. In 1928 , the Motilal Nehru Committee demanded a bill of rights. Upon independence, these were enshrined in Part III of the Constitution as Fundamental Rights .

Ordinary Legal Rights vs. Fundamental Rights
Dimension Ordinary Legal Rights Fundamental Rights
Protection & Enforcement Protected and enforced by ordinary law of the land. Protected and guaranteed by the Constitution of the country.
Alteration / Amendment Can be changed or amended by the legislature through ordinary law-making processes. Can only be changed by amending the Constitution itself.
Government Action No specific constitutional bar, though subject to legal limits. No organ of the government can act in a manner that violates them; executive/legislative actions can be declared illegal by the judiciary.
The Six Fundamental Rights: Conceptual Matrix
Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Right to Equality Equality before law, prohibition of discrimination, equal opportunity in public employment, abolition of untouchability and titles. Articles 14–18, Indian Constitution Eradicates social hierarchies, ensures equal dignity, and enables marginalized sections to participate equally.
Right to Freedom Freedom of speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession; protection of life, liberty, and rights of accused. Articles 19–22, Indian Constitution Guarantees individual liberty, prevents arbitrary state action, and forms the bedrock of democratic expression.
Right against Exploitation Prohibition of forced labour (begar), human trafficking, and employment of children in hazardous industries. Articles 23–24, Indian Constitution Protects vulnerable citizens (poor, children) from systemic economic abuse and physical subjugation.
Right to Freedom of Religion Freedom of conscience, free profession, practice, propagation of religion; freedom to manage religious affairs. Articles 25–28, Indian Constitution Sustains the secular fabric of India, ensuring state neutrality and individual spiritual autonomy.
Cultural & Educational Rights Protection of language, script, and culture of minorities; right of minorities to establish educational institutions. Articles 29–30, Indian Constitution Preserves India's diverse pluralistic identity and prevents majoritarian cultural assimilation.
Right to Constitutional Remedies Right to approach High Courts or Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights via writs. Article 32, Indian Constitution Acts as the "heart and soul" of the Constitution by making all other rights justiciable and enforceable.
3. Deep-Dive into Individual Fundamental Rights
Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)
  • Equality before Law: Equal protection of laws for all individuals.
  • Prohibition of Discrimination: No discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Guarantees equal access to public places (shops, hotels, wells, bathing ghats).
  • Equality of Opportunity: Equal opportunity in public employment. Under Article 16(4) , the state is permitted to make provisions for the reservation of appointments or posts for backward classes.
  • Abolition of Untouchability: Abolishes the practice of untouchability, one of the crudest forms of social inequality.
  • Abolition of Titles: No titles can be conferred except for military or academic excellence.

Caste and Gender Discrimination Scenarios: (1) Swadesh Kumar's friend being served tea in an earthen cup because he is a Dalit. (2) Female newsreaders over 45 being barred from presenting news while male newsreaders of the same age are not.

Political Analysis: These scenarios illustrate direct violations of the Right to Equality . The first violates Article 15 (discrimination based on caste and denial of equal access), while the second violates Article 16 (gender-based discrimination in public employment).

Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)
  • Six Democratic Freedoms (Article 19): Freedom of speech and expression; peaceful assembly without arms; forming associations/unions; free movement throughout India; residing/settling in any part of India; practicing any profession, trade, or business. These are subject to reasonable restrictions (public order, morality, security of the state).
  • Right to Life and Personal Liberty (Article 21): No person can be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. Expanded by the Supreme Court to include the right to live with human dignity , shelter, and livelihood.
  • Preventive Detention: Arresting a person out of apprehension that they may engage in unlawful activity. Can be extended only for three months before review by an advisory board. Often criticized for potential executive misuse.
  • Rights of the Accused: Protection against double jeopardy (no person punished for the same offence more than once); protection against retroactive laws (no law can declare an action illegal from a backdate); protection against self-incrimination (no person asked to give evidence against themselves).
Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)
  • Prohibition of traffic in human beings and begar (forced labour without payment).
  • Prohibition of employment of children below the age of 14 in dangerous jobs like factories and mines.
Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)
  • Freedom of Conscience: Right to freely profess, practice, and propagate any religion, or choose not to follow any.
  • Limitations: Subject to public order, morality, and health. The state can interfere to root out social evils (e.g., banning sati, bigamy, human sacrifice).
  • Secularism: India has no official state religion. State-run institutions cannot preach religion or discriminate in employment based on faith.
Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)
  • Protects the language, script, and culture of linguistic and religious minorities.
  • Guarantees the right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice, with non-discriminatory state aid.
Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

Considered by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as the heart and soul of the constitution . It empowers citizens to approach the Supreme Court or High Courts to restore violated Fundamental Rights through the issuance of writs .

Writ Literal Meaning / Definition When Issued / Target Legal Effect / Remedy
Habeas Corpus "To have the body of" Issued to an arresting authority (state or private individual). Orders the arrested person to be presented before the court; sets them free if the arrest is unlawful.
Mandamus "We command" Issued to a public official or office holder. Directs the official to perform a legal duty they have failed or refused to perform, which infringes on a citizen's rights.
Prohibition "To forbid" Issued by a higher court (Supreme/High Court) to a lower court. Prevents the lower court from proceeding with a case that exceeds its legal jurisdiction.
Quo Warranto "By what authority?" Issued to a person holding a public office. Inquires into the legality of the person's claim to the office; restricts them from acting if they are not legally entitled.
Certiorari "To be certified" Issued by a higher court to a lower court or administrative authority. Orders the transfer of a pending matter or quashes an order already passed by the lower authority.
4. Institutional Safeguards: National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)

Established in 1993 to protect fundamental and human rights, particularly for the poor and deprived sections.

  • Composition: Comprises a former Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, a former Supreme Court Judge, a former Chief Justice of a High Court, and two members with practical experience in human rights.
  • Functions: Inquires into human rights violations (either on petition or suo motu); visits jails to study inmate conditions; promotes research in human rights.
  • Limitations: Lacks the power of prosecution; can only make recommendations to the government or courts.
5. Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) & Fundamental Duties

The makers of the Constitution incorporated non-justiciable guidelines in Part IV to ensure socio-economic justice without legally binding future governments.

DPSP Categories and Core Elements
Category Core Elements / Provisions Purpose / Objective
Goals Welfare of the people; Social, economic, and political justice; Raising standard of living; Equitable distribution of resources; Promotion of international peace. To establish a democratic socialist welfare state and guide national development.
Non-justiciable Rights Adequate livelihood; Equal pay for equal work for men and women; Right against economic exploitation; Right to work; Early childhood care and education for children below six years. To secure socio-economic entitlements for citizens that the state must strive to fulfill as resources permit.
Policies Uniform Civil Code; Prohibition of consumption of alcoholic liquor; Promotion of cottage industries; Prevention of slaughter of useful cattle; Promotion of village panchayats. To provide concrete policy directions for legislative and executive action.
Fundamental Duties of Citizens

Inserted by the 42nd Amendment in 1976 , listing ten (now eleven) duties. These are non-enforceable moral obligations (e.g., abiding by the Constitution, protecting the environment, promoting harmony) and do not restrict the enjoyment of Fundamental Rights.

6. Relationship and Conflicts: FRs vs. DPSPs

While Fundamental Rights protect individual liberties (restraining government action), DPSPs ensure societal well-being (exhorting government action). This difference has historically led to major constitutional conflicts.

The Right to Property Controversy

Originally, the right to 'acquire, possess, and maintain' property was a Fundamental Right. When the government enacted land reform and zamindari abolition laws to implement DPSPs, they were challenged for violating the Right to Property. In 1973 , the Supreme Court ruled that property was not part of the basic structure. Finally, the 44th Amendment (1978) removed it from the list of Fundamental Rights, converting it into a simple legal right under Article 300A .

Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973) & Basic Structure

The conflict over whether Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights to implement DPSPs was settled in the landmark Kesavananda Bharati case . The Supreme Court ruled that while Parliament can amend the Constitution, it cannot alter its basic structure , which includes core features like democracy, secularism, and fundamental liberties.

7. Thinker Profiles & Historical Perspectives

Profile: Somnath Lahiri (1901–1984)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the Communist Party of India, Member of the Constituent Assembly.
  • Ideological Contributions: Critiqued the draft Fundamental Rights for being overly restricted by provisos, arguing they were framed from the perspective of a police constable rather than a free citizen.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Advocated for absolute, uninhibited rights for citizens and warned against state overreach through constitutional exceptions.

Profile: Sardar Hukam Singh (1895–1983)

  • Role & Affiliation: Member of the Constituent Assembly, later Speaker of the Lok Sabha (1962–1967) and Governor of Rajasthan.
  • Ideological Contributions: Strongly advocated for minority safeguards, emphasizing that the majority community must act to make minorities feel secure in a secular state.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Highlighted that minority demands for linguistic, cultural, and administrative safeguards stem from genuine fears of majoritarian dominance.

Profile: Jotirao Govindrao Phule (1827–1890)

  • Role & Affiliation: Radical social reformer, writer, and activist from Maharashtra; founder of the Satyashodhak Samaj.
  • Ideological Contributions: Articulated early visions of rights that intrinsically linked freedom with social equality, challenging caste hierarchies and gender oppression.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Championed education for lower castes and women, laying the intellectual foundation for modern Indian constitutional rights.
