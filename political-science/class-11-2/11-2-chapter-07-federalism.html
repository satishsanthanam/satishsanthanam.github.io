Political Science: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 7: Federalism

Introduction & Conceptual Foundations of Federalism

Federalism is not a fixed set of dogmatic principles but a dynamic framework of governance that evolves to accommodate regional diversities, historical realities, and political negotiations. The geographical and cultural landscape of India has undergone massive transformations since 1947, reflecting the active functioning of its federal structure.

What is Federalism?

Federalism is an institutional mechanism designed to accommodate two sets of polities: one at the regional level and the other at the national level. It operates on the principle of shared rule and self-rule, ensuring unity without eliminating regional identities.

  • Dual Polity : Existence of two autonomous spheres of government (national/union and regional/state), each sovereign within its constitutionally demarcated domain.
  • Dual Identity : Citizens hold overlapping loyalties to both their specific region (e.g., Gujarati, Tamilian) and the nation as a whole (Indian).
  • Written Constitution : A supreme, written document that acts as the source of power for both tiers of government, preventing unilateral alterations of the federal balance.
  • Independent Judiciary : An impartial judicial system with the power to resolve legal disputes and jurisdictional conflicts between the centre and the states.
Conceptual Definition & Characterization Matrix
Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Federalism A system of governance distributing power between a central authority and constituent regional units. Written Constitution, Supreme Court interpretation. To accommodate socio-cultural diversity, prevent central tyranny, and foster local self-governance.
Dual Citizenship The constitutional entitlement of holding citizenship of both a constituent state and the federal nation. Federal constitutions (e.g., USA). Note: India rejects this in favor of single citizenship. To reinforce regional political identity alongside national allegiance.
Constitutional Supremacy The principle that the constitution is the supreme law of the land, binding all legislative and executive organs. The sovereign will of the people via the Constituent Assembly. To protect the division of powers from arbitrary encroachment by any single level of government.
Comparative Case Studies: Global Federal Failures & Challenges

The Dissolution of the USSR, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia

The collapse of the Soviet Union (USSR) post-1989, alongside the divisions of Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, highlights that merely adopting a federal constitution is insufficient to guarantee national integrity. Excessive centralization, the concentration of power in a dominant region (such as Russia in the USSR), and the suppression of distinct regional languages and cultures led to systemic alienation and eventual disintegration.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Highly centralized federations that fail to cultivate a culture of trust, mutual respect, and democratic accommodation are highly susceptible to secessionist movements and civil conflict. True federalism requires both constitutional design and democratic practice.

Federalism in the West Indies

Formed in 1958 under British colonial influence, the Federation of the West Indies collapsed in 1962 due to a weak central government, independent provincial economies, and intense political competition. In 1973, the Treaty of Chiguaramas established joint authorities (common legislature, supreme court, currency, and market) under the Caribbean Community, illustrating a unique model where units can neither live fully integrated nor completely apart.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: A federation cannot survive without a minimum threshold of shared economic interest and central coordinating authority. Decentralization without political cohesion leads to structural dissolution.

Federalism in Nigeria

Originally divided into Northern and Southern British colonies, Nigeria adopted a federal constitution in 1950. The three major ethnic groups—Yoruba (West), Ibo (East), and Hausa-Fulani (North)—struggled for regional dominance, leading to military coups, centralized military regimes, and severe conflict. Despite the restoration of democracy in 1999, disputes over the control of oil revenues and religious differences continue to threaten the federation.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: When ethnic, religious, and economic cleavages overlap directly with regional boundaries, federal systems face extreme strain. Resource distribution (like oil revenues) requires transparent, non-partisan institutional mechanisms to prevent regional alienation.

Federalism in the Indian Constitution

The Indian model of federalism was forged in the crucible of partition, massive regional diversity, and the urgent need for socio-economic development. The framers of the Constitution designed a system that balances regional aspirations with national integrity.

Historical Context & Article 1

Before Independence, the national movement recognized the necessity of dividing powers to govern a vast country. While the Muslim League agitated for maximum regional autonomy to protect minority interests, the trauma of Partition led the Constituent Assembly to prioritize national unity and cooperation over extreme decentralization.

  • Article 1(1) : "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States ."
  • The word "federation" is deliberately omitted from the \text of the Constitution, emphasizing that the Indian Union is not the result of an agreement by the states, and no state has the right to secede from it.
The Constitutional Division of Powers

The Constitution of India clearly demarcates the legislative and executive jurisdictions of the Union and State governments under the Seventh Schedule, categorizing subjects into three distinct lists, alongside residuary powers.

List Type Jurisdiction & Legislative Authority Key Subjects Included
Union List Union Parliament has exclusive power to legislate on these matters of national importance. Defence, Atomic Energy, Foreign Affairs, War and Peace, Banking, Railways, Post and Telegraph, Airways, Ports, Foreign Trade, Currency & Coinage.
State List State Legislatures normally have exclusive power to legislate on these matters of local/regional importance. Agriculture, Police, Prison, Local Government, Public Health, Land, Liquor, Trade and Commerce, Livestock and Animal Husbandry, State Public Services.
Concurrent List Both Union Parliament and State Legislatures can make laws. Union law prevails in case of conflict. Education, Transfer of Property (other than agricultural land), Forests, Trade Unions, Adulteration, Adoption and Succession.
Residuary Powers Union Legislature alone has the power to legislate on matters not mentioned in any of the three lists. Cyber Laws, Information Technology, and modern digital infrastructure.
Federalism with a Strong Central Government

The Indian Constitution exhibits a pronounced unitary bias, vesting overwhelming structural, financial, and administrative powers in the central government to prevent balkanization and drive planned economic development.

Rationales for a Strong Centre
  1. Continental Dimensions and Diversity : The vast size and deep socio-cultural divisions required a strong central authority to maintain law and order and foster national integration.
  2. Integration of Princely States : At independence, over 500 princely states had to be integrated into the Indian Union, requiring decisive central intervention.
  3. Socio-Economic Planning : Eradicating poverty, illiteracy, and deep-seated inequalities required coordinated national planning, leading to the creation of the centralized Planning Commission.
Constitutional Provisions Favoring the Union
  • Territorial Integrity : Under Article 3, Parliament can unilaterally alter state boundaries, create new states, or change a state's name without its consent. The existence of a state is not constitutionally guaranteed.
  • Emergency Provisions : Part XVIII of the Constitution allows the central government to convert the federal polity into a highly centralized unitary system during national, state, or financial emergencies.
  • Financial Dominance : Revenue-generating sources are heavily concentrated with the Centre. States are structurally dependent on central grants-in-aid and discretionary financial allocations.
  • The Office of the Governor : The Governor, appointed by the President, acts as an agent of the Centre and has the power to reserve state bills for the President's assent, delaying or vetoing state legislation.
  • Article 257(1) : The executive power of every State must be exercised so as not to impede the executive power of the Union, allowing the Centre to issue binding directions to states.
  • All-India Services : An integrated administrative system where officers of the IAS and IPS are recruited and controlled by the central government but serve in key state administrative posts.
  • Articles 33 and 34 : Authorize Parliament to protect military and civil personnel during martial law, providing the constitutional basis for centralized security acts like the Armed Forces Special Powers Act (AFSPA).
Constituent Assembly Perspective

Profile: T.T. Krishnamachari (1899–1978)

  • Role & Affiliation: Member of the Drafting Committee of the Constitution; later served as Union Finance Minister.
  • Ideological Contributions: Argued that modern constitutional evolution globally drifts toward centralization due to the transition of states from "Police States" to "Welfare States."
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Emphasized that the ultimate responsibility for the economic well-being of the entire country must rest as the paramount responsibility of the central government.
Conflicts and Dynamics in India's Federal System

The actual working of Indian federalism is shaped by political processes, party dynamics, and shifting power relations between the Centre and the States.

Evolutionary Phases of Centre-State Relations
  1. Phase I: Congress Dominance (1950s – mid-1960s) : Characterized by single-party dominance at both the Centre and in most states. Relations were relatively smooth, resolved through intra-party negotiations under Jawaharlal Nehru's leadership.
  2. Phase II: Rise of Opposition & Conflict (mid-1960s – 1980s) : Congress dominance declined; opposition parties formed governments in several states. This led to intense political friction, protests against central interference, and demands for greater state autonomy.
  3. Phase III: Coalition Era (1990s – Present) : The emergence of regional parties as key players in national coalition governments at the Centre. This led to a more mature, cooperative federalism, respecting diversity and giving states a greater say in national policy.
Four Dimensions of Demands for Autonomy
  • Political/Constitutional Autonomy : Demands to alter the division of powers in favor of the states, transferring more subjects from the Union and Concurrent lists to the State list.
  • Financial Autonomy : Demands for independent sources of revenue and greater control over resources to reduce dependence on central grants (e.g., the 1977 West Bengal Left Front memorandum).
  • Administrative Autonomy : Resentment against the central government's control over the administrative machinery, particularly the All-India Services (IAS/IPS).
  • Cultural and Linguistic Autonomy : Resistance to the perceived imposition of Hindi (e.g., anti-Hindi agitations in Tamil Nadu during the 1960s) and demands for the promotion of regional languages and cultures.
The Controversy of Article 356 (President's Rule)

Article 356 allows the central government to dismiss an elected state government and impose President's Rule if the constitutional machinery in the state breaks down. This has been one of the most abused provisions of the Constitution.

  • Pre-1967 : Used sparingly.
  • Post-1967 : Used extensively by the Centre to dismiss state governments ruled by opposition parties (e.g., Kerala in 1959, and multiple states in the 1970s and 1980s).
  • Sarkaria Commission (1983/1988) : Recommended that Article 356 should be used only as a measure of last resort and that the office of the Governor must be strictly non-partisan.
  • Judicial Intervention : The Supreme Court ruled that the constitutional validity of imposing President's Rule is subject to judicial review, significantly curbing its arbitrary use.
Territorial Reorganisation & Interstate Disputes

Indian federalism must manage not only conflicts between the Centre and states but also deep-seated disputes between different states over territory, resources, and identity.

Demands for New States

The Indian national movement promised the creation of states based on linguistic and cultural identity. This led to a continuous process of territorial reorganisation post-independence.

  • States Reorganisation Commission (SRC) 1953 : Recommended the creation of linguistic states, leading to major reorganisation in 1956.
  • Key Milestones :
    • 1960: Bombay bifurcated into Gujarat and Maharashtra.
    • 1966: Punjab bifurcated into Punjab and Haryana.
    • North-East Reorganisation: Creation of Manipur, Tripura, Meghalaya, Mizoram, and Arunachal Pradesh.
    • 2000: Creation of Chhattisgarh (from MP), Uttarakhand (from UP), and Jharkhand (from Bihar) for administrative efficiency and regional aspirations.
    • 2014: Telangana carved out of Andhra Pradesh.
Interstate Conflicts

Interstate disputes are highly emotional and politically charged, requiring judicial arbitration and political negotiation.

Dispute Category Key Examples & Locations Underlying Issues & Dynamics
Border Disputes Belgaum (Maharashtra vs. Karnataka); Chandigarh (Punjab vs. Haryana); Manipur vs. Nagaland. Claims over territories based on linguistic majorities in border areas. Chandigarh remains a shared capital despite agreements to transfer it to Punjab.
River Water Disputes Cauvery/Kaveri Water Dispute (Tamil Nadu vs. Karnataka); Narmada River Dispute (Gujarat, MP, and Maharashtra). Crucial for drinking water and agricultural irrigation. Disputes persist despite the establishment of specialized water tribunals and Supreme Court rulings.
Asymmetrical Federalism & Special Provisions

To accommodate unique historical, cultural, and social circumstances, the Indian Constitution incorporates elements of asymmetrical federalism, treating different states differently.

Asymmetrical Representation & Special Status
  • Rajya Sabha Representation : Unlike the US Senate, where all states have equal representation, seats in India's Rajya Sabha are allocated based on population, ensuring larger states have greater representation while guaranteeing minimum representation to smaller states.
  • North-Eastern States : Special provisions (Articles 371 to 371-I) exist for states like Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh, and Mizoram to protect the land rights, culture, and customary laws of indigenous tribal populations.
  • Hilly and Other States : Special provisions also apply to hilly states like Himachal Pradesh and others like Andhra Pradesh, Goa, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Sikkim, and Telangana to address developmental imbalances.
The Case of Jammu and Kashmir (Article 370)

"According to Article 370, the concurrence of the State was required for making any laws in matters mentioned in the Union and Concurrent lists... J&K had a separate constitution and a flag..."

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Article 370 was a prime example of asymmetrical federalism, granting J&K greater autonomy due to the unique circumstances of its accession in 1947. However, this special status was revoked by the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, which bifurcated the state into two Union Territories: (i) Jammu and Kashmir, and (ii) Ladakh, bringing it fully in line with the standard constitutional framework of the Indian Union.
