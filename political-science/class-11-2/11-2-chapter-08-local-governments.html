Political Science: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 8: Local Governments

Introduction & Conceptual Foundations of Local Government

In a democracy, an elected government at the centre and state levels is insufficient. True democratic depth requires an elected government at the local level to manage local affairs, ensure meaningful participation, and establish purposeful accountability.

Conceptual Definition & Characterization Matrix
Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Local Government Government at the village and district level closest to the common people, dealing with day-to-day life and problems of ordinary citizens. 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts (1992), State Legislations. Ensures quick, cost-effective resolution of local issues using local knowledge and interest.
Democratic Decentralisation The devolution of political, administrative, and financial powers from central and state governments to local self-governing bodies. Gandhian philosophy, Article 40 of the Directive Principles of State Policy. Strengthens democratic processes by involving common citizens directly in decision-making and development.
Narrative Case Studies of Grassroots Empowerment

Geeta Rathore belongs to Jamonia Talab Gram Panchayat, Sehore district, Madhya Pradesh. Elected Sarpanch in 1995 from a reserved seat, she was re-elected in 2000 from a non-reserved seat. She harnessed the collective energy of her Panchayat to renovate water tanks, build a school, construct roads, fight domestic violence, and encourage afforestation and water management.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Demonstrates how constitutional reservations for women act as a catalyst for leadership transformation, shifting individuals from domestic roles to active political agents capable of executing comprehensive local development.

In 1997, the Tamil Nadu government allotted land within Vengaivasal Gram Panchayat to 71 government employees. The District Collector directed the Panchayat to endorse this. The Panchayat refused, asserting its autonomy. When the Collector ordered land acquisition, the Panchayat filed a writ petition. The Madras High Court Division Bench ruled in favor of the Panchayat, holding the state's unilateral order to be a gross violation of the constitutional status of Panchayats.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Highlights the legal and constitutional protection of local bodies against arbitrary state executive actions, establishing local governments as distinct, self-governing constitutional entities rather than mere administrative arms of the state.

Growth of Local Government in India

Self-governing village communities have historically existed in India in the form of assemblies, evolving over centuries into modern institutional frameworks.

Historical Timeline of Evolution
  1. Ancient & Medieval Era: Self-governing village communities existed as sabhas (assemblies) and Panchayats (assemblies of five persons) resolving local disputes and managing resources.
  2. Colonial Period (Post-1882): Lord Ripon (Viceroy of India) initiated the creation of elected local boards . Progress remained slow, prompting the Indian National Congress to demand effective local self-governance.
  3. Government of India Act 1919: Established village panchayats in several provinces, a trend that continued under the Government of India Act 1935 .
  4. Constituent Assembly Debates (1947-1949): The subject of local government was assigned to the States and placed under the non-justiciable Directive Principles of State Policy (Article 40) due to concerns over national unity and rural factionalism.
  5. Community Development Programme (1952): Launched to promote public participation in local development, leading to the recommendation of a three-tier Panchayati Raj system.
  6. P.K. Thungon Committee (1989): Recommended constitutional recognition for local bodies, periodic mandatory elections, and the constitutional allocation of funds.
Thinker & Biographical Profiles on Decentralisation

Profile: Mahatma Gandhi (1869-1948)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the Indian National Movement, philosopher of decentralised democracy.
  • Ideological Contributions: Strongly advocated for the decentralisation of economic and political power. Envisioned every village as a self-sustained republic where life is structured as a pyramid with the apex sustained by the bottom.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Championed Gram Swaraj (village self-rule) as the foundational basis of true Indian independence.

Profile: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (1891-1956)

  • Role & Affiliation: Chairman of the Constituent Assembly's Drafting Committee, champion of Dalit rights.
  • Ideological Contributions: Expressed deep skepticism toward localism and village-level self-government. Argued that the faction-ridden and caste-dominated nature of rural society would defeat the noble purpose of local governance, further oppressing marginalized communities.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Ensured individual rights, rather than the village, remained the primary unit of the Indian Constitution.
The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act (Rural Local Governments)

Passed by Parliament in 1992 and coming into force in 1993, the 73rd Amendment constitutionalized rural local governments, known as Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) .

Core Structural Provisions
  • Three-Tier Structure: Mandates a uniform three-tier system across all states:
    • Gram Panchayat: Base level, covering a single village or a group of villages.
    • Mandal Panchayat: Intermediary level (also called Block or Taluka Panchayat), not mandatory in smaller states with populations under 20 lakhs.
    • Zilla Panchayat: Apex level, covering the entire rural area of a district.
  • Gram Sabha: A mandatory body comprising all adult members registered as voters within the Panchayat area. Its role and functions are determined by state legislation.
  • Direct Elections: Members at all three levels are elected directly by the people for a fixed term of 5 years. If a Panchayat is dissolved early, fresh elections must be held within 6 months.
  • Mandatory Reservations:
    • One-third ($1/3$) of all seats are reserved for women.
    • Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes ( SC ) and Scheduled Tribes ( ST ) in proportion to their population.
    • Reservations apply to both ordinary members and the offices of Chairpersons ( Adhyakshas ) at all three levels.
    • States are permitted to extend reservations to Other Backward Classes ( OBCs ).
Transfer of Subjects & Eleventh Schedule

Under Article 243G , 29 subjects previously under the State List were identified and listed in the Eleventh Schedule to be transferred to Panchayati Raj Institutions.

Institutional Safeguards & Special Provisions
  • State Election Commissioner: An autonomous, independent officer appointed by the State government, responsible for conducting free, fair, and periodic elections to local bodies. This office is structurally independent of the Election Commission of India.
  • State Finance Commission: Appointed once every 5 years to examine the financial position of local governments, review revenue distribution between the State and local bodies, and ensure non-political allocation of funds.
  • PESA Act (1996): The provisions of the 73rd Amendment were initially not applicable to areas with significant Adivasi populations. In 1996, the Panchayat Extension to Scheduled Areas (PESA) Act was passed, protecting traditional customs, forest rights, and requiring Gram Sabha consent for local resource management.
The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act (Urban Local Governments)

The 74th Amendment constitutionalized urban local bodies, also known as Nagarpalikas (Municipal Corporations, Municipalities, and Nagar Panchayats).

Defining Urban Areas

The Census of India defines an urban area based on three strict criteria:

  • A minimum population of 5,000 .
  • At least 75% of the male working population engaged in non-agricultural occupations.
  • A population density of at least 400 persons per square kilometre .
Structural Alignment & Twelfth Schedule

The 74th Amendment is largely a replication of the 73rd Amendment adapted for urban areas:

  • Incorporates identical provisions for direct elections, reservations, State Election Commissions, and State Finance Commissions.
  • Mandates the transfer of urban functions listed in the Twelfth Schedule of the Constitution from State governments to urban local bodies.
Implementation, Achievements, and Structural Challenges

The implementation of the 73rd and 74th Amendments has fundamentally transformed the democratic landscape of India, creating the largest democratic exercise in the world.

Quantitative Achievements of Local Governance
  • Institutional Network: India currently has over 600 Zilla Panchayats , approximately 6,000 block/intermediary Panchayats , and over 2,40,000 Gram Panchayats in rural areas.
  • Urban Network: Includes over 100 Municipal Corporations , 1,400 Municipalities , and over 2,000 Nagar Panchayats .
  • Elected Representatives: More than 32 lakh members are elected to these bodies every 5 years, compared to fewer than 5,000 representatives in Parliament and State Assemblies combined.
  • Women's Representation: At least 13 lakh elected representatives are women , including over 200 Zilla Adhyakshas, 2,000 Block Presidents, and over 80,000 Gram Sarpanchas.
  • Social Diversity: Approximately 6.6 lakh elected members hail from SC and ST communities, aligning local governance with the demographic profile of the nation.
Critical Challenges & Structural Limitations
  • Limited Autonomy: Many States have failed to transfer significant powers and subjects to local bodies, leaving them with limited decision-making authority.
  • Financial Dependence: Local bodies have very little revenue-raising capacity of their own. While rural local bodies raise only 0.24% of total revenues, they account for 4% of total government expenditure, making them heavily dependent on state and central grants.
  • Social Tensions: The entry of marginalized groups and women into positions of power has challenged traditional power structures, occasionally leading to social conflict and resistance from dominant groups.
  • Proxy Representation: In some instances, women elected representatives have been treated as mere proxies by male family members, though this trend is steadily declining as women gain political experience.
Comparative Global Case Study: Bolivia

In 1994, Bolivia passed the Popular Participation Law, decentralising power to the local level. It divided the country into 314 municipal governments headed by popularly-elected mayors and municipal councils. Bolivian local governments were entrusted with building local health and education facilities. Crucially, 20% of nationwide tax collections are distributed among municipalities on a per capita basis, and municipalities are empowered to levy local taxes on motor vehicles and property.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Highlights how successful decentralisation requires not just political representation, but also guaranteed fiscal transfers and local tax-raising powers to ensure local bodies do not remain financially dependent on higher levels of government.
