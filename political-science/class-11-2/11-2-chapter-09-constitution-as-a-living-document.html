Political Science: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 9: Constitution as a Living Document

Introduction & The Static vs. Living Debate
Are Constitutions Static?

Constitutions are not frozen documents; they must adapt to changing societal needs, political upheavals, and evolving ideas.

  • Soviet Union : Adopted four different constitutions within 74 years (1918, 1924, 1936, 1977) before disintegrating in 1991, leading to the Russian Federation's 1993 constitution.
  • France : Experienced continuous constitutional experimentation, transitioning through five republics in two centuries (republics formed in 1793, 1848, 1875, 1946, and 1958).
  • India : Adopted on 26 November 1949 and implemented on 26 January 1950, the same framework has functioned for over seven decades. This durability stems from a robust design combining rigidity with flexibility.
The Living Document Philosophy

The Indian Constitution is considered a living document because it accepts the necessity of modifications according to the changing needs of society.

  • Flexibility of Interpretation : Both political practice and judicial rulings have shown maturity and flexibility in implementing the provisions.
  • Balancing Act : The framers placed the Constitution above ordinary law but recognized that future generations would require a framework that could respond to unforeseen challenges.
How to Amend the Constitution (Article 368)
Amending Procedures and Typologies

The Constitution of India balances rigidity and flexibility by providing three distinct pathways for amendment under Article 368 .

Amending Method Procedural Requirements Scope / Examples
Simple Majority Passed by a simple majority of members present and voting in both Houses of Parliament, similar to ordinary law. Creation of new states ( Article 2 ), altering state boundaries ( Article 3 ), citizenship provisions.
Special Majority (Article 368) Requires support of at least half of the total strength of each House, and two-thirds of those present and voting. No joint session allowed. Amendments to Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles of State Policy.
Special Majority + State Ratification Requires a special majority in both Houses of Parliament, plus ratification by the legislatures of at least half of the states. Federal structure, distribution of powers between Union and States, representation of states in Parliament.
The Concept of Special Majority

The requirement of a special majority ensures that amendment bills cannot be passed by a thin ruling majority without broad political consensus.

  • Double Condition : An amendment bill must satisfy both the total membership threshold (at least 273 votes in a 545-member Lok Sabha) and the active voting threshold (two-thirds of those present and voting).
  • Sovereignty of Elected Representatives : The amending power is vested solely in the elected legislatures; no outside agency or referendum is required.
Why Have There Been So Many Amendments?
Chronological Trends and Political Context

As of January 2024, the Indian Constitution has been amended 106 \times. The frequency of amendments is not merely a product of political majorities but reflects shifting socio-political realities.

  • 1970-1990 Period : Witnessed a high concentration of amendments, driven by intense political competition and institutional clashes.
  • 2001-2003 Period : Despite being a phase of coalition politics, ten amendments were passed, demonstrating that consensus-driven amendments are common even without single-party dominance.
Classification of Amendments

Amendments made to the Indian Constitution can be broadly classified into four functional categories:

  1. Technical or Administrative Amendments : Clarifications and minor modifications that do not alter the basic provisions (e.g., raising the retirement age of High Court judges from 60 to 62 via the 15th amendment; extending SC/ST reservations in legislatures every ten years).
  2. Differing Interpretations : Amendments enacted by Parliament to override judicial interpretations and assert legislative intent (e.g., clashes over the right to property and fundamental rights between 1970 and 1975).
  3. Political Consensus : Amendments passed with unanimous or near-unanimous support to reflect evolving political philosophies (e.g., anti-defection laws via the 52nd and 91st amendments; lowering the voting age from 21 to 18 via the 61st amendment; local self-government via the 73rd and 74th amendments).
  4. Controversial Amendments : Amendments passed during periods of political crisis, particularly the 38th, 39th, and 42nd amendments enacted during the 1975 Emergency, which sought to alter the basic balance of power. These were largely reversed by the 43rd and 44th amendments in 1977 to restore constitutional equilibrium.
The Basic Structure Doctrine and Judicial Evolution
The Kesavananda Bharati Landmark (1973)

The theory of the basic structure of the Constitution has been the most influential development in India's constitutional history.

  • Judicial Limitation : The Supreme Court ruled that while Parliament has the power to amend any part of the Constitution, it cannot alter or destroy its core, foundational features.
  • Judiciary as Final Arbiter : The ruling positioned the judiciary as the final authority in deciding whether an amendment violates the basic structure.
  • Informal Amendment : This doctrine evolved entirely through judicial interpretation rather than formal legislative \text, illustrating how the Constitution grows dynamically.
Review of the Constitution and Other Judicial Interpretations

The basic structure doctrine has successfully withstood political challenges and guided subsequent constitutional reviews.

  • Venkatachaliah Commission (2000) : A commission appointed to review the working of the Constitution upheld the basic structure doctrine and avoided recommending any measures that would compromise it.
  • Other Judicial Milestones : The Supreme Court has shaped policy by capping reservations at 50%, introducing the creamy layer concept for backward classes, and expanding the scope of the right to life, liberty, and education.

Thinker & Biographical Profiles

Profile: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (1891-1956)

  • Role & Affiliation: Chairman of the Drafting Committee of the Indian Constitution; first Law Minister of independent India.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed social justice, annihilation of caste, and constitutional morality. Argued that the constitution must strike a balance between stability and adaptability.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Envisioned the amendment process as a mechanism to reflect public opinion and prevent the constitution from becoming an unalterable, sacred dogma that stifles future generations.
Narrative Case Studies & Historical Excerpts

"The ideals on which this draft constitution is framed have no manifest relation to the fundamental spirit of India. ...this Constitution ...would not prove suitable and would break down soon after being brought into operation."
— Lakshminarayan Sahu, Constituent Assembly Debates, 17 November 1949

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This excerpt highlights the skepticism present during the drafting phase regarding the suitability of a Western-style constitutional framework for India. However, the survival and adaptation of the Constitution over seven decades disprove these early anxieties, demonstrating that the document's flexibility and the maturity of India's political leadership successfully localized and sustained democratic governance.

"If those who are dissatisfied with the Constitution have only to obtain a 2/3 majority and if they cannot obtain even (that)..., their dissatisfaction with the Constitution cannot be deemed to be shared by the general public."
— Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, 25 November 1949

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Dr. Ambedkar's defense of the amendment procedure emphasizes that constitutional change must be backed by genuine public consensus. By requiring a two-thirds majority, the framers ensured that amendments represent the collective will of the nation rather than the transient interests of a single ruling party.
