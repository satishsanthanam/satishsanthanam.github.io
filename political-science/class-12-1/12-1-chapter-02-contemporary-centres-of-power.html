Political Science: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 2: Contemporary Centres of Power

Overview: Alternative Centres of Power

After the end of the bipolar structure of world politics in the early 1990s, alternative centres of political and economic power emerged to limit America's dominance. Key regional forces include the European Union (EU) in Europe and the Association of South East Asian Nations (ASEAN) in Asia. Additionally, the rapid economic rise of China has dramatically impacted global politics.

The European Union (EU)
Historical Evolution & Integration

The devastation of the Second World War shattered the traditional assumptions and structures on which European states had based their relations, prompting leaders to grapple with the 'Question of Europe'. Integration was accelerated by the Cold War and supported by US financial aid under the Marshall Plan .

  • 1948: Establishment of the Organisation for European Economic Cooperation (OEEC) to channel US aid and foster trade cooperation.
  • 1949: Creation of the Council of Europe , marking a step forward in political cooperation.
  • 1957: Signing of the Treaties of Rome, establishing the European Economic Community (EEC) and the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom) .
  • 1992: Signing of the Treaty of Maastricht , formally establishing the European Union (EU) and laying the foundation for a common foreign, security, and currency policy.

The EU has evolved from an economic union into an increasingly political entity, adopting state-like symbols including its own flag, anthem, founding date, and currency ( Euro ).

Dimensions of EU Influence
  • Economic Influence: The EU's GDP is projected to be approximately $19.35 trillion in 2024. Its currency, the Euro, poses a potential threat to the dominance of the US dollar. The EU's share of world trade is larger than that of the US, giving it significant leverage in trade disputes.
  • Political & Diplomatic Influence: One EU member, France, holds a permanent seat on the UN Security Council, and several others serve as non-permanent members. This allows the EU to influence global policies, such as negotiations on Iran's nuclear programme. The EU prioritizes diplomacy and economic engagement over military coercion.
  • Military Influence: The EU's combined armed forces are the second largest in the world, and its defense spending ranks second only to the US. France possesses a nuclear arsenal of approximately 335 warheads. The EU is also a leading global source of space and communications technology.
Challenges and Internal Friction

Despite high integration, the EU faces significant internal challenges. Member states retain their own foreign relations and defense policies, which are often at odds (e.g., Britain's support of the US-led Iraq invasion versus opposition from France and Germany). Additionally, deep-seated Euro-skepticism exists in parts of Europe, leading to resistance against treaties like Maastricht and the adoption of the Euro (notably by Denmark and Sweden).

The Titanic Cartoon (2003): The cartoon depicts the EU as the Titanic, representing the failure of the initiative to draft a common constitution.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: The cartoon highlights the limits of supranationalism. When the EU attempted to transition from an economic bloc to a unified political entity with a single constitution, national sovereignty concerns and diverse domestic interests caused the initiative to collapse, mirroring the unexpected vulnerability of the Titanic.

Association of South East Asian Nations (ASEAN)
Origin and Institutional Design

Before and during the Second World War, Southeast Asia suffered from repeated colonialisms and economic devastation. Post-war attempts at unity, such as the Bandung Conference and the Non-Aligned Movement, were ineffective at establishing informal cooperation. Consequently, Southeast Asian nations sought a regional alternative.

  • Establishment: Founded in 1967 by five countries—Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand—by signing the Bangkok Declaration .
  • Subsequent Members: Brunei Darussalam, Vietnam, Lao PDR, Myanmar (Burma), and Cambodia joined later, bringing total membership to ten.
  • The ASEAN Way: A unique form of interaction characterized by informal, non-confrontational, and cooperative dialogue that respects national sovereignty.
The Three Pillars of the ASEAN Community

In 2003, ASEAN agreed to establish an ASEAN Community comprising three distinct pillars:

  1. ASEAN Security Community: Based on the conviction that territorial disputes should not escalate into armed conflict. It led to the establishment of the ASEAN Regional Forum (ARF) in 1994 to coordinate security and foreign policy.
  2. ASEAN Economic Community: Aims to create a common market and production base within ASEAN states, resolve economic disputes through an improved Dispute Settlement Mechanism, and establish a Free Trade Area (FTA) for investment, labour, and services.
  3. ASEAN Socio-Cultural Community: Focuses on social progress, cultural development, and regional integration.
Vision 2020 & India-ASEAN Relations

ASEAN's Vision 2020 defines an outward-looking role in the international community, emphasizing negotiation over conflict. ASEAN successfully mediated the end of the Cambodian conflict and the East Timor crisis.

India's foreign policy historically neglected ASEAN during the Cold War. However, India's Look East Policy (initiated in the early 1990s) and Act East Policy (since 2014) have significantly increased economic and strategic interaction, leading to bilateral trade agreements with Malaysia, Singapore, and Thailand, and the ASEAN-India FTA which came into effect in 2010.

The Rise of the Chinese Economy
The Soviet Model and Its Limits (1949–1970s)

Following the communist revolution of 1949 under the leadership of Mao Zedong , China adopted a Soviet-style command economy. It severed links with the capitalist world and relied on domestic resources and limited Soviet aid.

  • Strategy: Create a state-owned heavy industries sector using capital accumulated from agriculture.
  • Achievements: Established foundations of an industrial economy, provided employment and social welfare to all citizens, and improved education and healthcare.
  • Failures: Industrial growth was offset by a 2-3% annual population growth, leaving agricultural production insufficient to generate a surplus. Per capita income remained very low, and international trade was minimal.
Economic Transition and the "Open Door" Policy

The Chinese leadership took major policy decisions in the 1970s to end political and economic isolation:

  • 1972: Established diplomatic relations with the United States.
  • 1973: Premier Zhou Enlai proposed the Four Modernisations (agriculture, industry, science and technology, and military).
  • 1978: Deng Xiaoping announced the Open Door Policy and economic reforms to generate higher productivity through foreign capital and technology.

Unlike the Soviet Union's "shock therapy", China opened its economy step-by-step:

  1. Privatisation of agriculture in 1982, leading to a dramatic rise in rural incomes and savings.
  2. Privatisation of industry in 1998.
  3. Establishment of Special Economic Zones (SEZs) where trade barriers were eliminated to attract foreign investors.
  4. Accession to the World Trade Organisation (WTO) in 2001.

Profile: Deng Xiaoping (1904–1997)

  • Role & Affiliation: Paramount Leader of the People's Republic of China; leader of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP).
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed pragmatic economic reforms over ideological purity ("Socialism with Chinese Characteristics").
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Introduced the "Open Door" policy in 1978, initiated the step-by-step marketization of the Chinese economy, and established Special Economic Zones (SEZs).
Current Status and Challenges

China has become the fastest-growing economy and is projected to overtake the US as the world's largest economy by 2040. It is a prime destination for Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) and holds massive foreign exchange reserves. However, the benefits of reform have not reached everyone:

  • Unemployment has risen, with nearly 100 million people seeking jobs.
  • Female employment and working conditions have deteriorated.
  • Environmental degradation and corruption have increased.
  • Economic inequality between rural-urban areas and coastal-inland provinces has widened.
India-China Relations
Historical Context and the 1962 Conflict

Historically, India and China had limited political interaction despite co-existing as great powers in Asia. After independence, the popular slogan 'Hindi-Chini bhai-bhai' generated hope for cooperative leadership in Asia. However, relations deteriorated due to:

  • The Chinese takeover of Tibet in 1950.
  • Border disputes over competing territorial claims in Arunachal Pradesh (NEFA) and the Aksai Chin region of Ladakh.
  • The 1962 Sino-Indian War , which resulted in military reverses for India and a long-term freeze in diplomatic relations until 1976.
Rapprochement and Modern Challenges

Relations began to improve slowly after changes in China's political leadership in the late 1970s. Border talks were initiated in 1981, and Rajiv Gandhi's visit in December 1988 provided a major breakthrough.

  • Economic Cooperation: Bilateral trade grew from $338 million in 1992 to over $84 billion in 2017. Both nations have agreed to cooperate on energy deals abroad and coordinate policies at the WTO.
  • Friction Points: India's 1998 nuclear tests were justified partly by a threat from China. China's military assistance to Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Myanmar, the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), and China's stance on terrorism in the UN remain major sources of tension.
Other Emerging Centres of Power
Japan

Despite having few natural resources, Japan rapidly rebuilt its economy after WWII. It became an OECD member in 1964 and was the third-largest economy in the world in 2017. It is the only Asian member of the G-7 and the second-largest contributor to the regular UN budget. Under Article 9 of its Constitution, the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right.

South Korea

Divided along the 38th Parallel after WWII, South Korea emerged as a major economic power between the 1960s and 1980s, a phenomenon known as the "Miracle on the Han River" . It joined the OECD in 1996 and ranked 18th in the Human Development Report 2016. Major brands like Samsung, LG, and Hyundai have established strong commercial ties with India.
