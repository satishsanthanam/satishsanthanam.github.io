Political Science: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 5: Security in the Contemporary World

Overview & Conceptual Foundations of Security

In world politics, the term security is frequently invoked to debate national survival, policy priorities, and international stability. Historically, the concept has been interpreted narrowly, focusing primarily on nation-states rather than ordinary citizens. However, contemporary political theory recognizes that security is a dynamic, multi-dimensional concept that varies across local contexts and historical situations.

Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Traditional Security (External) Protection of state sovereignty, independence, and territorial integrity from external military threats. State sovereignty and international law. Safeguards the physical boundaries and political independence of the democratic state.
Traditional Security (Internal) Maintenance of domestic peace, public order, and territorial integrity against internal rebellions or secessionist movements. National constitution and domestic law enforcement. Ensures stable governance and protects citizens from internal violence and civil war.
Human Security (Narrow) Protection of individuals and communities from violent threats, civil war, and state-sponsored atrocities. Universal human rights and humanitarian law. Protects the basic right to life and physical safety of ordinary citizens.
Human Security (Broad) Protection of individuals from non-violent existential threats including hunger, disease, natural disasters, and economic deprivation. Human development paradigms (e.g., UNDP 1994). Promotes human dignity, social justice, and the realization of individual potential.
Global Security Collective international response to trans-border existential threats like global warming, international terrorism, and pandemics. International treaties, global cooperation, and multilateral organizations. Preserves the global commons and ensures the survival of humankind across national borders.

"The concept of security has for too long been interpreted narrowly... It has been more related to nation states than people... Forgotten were the legitimate concerns of ordinary people who sought security in their daily lives."

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This excerpt from the 1994 UNDP Human Development Report marks a paradigm shift in global political science, transitioning the analytical focus of security from state-centric military defense to individual-centric human development and well-being.

Traditional Notions of Security: External Threats & State Responses
The Nature of External Threats
  • Military Aggression: The greatest danger to a country's security comes from another nation-state threatening military action.
  • Core Values at Risk: External military threats directly endanger a state's sovereignty , independence , and territorial integrity .
  • Targeting Civilians: Modern warfare does not spare ordinary citizens; military actions often deliberately target civilians to break their domestic support for the war.
State Responses to War

When confronted with the threat of war, a government has three basic choices:

  1. Surrender: To yield to the attacking country's demands (though governments rarely advertise this as an active policy).
  2. Deterrence : To prevent the other side from attacking by promising to raise the costs of war to an unacceptable, prohibitive level.
  3. Defence : To defend itself when war actually breaks out, denying the attacking country its objectives and defeating the invading forces.
Balance of Power & Alliance Building
  • Balance of Power : A critical component of traditional security policy. Countries closely monitor the relative strength of neighboring states. To maintain a favorable balance, states build up their own military, economic, and technological capabilities.
  • Alliance Building : A coalition of states that coordinate their actions to deter or defend against military attack. Most alliances are formalized in written treaties and are based on shared national interests.
  • Dynamic Alliances: Alliances change when national interests change. For example, the US backed Islamic militants in Afghanistan against the Soviet Union in the 1980s, but later launched military campaigns against them after the Al Qaeda terrorist strikes on 11 September 2001.
  • Anarchy in World Politics: Unlike domestic systems where a central authority (the government) regulates violence, the international system lacks a central governing body. The United Nations is a creature of its member states and possesses authority only to the extent allowed by its members.
Traditional Notions of Security: Internal Threats & Historical Context
The Post-World War II Context
  • Western Security Focus: After 1945, the most powerful countries (the US and the Soviet Union) faced relatively stable internal conditions. Consequently, they focused almost exclusively on external threats arising from the Cold War.
  • Colonial Wars: European powers continued to face violent resistance in their colonies from people seeking independence (e.g., the French in Vietnam, the British in Kenya).
Security Challenges of Newly Independent States

The newly independent countries of Asia and Africa faced security challenges fundamentally different from those of Europe in two major ways:

  1. External Border Disputes: New states faced immediate military conflicts with neighbors over borders, territories, and populations. Many feared their neighbors more than the former colonial powers.
  2. Internal Secessionist Movements: These states faced severe threats from internal separatist movements wanting to form independent countries.
  3. Merged Threats: External and internal threats frequently merged, as hostile neighbors often instigated or supported internal separatist rebellions.
  4. Civil War Statistics: Between 1946 and 1991, there was a twelve-fold rise in the number of civil wars. Internal wars now make up more than 95% of all armed conflicts fought globally.
Traditional Security and Cooperation
Limiting the Violence of War
  • Just War Theory: Traditional security recognizes that cooperation is possible to limit the ends and means of war. Countries should only go to war for legitimate reasons, such as self-defence or protection against genocide.
  • Proportionality and Discrimination: Armies must avoid killing or hurting non-combatants, as well as unarmed or surrendering soldiers. Force must be used only as a last resort after all diplomatic alternatives have failed.
Disarmament, Arms Control, and Confidence Building
Treaty / Convention Year Core Objective / Regulation Key Signatories / Scope
Biological Weapons Convention (BWC) 1972 Banned the production, development, and possession of biological weapons. Acceded to by more than 155 states, including all major global powers.
Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC) 1997 Banned the production, stockpiling, and use of chemical weapons. Acceded to by 193 states, establishing a near-universal ban.
Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty 1972 Stopped the US and USSR from deploying nationwide ballistic missile defense shields. Bilateral treaty between the United States and the Soviet Union.
Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) 1968 Regulated the acquisition of nuclear weapons; allowed only pre-1967 nuclear states to retain them. Multilateral treaty; criticized as highly discriminatory by non-nuclear states.
  • Confidence Building Measures (CBMs) : A process where rival countries share ideas, military information, and plans on a regular basis. This demonstrates that they are not planning surprise attacks, reducing the risk of war through misperception.
Non-Traditional Notions of Security: Human & Global Security
The Shift in Referent
  • From State to Individual: Non-traditional security questions the traditional referent of security. Instead of asking "Security for whom?" and answering "the state," it expands the referent to include individuals, communities, and all of humankind.
  • Human Security vs. State Security: Secure states do not automatically guarantee secure peoples. Historically, during the last 100 years, more people have been killed by their own governments than by foreign invading armies.
  • Narrow vs. Broad Human Security: Proponents of the narrow view focus on protecting individuals from violent threats (e.g., civil war, state violence). Proponents of the broad view argue that the security agenda must include hunger, disease, and natural disasters ("freedom from want" and "freedom from fear").
Global Security Threats

The concept of global security emerged in the 1990s to address threats that are transnational in nature and cannot be resolved by any single country acting alone:

  • Global Warming: A projected sea-level rise of 1.5 to 2.0 meters would submerge 20% of Bangladesh, flood most of the Maldives, and threaten half the population of Thailand.
  • International Terrorism: Political violence targeting civilians deliberately and indiscriminately across multiple national borders.
  • Health Epidemics: Rapidly spreading diseases like HIV-AIDS, bird flu, SARS, and Corona, which travel via global migration, trade, and tourism.
New Sources of Threats
Terrorism
  • Definition: Political violence that targets civilians deliberately and indiscriminately to terrorize the public and use their fear as a weapon against governments.
  • Classic Tactics: Hijacking planes, planting bombs in trains, cafes, markets, and other crowded public spaces.
  • Global Shift: Since the 11 September 2001 attacks on the US World Trade Centre, global attention on terrorism has intensified, though the phenomenon has long affected regions like the Middle East, Europe, Latin America, and South Asia.
Human Rights Violations

Human rights are broadly classified into three distinct categories:

  1. First Type: Political rights, such as freedom of speech, expression, and assembly.
  2. Second Type: Economic and social rights, ensuring basic survival and dignity.
  3. Third Type: Rights of colonized peoples, ethnic minorities, and indigenous populations.
  • UN Intervention Debate: Since the 1990s, atrocities in Kuwait, Rwanda, and East Timor have sparked intense debate over whether the UN Charter authorizes military intervention to stop human rights abuses, or if such actions are driven by the national interests of powerful states.
Global Poverty, Migration, and Refugees
  • Demographic Disparities: World population is projected to reach nearly 1000 crore by the mid-21st century. Currently, half of this growth occurs in just six countries: India, China, Pakistan, Nigeria, Bangladesh, and Indonesia.
  • North-South Divide: High per capita incomes and low population growth make rich Northern states richer, while low incomes and high population growth make poor Southern states poorer.
  • Migrants vs. Refugees: Migrants voluntarily leave their home countries for better economic opportunities, and states are not legally bound to accept them. Refugees flee war, natural disasters, or political persecution, and international law generally obligates states to accept them.
  • Internally Displaced People : Individuals who have fled their homes due to violence or conflict but remain within their national borders (e.g., Kashmiri Pandits who fled the Kashmir Valley in the early 1990s).
Cooperative Security
The Limits of Military Force
  • Inadequacy of Force: Military force is ineffective against non-traditional threats like poverty, migration, epidemics, and environmental degradation. In most cases, using military force to address these issues only worsens the situation.
  • Multilateral Cooperation: Effective strategies require international cooperation, which can be bilateral, regional, continental, or global.
  • Diverse Global Actors: Cooperative security involves not just states, but international organizations (UN, WHO, World Bank, IMF), non-governmental organizations (Amnesty International, Red Cross), private charities, trade unions, and prominent global figures.
  • Force as a Last Resort: Cooperative security may involve the collective, internationally sanctioned use of force to deal with governments that commit genocide or to combat international terrorist networks.
India's Security Strategy

India's national security strategy is a comprehensive, four-pronged approach designed to address both traditional and non-traditional threats:

Strategy Component Core Objective Key Policies & Historical Milestones Systemic Significance
First Component: Military Capabilities Strengthening defense capabilities to deter external military aggression. Conflicts with Pakistan (1947-48, 1965, 1971, 1999) and China (1962); Nuclear tests conducted in 1974 and 1998. Ensures deterrence in a highly volatile, nuclear-armed South Asian neighborhood.
Second Component: International Norms Strengthening international institutions and global cooperative norms. Jawaharlal Nehru's support for Asian solidarity, decolonisation, non-alignment, and signing the Kyoto Protocol (1997). Protects national interests by promoting a rule-based, non-discriminatory global order.
Third Component: Internal Security Preserving national unity and territorial integrity against domestic rebellions. Addressing militant movements in Nagaland, Mizoram, Punjab, and Kashmir through democratic political integration. Maintains national integration by allowing diverse communities to share power and voice grievances.
Fourth Component: Economic Development Lifting the vast mass of citizens out of poverty and reducing economic inequalities. Democratic planning, poverty alleviation programs, and combining economic growth with human development. Ensures social stability and strengthens the democratic foundation of national security.

Profile: Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964)

  • Role & Affiliation: First Prime Minister of India and Minister of External Affairs (1947–1964).
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed non-alignment , Asian solidarity, decolonisation, and peaceful coexistence.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Co-founded the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM), advocated for global disarmament, and sought to resolve international conflicts through the United Nations.
Narrative Case Studies & Cartoon Analyses

"Kotabagh, Gewali, Kandali and Goppa are villages adjoining each other beside a river. Kotabagh were the first settlers and claimed uninterrupted access to resources. As population expanded, resources did not. Kandali and Gewali claimed a greater share due to larger populations. Goppa claimed a bigger share to maintain their extravagant lifestyle. Disagreements led to frequent clashes and loss of peace."

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This hypothetical scenario of four villages illustrates the core dynamics of resource conflict in international relations. It mirrors how nation-states clash over scarce natural resources (like water, oil, or minerals) based on historical claims, demographic needs, or high consumption rates, highlighting the absolute necessity of cooperative resource management to prevent mutual destruction.

"A cartoon depicting a giant pig labeled 'WAR' nursing several piglets labeled 'TERRORISM'."

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This cartoon illustrates the systemic connection between state-sponsored or large-scale military conflicts ("War") and the rise of asymmetric, non-state violent threats ("Terrorism"). War creates power vacuums, breeds resentment, and provides the breeding ground and justification for terrorist networks to multiply.
