Political Science: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 6: Environment and Natural Resources

Environmental Concerns in Global Politics

While traditional world politics focuses on national security, wars, treaties, and state power, contemporary global politics increasingly encompasses ecological challenges that transcend national borders and require collective international action.

Key Global Environmental Challenges
  • Agricultural Land Degradation : Cultivable land is no longer expanding globally, and existing agricultural tracts are rapidly losing fertility due to intensive farming, overgrazing of grasslands, and depletion of water tables.
  • Water Scarcity and Sanitation : According to the Human Development Report 2016 , over 663 million people in developing nations lack access to safe drinking water, and 2.4 billion lack basic sanitation, leading to more than three million child deaths annually.
  • Deforestation and Biodiversity Loss : Rapid destruction of natural forests for timber and agriculture destabilizes local climates, disrupts water supplies, and drives massive habitat destruction, leading to an unprecedented loss of terrestrial biodiversity.
  • Ozone Depletion : The steady decline of stratospheric ozone (the ozone hole ) increases ultraviolet radiation exposure, posing severe threats to human health and marine ecosystems.
  • Coastal and Marine Pollution : Land-based industrial runoff, plastic waste, and intensive coastal human settlements are severely degrading marine environments, even as open seas remain relatively clean.

Around the Aral Sea, thousands of people have been forced to abandon their homes as toxic, hyper-saline waters completely destroyed the local fishing and shipping industries, causing widespread crop failures and creating a massive population of environmental refugees.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This ecological disaster demonstrates that environmental degradation is not merely a geographical issue but a profound political crisis. It highlights how resource mismanagement can collapse local economies, trigger mass displacement, and create regional instability that no single state can resolve independently.

Evolution of Global Environmental Politics

The political character of environmental issues solidified in the late 20th century as international organizations and scientific bodies began documenting the ecological limits of industrial growth.

Key Milestones in Global Environmentalism
  • Limits to Growth (1972) : A seminal book published by the global think tank, the Club of Rome , which dramatized the impending depletion of Earth's resources against the backdrop of rapid population and industrial growth.
  • United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) : Established to coordinate international environmental initiatives, promote scientific studies, and facilitate multilateral conferences.
  • The Earth Summit (1992) : Formally known as the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED) , held in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. It was attended by 170 states, thousands of NGOs, and numerous multinational corporations, firmly consolidating environmental issues at the center-stage of global politics.
  • Agenda 21 : A comprehensive blueprint of development practices recommended at the Rio Summit, aiming to balance economic growth with ecological responsibility, a concept known as sustainable development . Critics, however, argue that Agenda 21 remains biased toward economic growth over strict ecological conservation.
The North-South Divide at Rio
Dimension The Global North (First World) The Global South (Third World)
Primary Concern Ozone depletion, global warming, and general ecological conservation. Economic development, poverty alleviation, and equitable resource management.

The Protection of Global Commons

The global commons refer to resource areas located outside the sovereign jurisdiction of any single state, requiring cooperative international governance.

Key Areas of the Global Commons
  • The Earth's atmosphere
  • Antarctica
  • The ocean floor
  • Outer space
Major International Environmental Treaties
Treaty / Agreement Year Core Objective & Scope
Antarctic Treaty 1959 Demilitarized the Antarctic continent, established it as a zone for scientific research, and suspended all territorial sovereignty claims.
Montreal Protocol 1987 A landmark agreement designed to protect the ozone layer by phasing out the production of ozone-depleting substances like chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs).
Antarctic Environmental Protocol 1991 Designated Antarctica as a natural reserve devoted to peace and science, banning commercial mining and setting strict environmental protection rules.
Kyoto Protocol 1997 Set legally binding emission reduction targets for industrialized nations to combat global warming, based on principles of the UNFCCC.

Antarctica covers over 14 million square kilometers, containing 90 percent of the world's ice and 70 percent of its fresh water. While countries like the UK, Argentina, Chile, Norway, France, Australia, and New Zealand have made historical territorial claims, most states view it as a global commons. Despite strict treaties, scientific research, tourism, and oil spills continue to threaten its fragile marine ecosystem.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Antarctica represents the complex geopolitics of the global commons. The tension between national sovereignty claims and international stewardship highlights the difficulty of enforcing environmental regulations in areas devoid of permanent human populations but rich in scientific and strategic value.

Common but Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR)

The principle of Common but Differentiated Responsibilities is a cornerstone of international environmental law, formally recognized at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit.

Core Tenets of CBDR
  • Shared Responsibility : All states share a common obligation to protect the Earth's ecosystem.
  • Differentiated Obligations : Developed countries must bear a larger share of the financial and technological burden of environmental protection because their historical and current industrial activities have contributed the most to global greenhouse gas emissions.
  • Developing Country Exemptions : Developing nations, currently in the process of industrialization, are granted exemptions or flexible targets under major protocols (such as the Kyoto Protocol) to ensure their economic growth is not compromised.
  • UNFCCC Framework : The 1992 United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change explicitly states that parties should protect the climate system "on the basis of equity and in accordance with their common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities."
Common Property Resources & India's Stand

Common property resources represent shared community assets where members hold both rights and duties regarding resource use and maintenance, preventing overexploitation through customary norms.

Sacred groves in India (parcels of uncut forest vegetation protected in the name of local deities) represent an ancient, community-based resource management system that preserves biodiversity and cultural heritage through religious reverence.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: Sacred groves illustrate how traditional, decentralized systems can successfully manage common property. However, modern state-centric forest policies, commercial encroachment, and legal ownership disputes often undermine these community-led conservation efforts, highlighting the clash between state authority and local customary practices.

India's Environmental Policy and Global Negotiations
  • Kyoto Protocol Ratification : India signed and ratified the Kyoto Protocol in August 2002. Along with China, India was exempted from binding emission targets because its per capita emissions were a fraction of those in developed nations.
  • Defense of CBDR : At international forums like the G-8 meeting in 2005, India argued that the primary responsibility for reducing emissions lies with developed nations, which have accumulated historical emissions over centuries.
  • Paris Climate Agreement : India ratified the Paris Agreement on October 2, 2016, committing to voluntary national targets while expanding its renewable energy capacity.
  • Domestic Initiatives : India has implemented several domestic policies to improve energy efficiency and reduce emissions:
    • National Auto-fuel Policy : Mandates cleaner fuels and emission standards for vehicles.
    • Energy Conservation Act (2001) : Outlines initiatives to improve energy efficiency across industries.
    • Electricity Act (2003) : Encourages the generation and consumption of renewable energy.
    • National Mission on Biodiesel : Aimed at producing biodiesel using millions of hectares of wasteland.
Environmental Movements & Resource Geopolitics

Environmental protection is driven not only by state treaties but also by vibrant, diverse local social movements and the strategic geopolitics of resource control.

Types of Environmental Movements
  • Forest Movements : Active in the Global South (e.g., Mexico, Brazil, Malaysia, India), these movements resist rapid deforestation and commercial logging, emphasizing that local communities live in and depend on these forests, unlike the Northern view of forests as uninhabited "wilderness."
  • Anti-Mining Campaigns : Local communities resist multinational corporations (MNCs) due to chemical pollution, displacement, and destruction of native vegetation. A key example is the campaign against the Western Mining Corporation (WMC) in the Philippines.
  • Anti-Dam and Pro-River Movements : Oppose mega-dams that submerge forests, displace communities, and disrupt river ecosystems. Examples include the campaign to save the Franklin River in Australia and the Narmada Bachao Andolan in India, which champions non-violent resistance.
Resource Geopolitics: Oil and Water
  • Oil Geopolitics : Throughout the 20th century, global economic power relied heavily on petroleum. West Asia (specifically the Gulf region) holds approximately 64 percent of the planet's known oil reserves, making it the focal point of military interventions, strategic alliances, and interstate conflicts.
  • Water Wars : Scarcity of freshwater is a major source of potential conflict in the 21st century. Disagreements arise between upstream (upper riparian) and downstream (lower riparian) states over dam construction, pollution, and water diversion. Historical conflicts include disputes between Israel, Syria, and Jordan over the Jordan River, and tensions between Turkey, Syria, and Iraq over the Euphrates.
Indigenous Peoples and Their Rights

The struggles of indigenous peoples bring together issues of environmental conservation, resource control, and human rights in global politics.

Profile: World Council of Indigenous Peoples (WCIP) (Est. 1975)

  • Role & Affiliation: An international non-governmental organization formed to represent indigenous communities globally; it became the first of 11 indigenous NGOs to receive consultative status in the United Nations.
  • Ideological Contributions: Advocated for the recognition of indigenous populations as equal members of the international community, emphasizing their unique spiritual and economic relationship with ancestral lands.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Successfully brought indigenous land rights, cultural preservation, and environmental stewardship into mainstream global political discourses, particularly within anti-globalization movements.
Indigenous Rights and Land Loss
  • UN Definition : The UN defines indigenous populations as descendants of peoples who inhabited a territory at the time when groups of a different culture or ethnic origin arrived and overcame them.
  • Global Demographics : Approximately 30 crore indigenous people live worldwide, including the Cordillera region of the Philippines (20 lakh), Mapuche of Chile (10 lakh), Chittagong Hill Tracts of Bangladesh (6 lakh), and the Small Peoples of the Soviet North (10 lakh).
  • The Indian Context : In India, indigenous peoples are usually referred to as Scheduled Tribes (or Tribals), constituting nearly eight percent of the national population. While they enjoy constitutional protection and political representation, they have paid a disproportionate cost for national development, representing the single largest group displaced by major developmental projects since independence.
