Political Science: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 7: Globalisation

Overview and the Concept of Globalisation

Globalisation is a multi-dimensional concept that cannot be restricted to a single domain. It encompasses political, economic, and cultural dimensions, which must be distinguished to understand its uneven impact across different societies.

Core Concept: The Dynamics of Global Flows

At its core, globalisation is defined by the concept of constant, multi-directional flows across national boundaries. These flows create and sustain a state of worldwide interconnectedness .

  • Flow of Ideas: The movement of concepts, beliefs, and knowledge from one part of the world to another.
  • Flow of Capital: The movement of financial resources and investments between countries.
  • Flow of Commodities: The trade of physical goods and products across international borders.
  • Flow of People: The migration of individuals in search of better livelihoods, education, or employment.
Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Globalisation A multi-dimensional process of worldwide interconnectedness driven by constant global flows. Technological advancements, international trade agreements, and state policies. Integrates global societies, expands economic opportunities, and fosters cross-cultural exchange.
Worldwide Interconnectedness The mutual dependence and systemic links established between distant communities. Global communication networks, transport infrastructure, and shared global challenges. Enables collective action for global issues and facilitates rapid sharing of resources and ideas.
Social Safety Nets Institutional safeguards designed to protect vulnerable populations from economic shocks. State welfare policies, social security legislation, and international human rights standards. Mitigates the negative fallout of market deregulation and ensures basic survival for the economically weak.
Narrative Case Studies of Globalisation

Janardhan works in a call centre, adopting a Western pseudonym, accent, and working hours that align with US daytime. Ramdhari purchases a bicycle manufactured in China and a doll made in the US for his daughter. Sarika, a first-generation learner, secures an independent career opportunity previously unavailable to women in her family.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: These cases illustrate the diverse dimensions of globalisation. Janardhan represents the globalisation of services and the cultural adaptation required by global labor markets. Ramdhari highlights the seamless movement of commodities across borders, showcasing consumer choice and global manufacturing chains. Sarika demonstrates how globalisation can challenge traditional social structures and expand individual liberties, particularly for marginalized groups.

An insider's account of working in an Indian call centre reveals the complex reality of interacting with American clients. While some interactions are positive, workers frequently face irate, abusive, and xenophobic calls from individuals who resent the outsourcing of jobs to India.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This narrative highlights the friction points of economic globalisation. The outsourcing of service sector jobs creates economic opportunities in developing nations like India but simultaneously triggers protectionist anxieties and nationalist backlash in developed economies, manifesting as personal hostility toward offshore workers.

Causes of Globalisation

While globalisation is not caused by any single factor, technology remains a critical catalyst that has fundamentally altered the scale and speed of global interactions.

The Technological Revolution
  • Communication Infrastructure: The invention of the telegraph, telephone, and microchip has revolutionized communication, making real-time global interaction possible.
  • Historical Precedents: Global flows have existed throughout human history, but contemporary globalisation is unique due to the unprecedented speed and volume of these flows.
  • Global Awareness: Technological advancements foster a shared consciousness of global events, such as pandemics or natural disasters, which do not respect national boundaries.
Consequences of Globalisation

The impact of globalisation is highly uneven, affecting different societies and sectors in diverse ways. These consequences are categorized into political, economic, and cultural dimensions.

Comparative Matrix of Globalisation's Consequences
Dimension Positive Manifestations / Arguments Negative Manifestations / Concerns
Political
  • Enhanced state capacity through advanced technology for data collection and governance.
  • Primacy of the state remains unchallenged in core areas like national security and law and order.
  • Erosion of state capacity as the welfare state yields to a minimalist state.
  • Market forces replace state planning in determining economic and social priorities.
Economic
  • Greater economic growth, deregulation, and trade integration.
  • Increased consumer choice and efficiency through global competition.
  • Uneven distribution of gains, benefit concentrated among the wealthy.
  • Withdrawal of state subsidies and welfare, leading to economic insecurity for the poor.
  • Strict visa regimes in developed nations restrict the free movement of labor.
Cultural
  • Cultural heterogenisation , where external influences modify but enrich local cultures.
  • Expanded choices in food, clothing, and lifestyle without destroying traditional heritages.
  • Cultural homogenisation , leading to the imposition of Western culture and the 'McDonaldisation' of the world.
  • Shrinking of global cultural diversity under the influence of US soft power.
India and Globalisation

India's relationship with global flows has evolved significantly from the colonial era to the post-independence period and the landmark policy shifts of the late 20th century.

Historical Trajectory and Policy Shifts
  1. Colonial Period: Under British rule, India was integrated into the global economy as an exporter of raw materials and an importer of manufactured goods, leading to systemic economic drain.
  2. Post-Independence Protectionism: To achieve self-reliance, India adopted protectionist policies, restricting imports and foreign investment. While this nurtured domestic industries, it led to sluggish economic growth and neglected critical social sectors like primary education and healthcare.
  3. 1991 Economic Reforms: Facing a severe balance of payments crisis, India initiated structural adjustment programmes, embracing deregulation, trade liberalisation, and foreign direct investment to integrate with the global economy.
Resistance to Globalisation

Resistance to globalisation is diverse, originating from different ideological standpoints and organized through transnational networks.

Ideological Critiques and Global Platforms
Ideological Standpoint Core Arguments & Concerns Key Demands / Alternatives
Left-Wing Critique
  • Globalisation is a phase of global capitalism that enriches the wealthy while impoverishing the poor.
  • Weakening of the state deprives the poor of essential welfare protections.
  • Re-establishment of state welfare systems.
  • Resistance to forced economic integration and corporate dominance.
Right-Wing Critique
  • Anxiety over the erosion of national sovereignty and state capacity.
  • Fear of cultural decay and the loss of traditional values and social norms.
  • Return to economic self-reliance and protectionism.
  • Preservation of indigenous cultural heritage and social structures.

The World Social Forum (WSF) serves as a key global platform bringing together activists, environmentalists, and labor unions opposed to neo-liberal globalisation. It has held meetings globally, including Porto Alegre (2001), Mumbai (2004), and Nepal (2024).

Resistance to Globalisation in India
  • Economic Resistance: Left-wing political parties and trade unions protest against economic liberalisation and the entry of multinational corporations. Farmer groups have strongly opposed the patenting of indigenous plants (such as Neem) by foreign firms.
  • Cultural Resistance: Right-wing groups protest against Western cultural influences, including the availability of foreign television channels, the celebration of Valentine's Day, and changes in traditional dress codes.
