Political Science: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 1: Challenges of Nation Building

Challenges for the New Nation-State

At the midnight hour of 14-15 August 1947, India attained independence. Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru addressed the Constituent Assembly with his famous Tryst with Destiny speech. The new nation faced the immediate task of translating the promises of the national movement into reality under highly difficult circumstances of partition and violence.

Partition: Displacement and Rehabilitation

The partition of British India into two independent nation-states, India and Pakistan , was executed based on the two-nation theory advanced by the Muslim League . This theory asserted that India consisted of two distinct "peoples"—Hindus and Muslims—and demanded a separate homeland for Muslims. The Indian National Congress strongly opposed this division, but political developments in the 1940s led to the decision for creation of Pakistan.

The Complex Process of Partition
  • Principle of Religious Majorities: Areas where Muslims were in the majority were designated as Pakistan, while the rest remained with India.
  • Geographical Discontinuity: No single contiguous belt of Muslim-majority areas existed. This necessitated the creation of two separate territories: West Pakistan and East Pakistan , divided by thousands of miles of Indian territory.
  • Opposition from NWFP: Khan Abdul Gaffar Khan (known as "Frontier Gandhi"), the leader of the North Western Frontier Province, staunchly opposed the two-nation theory, though his voice was ignored.
  • Bifurcation of Punjab and Bengal: These provinces had vast areas with non-Muslim majorities, requiring them to be split at the district or lower levels. This decision was delayed, leaving millions of people unaware of their nationality on 15 August 1947.
Consequences of Partition
  • Massive Population Transfer: One of the largest, most abrupt, and tragic migrations in human history, forcing about 80 lakh people to cross borders.
  • Communal Violence: Cities like Lahore, Amritsar, and Kolkata became divided into communal zones . Between 5 to 10 lakh people were killed in partition-related violence.
  • Atrocities and Abductions: Thousands of women were abducted, forced into marriage, and converted. Many were killed by their own families to preserve "family honour."
  • Division of Assets: Partition involved not just territory but the division of financial assets, government employees, railway networks, and physical properties down to tables, chairs, and police band instruments.

Mahatma Gandhi's Sacrifice

On 15 August 1947, Mahatma Gandhi did not participate in any Independence Day celebrations. Instead, he stayed in Kolkata, working to end the gruesome communal riots between Hindus and Muslims. His presence and subsequent fasts had a dramatic calming effect on the violence, first in Kolkata and later in Delhi in January 1948. He was deeply committed to ensuring that Muslims could live in India with dignity as equal citizens. His lifelong struggle for truth, non-violence, and communal harmony ended on 30 January 1948, when he was assassinated by extremist Nathuram Vinayak Godse .

Integration of Princely States

At the time of independence, India was divided into British Indian Provinces (directly ruled by the British government) and Princely States (ruled by local princes who accepted British paramountcy or suzerainty). There were 565 princely states, covering one-third of India's land area and containing one-fourth of its population.

The Problem of Integration
  • Lapse of Paramountcy: The British announced that with the end of their rule, paramountcy over princely states would lapse, leaving them legally independent to join India, Pakistan, or remain independent.
  • Threat of Balkanisation: Rulers of states like Travancore and Hyderabad quickly declared their intention to remain independent, threatening the territorial integrity of a united India.
The Government's Approach

The interim government took a firm stance against the division of India into small principalities. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel , as Deputy Prime Minister and Home Minister, played a historic role in negotiating with the rulers firmly but diplomatically.

Profile: Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel (1875-1950)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the freedom movement, Congress leader, follower of Mahatma Gandhi, Deputy Prime Minister, and first Home Minister of independent India.
  • Ideological Contributions: Champion of national integration, firm but diplomatic negotiator, and key architect of India's territorial consolidation.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Successfully integrated 565 princely states into the Indian Union using the Instrument of Accession .

The government's approach was guided by three considerations:

  1. The people of most princely states clearly wanted to become part of the Indian Union.
  2. The government was prepared to be flexible in giving autonomy to some regions to accommodate plurality.
  3. The integration and consolidation of territorial boundaries had assumed supreme importance in the backdrop of Partition.
Case Study: Hyderabad
  • Status: The largest princely state, ruled by the Nizam , who wanted an independent status and signed a Standstill Agreement with India in November 1947.
  • People's Movement: A massive uprising arose against the Nizam's oppressive rule, particularly led by the peasantry in Telangana and women who had faced severe oppression.
  • The Razakars: The Nizam unleashed a sectarian para-military force known as the Razakars , who committed atrocities, targeting non-Muslims.
  • Military Intervention: In September 1948, the Indian Army moved in to control the Nizam's forces. After a few days of fighting, the Nizam surrendered, leading to Hyderabad's accession to India.
Case Study: Manipur
  • Accession: Maharaja Bodhachandra Singh signed the Instrument of Accession before Independence on the assurance that Manipur's internal autonomy would be maintained.
  • First Democratic Election: Under public pressure, the Maharaja held elections in June 1948, making Manipur the first part of India to hold an election based on universal adult franchise .
  • Merger: Despite sharp differences in the Legislative Assembly, the Government of India successfully persuaded the Maharaja to sign a Merger Agreement in September 1949.
Reorganisation of States

The process of nation-building required drawing internal boundaries that reflected the linguistic and cultural plurality of the country without affecting national unity. During colonial rule, boundaries were drawn based on administrative convenience or British conquests.

The Linguistic Principle and Postponement

The Indian National Congress had committed to the linguistic principle of state formation as early as the Nagpur Session of 1920. However, after Independence and Partition, leaders feared that carving states based on language might lead to disintegration and distract from urgent socio-economic challenges. The central leadership thus decided to postpone the matter.

The Vishalandhra Movement and Andhra State

The decision to postpone linguistic states was challenged by local protests, particularly in the Telugu-speaking areas of the Madras province. The Vishalandhra movement demanded a separate Andhra province. The movement gathered immense momentum when veteran Gandhian leader Potti Sriramulu went on an indefinite fast and died after 56 days. His death led to widespread violence and protests, forcing the Prime Minister to announce the formation of a separate Andhra state in December 1952.

Profile: Potti Sriramulu (1901-1952)

  • Role & Affiliation: Gandhian worker and Congress leader.
  • Ideological Contributions: Dedicated advocate for Dalit temple entry and linguistic self-determination.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Undertook a fast unto death from 19 October 1952 demanding a separate state of Andhra, dying during the fast on 15 December 1952, which directly led to the creation of Andhra State.
States Reorganisation Commission (SRC)

The creation of Andhra spurred demands for linguistic states across India. In 1953, the Central Government appointed the States Reorganisation Commission to look into the redrawing of boundaries. The Commission accepted that state boundaries should reflect linguistic divisions. This led to the passage of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 , which created 14 states and 6 union territories.
