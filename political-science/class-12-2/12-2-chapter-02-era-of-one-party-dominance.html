Political Science: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 2: Era of One-Party Dominance

The Challenge of Building Democracy

The birth of independent India was accompanied by the monumental task of establishing a democratic political order under extremely difficult conditions of poverty, illiteracy, and social division.

The Democratic Choice vs. Global Trends
  • Democratic Commitment : Unlike many post-colonial nations in Asia and Africa that opted for non-democratic regimes (such as military rule, one-party systems, or nominal democracies) citing national unity as a priority, Indian leaders chose the challenging path of representative democracy.
  • Role of Politics : Indian leaders did not view politics as a problem or a source of conflict, but as a vital mechanism for resolving societal differences and pursuing the public interest.
Setting Up the Electoral Machinery
  • Constitution of India : Adopted on 26 November 1949, signed on 24 January 1950, and came into effect on 26 January 1950, establishing the legal framework for elections.
  • Election Commission of India : Established in January 1950 to conduct free and fair elections. Sukumar Sen was appointed as the first Chief Election Commissioner.
  • Delimitation and Electoral Rolls : The Commission had to draw boundaries of electoral constituencies and prepare the list of eligible voters. The first draft of the rolls revealed that 40 lakh women were omitted or listed only as "wife of..." or "daughter of...", prompting the Commission to order a complete revision.
  • Scale of the First General Election : A mammoth exercise involving 17 crore eligible voters, 3,200 MLAs, and 489 Lok Sabha MPs. Only 15% of the electorate was literate, requiring the training of over 3 lakh polling staff and officers.
Evolution of Voting Methods

The methods used to record voter preferences in India have evolved significantly over the decades to ensure secrecy, efficiency, and accessibility.

Voting Method Period of Use Operational Mechanism
Individual Candidate Ballot Boxes First & Second General Elections (1951-1957) Each candidate had a dedicated steel ballot box with their election symbol inside the polling booth. Voters dropped a blank ballot paper into the box of their chosen candidate. Over 20 lakh steel boxes were used.
Stamped Ballot Papers Third General Election (1962) to late 1990s A single ballot paper listing the names and symbols of all candidates was introduced. Voters marked their choice by placing a stamp on the candidate's name/symbol and dropped it into a common box. Used for nearly 40 years.
Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs) Introduced in late 1990s; nationwide by 2004 Voters record their preferences electronically by pressing a button next to the candidate's name and symbol. This modernized the process, reduced paper waste, and accelerated counting.
The Historic Success of the 1952 Election
  • Skepticism and Criticism : Critics labeled the introduction of universal adult franchise in a poor, illiterate nation as "the biggest gamble in history" and an "absurd farce."
  • Execution : Held from October 1951 to February 1952 (referred to as the 1952 election). Campaigning, polling, and counting took six months.
  • Significance : Proved that democratic elections could be successfully conducted under conditions of poverty and illiteracy, establishing a global landmark for universal adult franchise.
Congress Dominance in the First Three General Elections

The Indian National Congress dominated the electoral landscape during the first decade of representative politics, winning overwhelming majorities at both the national and state levels.

Electoral Performance (1952-1962)
  • First General Election (1952) : Congress won 364 out of 489 Lok Sabha seats. The Communist Party of India (CPI) emerged as the second-largest party with just 16 seats.
  • Second (1957) and Third (1962) Elections : Congress maintained its dominance, securing three-fourths of the seats in the Lok Sabha in both elections. No opposition party could secure even 10% of the seats won by Congress.
  • State Assembly Elections : Congress formed governments across almost all states, with minor exceptions, establishing a virtual monopoly on political power.
The First-Past-The-Post (FPTP) Distortion

The extent of Congress's legislative victories was disproportionately higher than its actual popular vote share due to the electoral system.

  • Vote-Seat Discrepancy : In 1952, Congress won 74% of the Lok Sabha seats despite securing only 45% of the total votes cast.
  • Divided Opposition : The non-Congress votes were divided among numerous rival parties and independent candidates, allowing Congress candidates to win seats with simple pluralities.
The Communist Challenge in Kerala (1957)

In the March 1957 assembly elections in Kerala, the Communist Party of India (CPI) won 60 out of 126 seats and secured the support of five independents. The governor invited E.M.S. Namboodiripad to form the first democratically elected Communist government in the world. In response, the Congress party initiated a local 'liberation struggle' against the progressive policies of the state government. In 1959, the central Congress government dismissed the CPI ministry under Article 356 of the Constitution.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance : This event marked the first major electoral defeat for the Congress party and highlighted the intense friction between federalism and central dominance. The use of Article 356 to dismiss an elected state government became a highly controversial precedent, widely cited as an early misuse of constitutional emergency powers by the ruling party at the Centre.

Nature of Congress Dominance

The dominance of the Congress party in India was structurally distinct from one-party systems in other parts of the world, as it operated within a highly competitive, democratic framework.

Comparative Analysis of One-Party Systems
Type of One-Party System Countries / Examples Mechanism of Dominance
Constitutional Monopoly China, Cuba, Syria The constitution permits only a single ruling party; opposition is legally banned.
Military / Legal Control Myanmar, Belarus, Egypt, Eritrea Opposition is suppressed through direct military intervention, legal barriers, and authoritarian measures.
Democratic Dominance India (Congress), South Africa (ANC) Dominance is achieved through free, fair, and competitive elections under democratic conditions.
The Congress as a Social and Ideological Coalition
  • Social Coalition : Evolving from an elite pressure group of English-speaking, urban professionals in 1885, the Congress transformed through civil disobedience movements into a broad-based mass movement. It brought together diverse social groups, including peasants, industrialists, urban dwellers, villagers, and various castes.
  • Ideological Coalition : Congress accommodated a wide spectrum of political beliefs, including revolutionaries, pacifists, conservatives, radicals, extremists, moderates, right-wingers, and left-wingers, acting as a common platform for the national movement.
Tolerance and Management of Factions (The 'Congress System')
  • Factions : Internal groups based on ideological differences or personal rivalries existed within the Congress. The party's coalitional nature tolerated and encouraged these factions.
  • Balancing Mechanism : Instead of leaving the party to join the opposition, dissatisfied groups fought for power within the Congress. This internal competition acted as a self-correcting mechanism.
  • The Congress System : During the first decade, political competition took place within the Congress itself, meaning the party acted as both the ruling power and its own internal opposition.
Major Political Parties of the Era

Several opposition parties emerged during this period, representing distinct ideological alternatives to the Congress and laying the foundation for India's multi-party democracy.

The Socialist Party (SP)
  • Origins : Traced back to the Congress Socialist Party (CSP) formed within the Congress in 1934. In 1948, Congress amended its constitution to ban dual membership, forcing the socialists to split and form the independent Socialist Party.
  • Ideology : Believed in democratic socialism, distinguishing themselves from both the capitalist-leaning Congress and the revolutionary Communists. They criticized Congress for ignoring workers and peasants.
  • Dilemma and Splits : Faced a crisis when Congress declared its goal to be a "socialist pattern of society" in 1955. The party underwent multiple splits and reunions, leading to the formation of the Praja Socialist Party and Samyukta Socialist Party.
The Communist Party of India (CPI)
  • Origins : Emerged in the 1920s inspired by the Bolshevik revolution. Worked within the Congress from 1935 until 1941, when they split to support the British war effort against Nazi Germany.
  • Post-Independence Shift : Initially rejected Indian independence as a sham and led violent uprisings in Telangana. In 1951, they abandoned violent revolution to participate in democratic elections, emerging as the primary opposition party.
  • The 1964 Split : Split into the pro-Soviet CPI and the pro-China CPI(M) due to ideological rifts between the Soviet Union and China.
The Bharatiya Jana Sangh (BJS)
  • Origins : Formed in 1951 by Shyama Prasad Mukherjee, with roots in the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) and the Hindu Mahasabha.
  • Ideology : Emphasized the concept of "one country, one culture, one nation" (Akhand Bharat). Advocated for replacing English with Hindi as the official language, opposed concessions to religious minorities, and strongly supported the development of nuclear weapons.
Emergence of Opposition Parties and Early Democratic Culture

Despite their limited seat share, early opposition parties played a vital role in maintaining the democratic character of the Indian polity.

Role of the Opposition
  • Democratic Check : Offered principled criticism of Congress policies, keeping the ruling party under check and preventing public resentment from turning anti-democratic.
  • Leadership Grooming : Provided a training ground for leaders who would play a crucial role in shaping the country's political future.
Mutual Respect and Coexistence
  • Inclusive Cabinets : The early years were marked by mutual respect between leaders of the ruling party and the opposition. The interim government included opposition figures like Dr. B.R. Ambedkar and Shyama Prasad Mukherjee.
  • Nehru's Approach : Jawaharlal Nehru frequently expressed respect for opposition leaders and invited figures like Jayaprakash Narayan to join his government, reflecting a non-adversarial political culture that declined as competition intensified.
Biographical Profiles of Key Leaders

Profile: Babasaheb Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (1891-1956)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the anti-caste movement; founder of the Independent Labour Party and Scheduled Castes Federation; Chairman of the Constitution's Drafting Committee; Law Minister in Nehru's first cabinet.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed social justice, Dalit rights, and constitutional democracy; warned against the dangers of "hero-worship" in politics as a road to dictatorship.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Resigned from the cabinet in 1951 due to differences over the Hindu Code Bill; adopted Buddhism in 1956 with thousands of followers.

Profile: Maulana Abul Kalam Azad (1888-1958)

  • Role & Affiliation: Freedom fighter and Congress leader; member of the Constituent Assembly; Education Minister in the first cabinet of free India.
  • Ideological Contributions: Proponent of Hindu-Muslim unity; strongly opposed the Partition of India; scholar of Islam.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Laid the foundation for the educational system of independent India as its first Education Minister.

Profile: Rajkumari Amrit Kaur (1889-1964)

  • Role & Affiliation: Gandhian freedom fighter; member of the Constituent Assembly; Minister for Health in independent India's first ministry (held portfolio until 1957).
  • Ideological Contributions: Advocated for women's rights, healthcare access, and social reform based on Gandhian principles.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Belonged to the royal family of Kapurthala; inherited Christian faith from her mother; established key healthcare institutions in post-independence India.

Profile: Acharya Narendra Dev (1889-1956)

  • Role & Affiliation: Freedom fighter; founding President of the Congress Socialist Party (CSP); later led the Socialist Party and the Praja Socialist Party.
  • Ideological Contributions: Promoted democratic socialism; active in peasant movements; scholar of Buddhism.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Jailed several \times during the freedom struggle; advocated for agrarian reforms and peasant rights.

Profile: Shyama Prasad Mukherjee (1901-1953)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the Hindu Mahasabha; founder-President of the Bharatiya Jana Sangh (BJS); Minister in Nehru's first cabinet.
  • Ideological Contributions: Strongly opposed the policy of autonomy for Jammu & Kashmir; advocated for a unified India (Akhand Bharat).
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Resigned from the cabinet in 1950 due to differences over relations with Pakistan; arrested during the Jana Sangh's agitation against Kashmir policy and died during detention.

Profile: Deen Dayal Upadhyaya (1916-1968)

  • Role & Affiliation: Full-time RSS worker; founder member, General Secretary, and later President of the Bharatiya Jana Sangh.
  • Ideological Contributions: Initiated and formulated the concept of integral humanism as an alternative political philosophy.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Built the organizational structure of the Jana Sangh, emphasizing cultural nationalism and indigenous development models.

Media and Film Analysis

The 1981 Marathi film 'Simhasan' (directed by Jabbar Patel, screenplay by Vijay Tendulkar, based on Arun Sadhu's novels) depicts the intense power struggle for the post of Chief Minister in Maharashtra. The story, narrated through the eyes of a journalist, captures the complex factional fights within the ruling party, the bargaining with trade unions, and the secondary role played by the formal opposition against the backdrop of rural and urban social realities.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance : The film illustrates the operational reality of the 'Congress system' where the primary political contestation and competition occurred within the factions of the ruling party itself. The formal opposition is shown as a secondary player, highlighting how the ruling party's internal dynamics functioned as the real arena of political conflict and negotiation during the era of one-party dominance.
