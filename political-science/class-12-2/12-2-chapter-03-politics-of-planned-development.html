Political Science: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 3: Politics of Planned Development

Introduction & The Politics of Development

After achieving independence and establishing democracy, India faced its third major challenge: economic development to ensure the well-being of all citizens. Unlike the previous challenges, the path to economic development involved deep political choices, contestations, and conflicting interests regarding the model of growth to be adopted.

Orissa villagers protest against POSCO plant (Bhubaneswar, 23 June 2006): People facing displacement by the proposed POSCO-India steel plant in Jagatsinghpur district staged a demonstration outside the Korean company’s office. They demanded the cancellation of the MoU signed between the company and the Orissa government. Protesters from gram panchayats of Dhinkia, Nuagaon, and Gadakujanga argued that the company should not be allowed to set up its plant at the cost of their lives and livelihood. The demonstration was organised by the Rashtriya Yuva Sangathan and the Nabanirman Samiti.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This case highlights the core conflict of planned development: balancing economic growth (attracting global investment, utilizing natural resources like iron ore) against social justice and environmental protection (preventing displacement of tribal populations, protecting livelihoods, and avoiding environmental pollution). It demonstrates that development decisions are not purely technical or economic; they are deeply political, requiring democratic negotiation and consensus among diverse stakeholders.

Political Contestation & Decision Making
  • Political Decisions: Decisions regarding economic development involve weighing the interests of one social group against another, and the present generation against future generations.
  • Democratic Process: In a democracy, major economic decisions must be taken or approved by the people's representatives who are in touch with the feelings of the public, rather than being left solely to technical experts.
  • Shared Vision: After Independence, India's leaders sought a model of development that combined economic growth with social and economic justice.
Ideas of Development & Ideological Spectrum

The concept of "development" has different meanings for different sections of society. For an industrialist, it means setting up factories; for an urban consumer, it means access to goods; and for an Adivasi, it means preserving their traditional habitat and livelihood.

Left vs. Right Ideologies

The political debate on development is often structured around the Left and Right ideological spectrum:

Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Left Ideology Favour of state control of the economy and state regulation over free competition. Social justice, economic redistribution, and protection of marginalized classes. To ensure equitable distribution of resources and prevent capitalist exploitation.
Right Ideology Belief in free competition and market economy to ensure progress without state intervention. Liberalism, free-market capitalism, and individual enterprise. To maximize economic efficiency, growth, and individual liberty through market forces.
Models of Development
  • Modernisation: In the early decades of independence, development was equated with becoming "modern," which meant adopting Western standards of industrialisation, capitalism, liberalism, and scientific rationality.
  • Liberal-Capitalist Model: Prevalent in much of Europe and the US, emphasizing private enterprise and free-market competition.
  • Socialist Model: Prevalent in the USSR, emphasizing state ownership of production, elimination of private property, and centralized planning. This model deeply impressed many Indian leaders, including Jawaharlal Nehru, members of the Communist Party of India, and the Socialist Party.
Planning & The Planning Commission

Despite ideological differences, a broad consensus emerged during the national movement that India's development could not be left to private actors and required active government planning.

The Planning Commission
  • Establishment: Set up in March 1950 by a simple resolution of the Government of India. It was a non-constitutional and advisory body.
  • Role: To design plans for the nation's development, which became effective only after approval by the Union Cabinet.
  • Constitutional Basis: Guided by the Directive Principles of State Policy, aiming to secure adequate means of livelihood, distribute material resources for the common good, and prevent the concentration of wealth.
  • NITI Aayog: On 1 January 2015, the Government of India replaced the Planning Commission with a new institution named NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India) to serve as a policy think tank.
The Bombay Plan (1944)

In 1944, a section of big industrialists drafted a joint proposal for setting up a planned economy in India. Known as the Bombay Plan , it wanted the state to take major initiatives in industrial and other economic investments, demonstrating that even capitalists supported state-led planning at the time.

The Early Initiatives & Five Year Plans

Following the Soviet model, the Planning Commission of India opted for Five Year Plans (FYP) . Under this system, the government prepares a document detailing its income and expenditure for the next five years, dividing its budget into "non-plan" (routine yearly expenditure) and "plan" (targeted five-year developmental expenditure) budgets.

The First Five Year Plan (1951–1956)
  • Objective: To lift the country's economy out of the cycle of poverty.
  • Key Architect: Drafted under the guidance of young economist K.N. Raj , who argued that India should "hasten slowly" to protect democracy.
  • Focus: Primarily addressed the agrarian sector, including heavy investments in dams and irrigation (such as the Bhakra Nangal Dam). It identified unequal land distribution as a major obstacle and focused on land reforms.
  • Savings: Aimed to raise the level of national savings, which did rise during this initial phase but fell short of long-term expectations.
The Second Five Year Plan (1956–1961)
  • Objective: To bring about quick structural transformation through rapid industrialisation.
  • Key Architect: Drafted by a team of economists and planners led by P.C. Mahalanobis .
  • Focus: Heavy industries, infrastructure development (electricity, railways, steel, machineries, and communication), and active public sector expansion.
  • Socialist Pattern: Reflected the Congress party's resolution passed at the Avadi session declaring a "socialist pattern of society" as its goal.
  • Protectionism: The government imposed substantial tariffs on imports to protect domestic industries, creating a protected environment for both public and private sectors to grow.

Profile: P.C. Mahalanobis (1893-1972)

  • Role & Affiliation: Scientist and statistician of international repute; founder of the Indian Statistical Institute (1931); key advisor to the Planning Commission.
  • Ideological Contributions: Strong supporter of rapid industrialisation and an active, dominant role for the public sector in the economy.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Known as the architect of the Second Five Year Plan, which shifted India's developmental focus toward heavy industries and structural transformation.
Key Debates and Plan Holiday
  • Agriculture vs. Industry: Critics argued that the Second Plan's heavy focus on industry came at the cost of agriculture, leading to food shortages and an "urban bias" in planning.
  • Foreign Exchange Crisis: Rapid industrialisation required importing expensive technology, which depleted India's precious foreign exchange reserves.
  • Plan Holiday (1966): The Third Plan (1961–1966) continued the industrial focus but faced severe limitations. Due to acute economic crises, wars, and food shortages, the government was forced to take a "plan holiday" in 1966, suspending the regular five-year planning cycle temporarily.
