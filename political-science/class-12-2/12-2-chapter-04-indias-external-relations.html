Political Science: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 4: India's External Relations

International Context & Constitutional Principles

India emerged as an independent nation-state in a highly challenging post-World War II global environment, characterized by decolonization, the onset of the Cold War, and domestic pressures like partition and poverty.

The Global and Domestic Setting
  • Post-WWII Reconstruction : Global devastation led to the creation of the United Nations and efforts to rebuild war-torn economies.
  • Decolonization : Collapse of European empires led to the rise of new nation-states in Asia and Africa, grappling with the twin challenges of welfare and democracy.
  • Bipolarity : Division of the world into two rival camps led by the United States (Western bloc) and the Soviet Union (Eastern bloc).
  • Domestic Pressures : Legacy of British colonial disputes, resource scarcity, partition-induced displacement, and the urgent need for poverty alleviation.
Constitutional Mandate: Article 51

India's foreign policy objectives are anchored in the Directive Principles of State Policy ( DPSP ) under Article 51 of the Indian Constitution.

Article 51: Promotion of International Peace and Security

The State shall endeavour to:

  1. Promote international peace and security;
  2. Maintain just and honourable relations between nations;
  3. Foster respect for international law and treaty obligations in the dealings of organised people with one another; and
  4. Encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration.

The Policy of Non-Alignment & Afro-Asian Unity

India's early foreign policy was dominated by the strategy of non-alignment, seeking to carve out an independent path in global affairs while fostering solidarity among newly independent Asian and African nations.

Nehru's Role in Foreign Policy

As both Prime Minister and Foreign Minister from 1946 to 1964, Jawaharlal Nehru exercised profound influence over the formulation and implementation of India's external relations.

Profile: Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964)

  • Role & Affiliation: First Prime Minister and Foreign Minister of independent India; leader of the Indian National Congress.
  • Ideological Contributions: Architect of the Non-Aligned Movement ( NAM ), champion of democratic socialism, secularism, and scientific temper.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Formulated the three core objectives of Indian foreign policy: preserving hard-earned sovereignty, protecting territorial integrity, and promoting rapid economic development through planned industrialization.
Maintaining Distance from the Two Camps
  • Military Alliances : India rejected membership in US-led NATO , SEATO , and CENTO , as well as the Soviet-led Warsaw Pact .
  • Suez Canal Crisis (1956) : India led global protests against the Anglo-French neo-colonial invasion of Egypt.
  • Hungarian Crisis (1956) : India faced domestic and international criticism for failing to publicly condemn the Soviet invasion of Hungary, illustrating the difficult balancing act of non-alignment.
  • US-Pakistan Alliance : Pakistan joined US-led military alliances in the mid-1950s, causing unease in Indo-US relations and pushing India closer to a partnership with the Soviet Union.
Afro-Asian Solidarity and the Rise of NAM
  1. Asian Relations Conference (March 1947) : Convened under Nehru's leadership five months before independence, signaling India's commitment to Asian integration.
  2. Indonesian Independence (1949) : India organized an international conference to support the Indonesian freedom struggle against Dutch colonial rule.
  3. Anti-Racism and Decolonization : India remained a staunch opponent of apartheid in South Africa and supported liberation movements globally.
  4. Bandung Conference (1955) : The Afro-Asian Conference held in Bandung, Indonesia, marked the zenith of solidarity and laid the foundation for the Non-Aligned Movement.
  5. Belgrade Summit (September 1961) : The first official summit of the Non-Aligned Movement ( NAM ), co-founded by Nehru, Tito (Yugoslavia), Nasser (Egypt), Sukarno (Indonesia), and Nkrumah (Ghana).
Peace and Conflict with China

India's relations with China transitioned from early friendship and the slogan of "Hindi-Chini Bhai-Bhai" to a bitter border war in 1962, which reshaped India's security paradigm.

The Panchsheel Agreement (1954)

Signed on 29 April 1954 by Nehru and Chinese Premier Zhou Enlai , the Panchsheel agreement established five principles of peaceful coexistence:

  • Mutual respect for each other's territorial integrity and sovereignty;
  • Mutual non-aggression;
  • Mutual non-interference in each other's internal affairs;
  • Equality and mutual benefit; and
  • Peaceful coexistence.
The Tibet Dispute and Dalai Lama's Asylum

Tibet served as a historical buffer between India and China. Its annexation by China in 1950 eliminated this buffer, leading to long-term tensions.

In 1950, China took administrative control of Tibet. While India initially conceded China's claim over Tibet in the 1954 Panchsheel agreement, an armed uprising broke out in Tibet in 1958 against Chinese occupation. In 1959, the spiritual leader Dalai Lama crossed into India seeking political asylum, which was granted by the Indian government. China strongly protested this move, accusing India of permitting anti-China activities on its soil.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: The asylum granted to the Dalai Lama marked a critical turning point. It transformed a latent boundary dispute into an active geopolitical confrontation, as Beijing viewed India's humanitarian action as a direct violation of the Panchsheel principle of non-interference in internal affairs.

The Sino-Indian War of 1962
  • Boundary Dispute : China disputed the colonial-era borders, claiming two main regions: Aksai Chin in the Ladakh region of Jammu and Kashmir, and the North-Eastern Frontier Agency ( NEFA , now Arunachal Pradesh).
  • Military Escalation : Between 1957 and 1959, China occupied Aksai Chin and built a strategic military road. In October 1962, China launched a swift, massive, and coordinated invasion on both fronts.
  • War Outcome : Chinese forces overran Indian defenses in NEFA, advancing to the edge of the Assam plains, before declaring a unilateral ceasefire and withdrawing to their pre-war positions.
  • Political and Institutional Fallout :
    • Severe blow to India's international prestige and Nehru's domestic stature.
    • Resignation of Defence Minister V.K. Krishna Menon .
    • First-ever no-confidence motion moved against the Nehru government in the Lok Sabha.
    • Split of the Communist Party of India ( CPI ) in 1964 into the pro-Soviet CPI and the pro-China CPI (Marxist) ( CPI-M ).
    • Reorganization of the Northeast, leading to statehood for Nagaland and legislative assemblies for Manipur and Tripura.

Profile: V.K. Krishna Menon (1897–1974)

  • Role & Affiliation: Diplomat, Union Cabinet Minister, and Defence Minister of India (1957–1962); close confidant of Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Ideological Contributions: Strong advocate of non-alignment and socialist planning; represented India's anti-colonial stance at the United Nations.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Led India's delegation to the UN; oversaw military modernization efforts but faced severe criticism for India's lack of military preparedness in 1962, leading to his resignation.
Media Excerpt: Cinematic Representation of the 1962 War

Haqeeqat (1964 Film)
Directed by Chetan Anand; starring Dharmendra, Priya Rajvansh, and Balraj Sahni. Set in the backdrop of the 1962 Sino-Indian War, the film portrays a small platoon of the Indian Army rescued by gypsies in Ladakh, who eventually lay down their lives resisting overwhelming Chinese forces.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: The film serves as a cultural artifact reflecting the national trauma, sense of betrayal by China, and the collective urge to honor the sacrifice of Indian soldiers despite military defeat. It highlights how the war fostered a strong wave of nationalism across the country.

Wars and Peace with Pakistan

India's relationship with Pakistan has been defined by partition-related disputes, geopolitical alignments, and a series of armed conflicts in 1947, 1965, 1971, and 1999.

Early Cooperation and the Indus Waters Treaty
  • Kashmir Proxy War (1947) : A proxy conflict broke out immediately after partition over Jammu and Kashmir, which was subsequently referred to the United Nations.
  • Humanitarian Cooperation : Both governments worked together to recover and restore women abducted during the partition violence to their families.
  • Indus Waters Treaty (1960) : Brokered by the World Bank and signed by Nehru and General Ayub Khan , this treaty successfully resolved long-term river water sharing disputes and has survived subsequent wars.
The 1965 Indo-Pak War
  1. In April 1965, Pakistan launched armed attacks in the Rann of Kutch area of Gujarat.
  2. This was followed by a larger offensive in Jammu and Kashmir in August-September 1965, designed to incite a local uprising.
  3. Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri ordered Indian troops to launch a counter-offensive across the Punjab border, with Indian forces reaching the outskirts of Lahore.
  4. The war ended with UN intervention and the signing of the Tashkent Agreement in January 1966, brokered by the Soviet Union.
The Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971
  • Internal Crisis in Pakistan : The 1970 general elections in Pakistan produced a split verdict. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto's party won in West Pakistan, while Sheikh Mujib-ur Rahman's Awami League swept East Pakistan. The military regime refused to hand over power, launching a reign of terror in East Pakistan in early 1971.
  • Refugee Crisis : Over 80 lakh (8 million) refugees fled East Pakistan into neighboring Indian states, creating massive economic and social strain.
  • Geopolitical Realignment : To counter the emerging US-Pakistan-China axis (highlighted by Henry Kissinger's secret visit to China via Pakistan in July 1971), India signed a 20-year Treaty of Peace and Friendship with the Soviet Union in August 1971.
  • The War (December 1971) : Following Pakistani pre-emptive air strikes on western Indian airfields, India launched a multi-front military campaign. Within ten days, Indian forces surrounded Dhaka, leading to the surrender of 90,000 Pakistani troops and the creation of independent Bangladesh .
  • Shimla Agreement (1972) : Signed on 3 July 1972 by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi and Zulfikar Ali Bhutto , formalizing the return of peace and bilateral resolution of disputes.
The Kargil Confrontation (1999)

In early 1999, Pakistani forces/infiltrators occupied strategic heights along the Line of Control ( LoC ) in the Mashkoh, Dras, Kaksar, and Batalik sectors.

  • Operation Vijay : Indian forces launched a military campaign to reclaim the lost positions, successfully recovering them by July 1999.
  • Nuclear Dimension : The conflict occurred just one year after both nations conducted nuclear tests, drawing intense global attention and concern over potential escalation.
  • Political Fallout in Pakistan : The conflict led to a military coup in Pakistan, with Army Chief General Pervez Musharraf overthrowing the civilian government of Nawaz Sharif.

India's Nuclear Policy

India's nuclear program has balanced a commitment to peaceful scientific development with the necessity of national security in a highly unequal global nuclear order.

Origins and Early Phase
  • Homi J. Bhabha : Initiated India's nuclear program in the late 1940s under Nehru's guidance, focusing on generating atomic energy for peaceful developmental purposes.
  • Anti-Weapon Stance : Nehru strongly advocated for global, comprehensive nuclear disarmament and pleaded with superpowers to halt nuclear testing.
The Pokhran-I Test (1974) and Global Treaties
  • Pokhran-I (May 1974) : India conducted its first nuclear explosion, termed a "peaceful explosion," asserting its commitment to using nuclear technology solely for peaceful purposes.
  • Opposition to NPT and CTBT : India refused to sign the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty ( NPT ) of 1968 and the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty ( CTBT ) of 1996, criticizing them as discriminatory treaties that monopolized nuclear weapons in the hands of five permanent UN Security Council members while denying defensive capabilities to others.
  • Domestic Context : The 1974 test coincided with severe domestic economic turmoil, high inflation caused by the 1973 Arab-Israeli War Oil Shock , and widespread political agitations, including a historic railway strike.
Pokhran-II (1998) and Nuclear Doctrine
  1. In May 1998, India conducted a series of nuclear tests at Pokhran, demonstrating its capability to use nuclear energy for military purposes.
  2. Pakistan responded with its own nuclear tests shortly after, establishing nuclear parity in South Asia.
  3. The international community imposed economic sanctions on both nations, which were eventually waived.
  4. India formulated a clear Nuclear Doctrine based on:
    • Credible minimum deterrence;
    • A strict No First Use policy; and
    • A continued commitment to global, non-discriminatory, and verifiable nuclear disarmament.
Shifting Alliances in World Politics

With changes in domestic political leadership and the end of the Cold War, India's foreign policy adapted to a unipolar world and prioritized economic interests over ideological alignments.

The Janata Party and "Genuine" Non-Alignment (1977)
  • The non-Congress Janata Party government that came to power in 1977 sought to correct the perceived pro-Soviet tilt of Indira Gandhi's administration.
  • They announced a policy of genuine non-alignment , seeking to restore balanced relations with both superpowers and initiating steps to improve ties with China.
Post-Cold War Realignment (Post-1990)
  • Collapse of the USSR : The dissolution of the Soviet Union ended the bipolar world order, forcing India to adjust its foreign policy strategy.
  • Pro-US Shift : India's foreign policy shifted toward a more pro-US strategy, driven by economic liberalization, trade, and technological cooperation.
  • Economic Pragmatism : Contemporary international relations became increasingly guided by economic interests rather than military alliances, leading to deeper trade ties with China and other global economies.
  • Indo-Pak Peace Initiatives : Despite ongoing tensions over Kashmir and the 1999 Kargil setback, both nations launched confidence-building measures, including cross-border bus and train services, to foster cultural and economic exchange.
