Political Science: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 5: Challenges to and Restoration of the Congress System

Challenge of Political Succession
From Nehru to Shastri (1964-1966)

The passing of Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru in May 1964 triggered intense speculation regarding India's democratic survival and political succession.

  • Democratic Succession : Critics feared India would succumb to military rule or political disintegration, labeling the 1960s as the "dangerous decade" due to unresolved issues like poverty, inequality, and regional divisions.
  • Consensus Candidate : K. Kamaraj, the Congress party president, facilitated a smooth transition by building a consensus in favor of Lal Bahadur Shastri , who was unanimously chosen as the next Prime Minister.
  • Shastri's Tenure : Marked by two major crises: a severe food shortage (compounded by monsoon failure) and the 1965 war with Pakistan. Shastri coined the iconic slogan Jai Jawan Jai Kisan to resolve these challenges.
  • Tashkent Agreement : Shastri's sudden demise in Tashkent (USSR) on 11 January 1966 reopened the succession question.

Profile: Lal Bahadur Shastri (1904-1966)

  • Role & Affiliation: Prime Minister of India (1964-1966); participated in the freedom movement since 1930; Minister in UP cabinet; General Secretary of Congress; Union Cabinet Minister (1951-1956, 1957-1964).
  • Ideological Contributions: Known for simplicity, commitment to principles, and high moral standards (resigned as Railway Minister accepting moral responsibility for a major railway accident).
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Coined the famous slogan Jai Jawan Jai Kisan ; signed the Tashkent Agreement with Pakistan's President Ayub Khan in 1966.
From Shastri to Indira Gandhi

The second succession in two years witnessed an intense, competitive contest within the ruling party, showcasing the maturity of Indian democracy.

  • The Contestants : A direct contest emerged between Morarji Desai (former Chief Minister of Bombay state) and Indira Gandhi (daughter of Nehru and former Union Minister for Information).
  • Secret Ballot : Senior party leaders backed Indira Gandhi, believing her political inexperience would make her dependent on them. She defeated Desai by securing the support of more than two-thirds of the party's MPs in a secret ballot.
  • Early Challenges : Indira Gandhi had to lead the party in a general election within a year of taking office, amidst a rapidly deteriorating economic situation.

Profile: Indira Gandhi (1917-1984)

  • Role & Affiliation: Prime Minister of India (1966-1977, 1980-1984); daughter of Jawaharlal Nehru; Congress President in 1959; Union Minister in Shastri's cabinet.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed populist socialism, centralized party control, and direct mobilization of marginalized social groups.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Coined the slogan Garibi Hatao ; led India to victory in the 1971 war; initiated the nationalization of banks, abolition of the Privy Purse, and India's first nuclear test.
Fourth General Elections, 1967
Context of the Elections

The 1967 elections took place under a cloud of severe economic distress and political instability, transforming India's electoral landscape.

  • Economic Crisis : Successive monsoon failures, widespread drought, decline in agricultural production, and serious food shortages led to a depletion of foreign exchange reserves.
  • Rupee Devaluation : Under pressure from the United States, the Indira Gandhi government devaluated the Indian rupee, causing its value to drop from less than Rs. 5 to more than Rs. 7 per US dollar.
  • Popular Unrest : Sharp price rises of essential commodities triggered widespread protests, bandhs, and hartals, which the government treated primarily as a law-and-order issue.
  • Agrarian Struggles : Communist and socialist parties launched agitations for greater equality, leading to armed peasant struggles and some of the worst communal riots since Independence.
Non-Congressism

Opposition parties realized that the division of their votes allowed the Congress to retain power despite losing popular support.

  • Anti-Congress Fronts : Parties with disparate ideologies joined hands to form electoral adjustments and anti-Congress fronts in various states.
  • Lohia's Strategy : Socialist leader Ram Manohar Lohia termed this strategy non-Congressism , arguing that Congress rule was undemocratic and opposed to the interests of the poor.

Profile: Ram Manohar Lohia (1910-1967)

  • Role & Affiliation: Socialist leader and thinker; freedom fighter; co-founder of the Congress Socialist Party; leader of the Samyukta Socialist Party; Member of Lok Sabha (1963-1967).
  • Ideological Contributions: Developed original non-European socialist theories; advocated for the reservation of backward classes and opposed the dominance of the English language.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Formulated the political strategy of non-Congressism ; launched sharp ideological attacks against Nehru's policies.

In the Chomu constituency of Rajasthan during the 1967 assembly elections, the local dynamics of Devisar village became entangled with the state-level contest between Congress and the Swatantra party. A local rivalry between Sher Singh and his nephew Bhim Singh led to factional alliances, where Bhim Singh built a coalition of Rajputs and non-Rajputs to secure direct access to state-level ministerial power.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This case study demonstrates how local factionalism and caste dynamics intersect with macro-level party competition. It highlights how democratic mobilization allows newer, younger leaders to challenge traditional hierarchies by building cross-caste alliances, transforming state-level political patronage networks.

Electoral Verdict: The "Political Earthquake"

The results of the 1967 elections jolted the Congress party at both the national and state levels, signaling the end of absolute Congress dominance.

  • Reduced Majority : Congress managed to retain a majority in the Lok Sabha, but with its lowest tally of seats and share of votes since 1952.
  • Defeat of Stalwarts : Half the ministers in Indira Gandhi's cabinet lost their seats, including key party figures like K. Kamaraj (Tamil Nadu), S.K. Patil (Maharashtra), and Atulya Ghosh (West Bengal).
  • Loss of States : Congress lost its majority in nine states across the country, including Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Orissa, Madras, and Kerala.
  • Rise of Regional Parties : In Madras State, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK) secured a clear majority on its own by leading a massive student-led anti-Hindi agitation.

Profile: C. Natarajan Annadurai (1909-1969)

  • Role & Affiliation: Chief Minister of Madras (Tamil Nadu) from 1967; popular writer, journalist, and orator; initially associated with the Justice Party; later formed the DMK in 1949.
  • Ideological Contributions: Champion of Dravidian culture and regional identity; strongly opposed the imposition of Hindi as an official language.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Led the anti-Hindi agitations; advocated for greater state autonomy; led the first regional party to form a majority government in an Indian state.
Coalitions and Defections
The Coalition Phenomenon

Since no single party obtained a majority in many states, various non-Congress parties formed joint legislative fronts to establish governments.

  • Samyukt Vidhayak Dal (SVD) : These coalition governments brought together ideologically incongruent partners. For instance, the SVD government in Bihar included the left-wing SSP and PSP alongside the right-wing Jana Sangh.
  • Punjab's Popular United Front : Comprised rival Akali groups, communist parties (CPI and CPI(M)), the SSP, and the Jana Sangh.
The Politics of Defection

Defections played a critical role in the making and unmaking of state governments after the 1967 elections.

  • Defection Defined : Occurs when an elected representative leaves the political party on whose symbol they were elected to join another party.
  • Aya Ram, Gaya Ram : This popular phrase emerged to describe frequent floor-crossing by legislators, leading to extreme political instability and eventually prompting constitutional amendments to curb the practice.

The expression 'Aya Ram, Gaya Ram' originated from the actions of Gaya Lal, an MLA in Haryana in 1967, who changed his party affiliation three \times within a single fortnight—moving from Congress to the United Front, back to Congress, and back to the United Front within nine hours.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This behavior highlights the lack of institutionalized party loyalty and ideological commitment in the late 1960s. It demonstrated how individual legislators could hold entire state governments hostage, leading to the eventual passage of the Anti-Defection Law (52nd Constitutional Amendment Act) to restore executive stability.

Split in the Congress
Indira vs. the 'Syndicate'

The real challenge to Indira Gandhi's authority came from within her own party, specifically from the powerful organizational faction known as the Syndicate.

  • The Syndicate : An informal group of powerful organizational leaders who controlled the Congress party machinery, led by K. Kamaraj and including S.K. Patil, S. Nijalingappa, N. Sanjeeva Reddy, and Atulya Ghosh.
  • Sidelining the Old Guard : Indira Gandhi chose her trusted advisers from outside the party structure, gradually sidelining the Syndicate to assert her independence.
  • Ideological Shift : To counter the Syndicate, she converted a internal power struggle into an ideological battle, adopting a distinct Left orientation. She introduced the Ten Point Programme in May 1967, which included social control of banks, nationalization of general insurance, and land reforms.

Profile: K. Kamaraj (1903-1975)

  • Role & Affiliation: Freedom fighter; Congress President; Chief Minister of Madras (Tamil Nadu).
  • Ideological Contributions: Focused on spreading primary education and addressing social deprivation.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Introduced the mid-day meal scheme for schoolchildren; proposed the "Kamaraj Plan" in 1963, suggesting senior Congress leaders resign from office to work for the party organization.

Profile: S. Nijalingappa (1902-2000)

  • Role & Affiliation: Senior Congress leader; Member of the Constituent Assembly; Chief Minister of Mysore (Karnataka); President of Congress (1968-1971).
  • Ideological Contributions: Regarded as the maker of modern Karnataka; advocated for organizational discipline and traditional party structures.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Led the anti-Indira faction (Congress-O) during the historic 1969 split, formally expelling her from the party.

Profile: Karpoori Thakur (1924-1988)

  • Role & Affiliation: Chief Minister of Bihar (1970-1971, 1977-1979); freedom fighter and prominent socialist leader.
  • Ideological Contributions: Staunch follower of Ram Manohar Lohia; active in labor and peasant movements; strongly opposed the official use of the English language.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Introduced landmark reservations for backward classes in Bihar during his second tenure as Chief Minister.
The Presidential Election of 1969

The factional rivalry between Indira Gandhi and the Syndicate culminated in a formal split during the 1969 Presidential election.

  • The Candidates : Following the death of President Zakir Hussain, the Syndicate nominated N. Sanjeeva Reddy as the official Congress candidate. Indira Gandhi retaliated by encouraging Vice-President V.V. Giri to contest as an independent candidate.
  • Conscience Vote : The Congress President issued a whip to vote for Reddy, but Indira Gandhi openly called for a "conscience vote," allowing MPs and MLAs to vote freely.
  • The Split : V.V. Giri won the election. The defeat of the official candidate led to the expulsion of Indira Gandhi from the party, formalizing the split into Congress (O) (led by the Syndicate) and Congress (R) (led by Indira Gandhi).

Profile: V.V. Giri (1894-1980)

  • Role & Affiliation: President of India (1969-1974); Vice-President (1967-1969); Union Labour Minister; Governor of Uttar Pradesh, Kerala, and Mysore.
  • Ideological Contributions: Prominent trade unionist and labor leader from Andhra Pradesh; advocated for workers' rights and welfare.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Resigned as Vice-President to contest the 1969 presidential election as an independent; won with the backing of Indira Gandhi.
Abolition of the Privy Purse

The abolition of hereditary privileges became a major ideological battleground between Indira Gandhi and her conservative opponents.

  • Privy Purse : A hereditary government allowance and private property retention granted to former rulers of princely states at the time of integration.
  • Constitutional Challenge : Indira Gandhi supported the demand to abolish these privileges as they violated the principles of equality. Morarji Desai opposed the move, calling it a "breach of faith."
  • Resolution : A constitutional amendment failed in the Rajya Sabha in 1970, and an executive ordinance was struck down by the Supreme Court. Indira Gandhi made it a major campaign issue in 1971, eventually passing the amendment after her landslide victory.
The 1971 Election and Restoration of Congress
The Contest and Slogans

The 1971 Lok Sabha elections presented a direct clash of political strategies, programs, and slogans.

  • The Grand Alliance : All major non-communist, non-Congress opposition parties (including SSP, PSP, BJS, Swatantra, and BKD) formed a formidable electoral coalition.
  • Garibi Hatao : While the Grand Alliance focused on the single-point agenda of "Indira Hatao" (Remove Indira), Indira Gandhi countered with the positive, populist slogan Garibi Hatao (Remove Poverty).
  • Social Support Base : Through this slogan, she built a direct, nationwide support base among the landless laborers, Dalits, Adivasis, minorities, and women.
The Outcome and Aftermath

The results of the 1971 elections dramatically restored the dominant position of the Congress in Indian politics.

  • Landslide Victory : The Congress(R)-CPI alliance won 375 seats in the Lok Sabha, with Indira Gandhi's Congress(R) alone winning 352 seats (securing 44% of the popular vote).
  • Collapse of Opposition : The Congress(O) won only 16 seats, and the Grand Alliance failed completely, securing less than 60 seats combined.
  • 1971 War and 1972 Sweep : India's victory in the 1971 Indo-Pak war and the creation of Bangladesh immensely boosted Indira Gandhi's popularity. Her party swept the State Assembly elections in 1972, consolidating power across the country.
Restoration?

While Indira Gandhi restored the dominant position of the Congress, she fundamentally altered the nature of the Congress system itself.

  • Re-invented Party : The restored Congress was not a revival of the old party. It relied entirely on the popularity of a single supreme leader and possessed a weak organizational structure.
  • Lack of Factional Accommodation : Unlike the old Congress, which accommodated diverse opinions and internal factions, the new Congress was highly centralized and did not tolerate internal dissent.
  • Shrinking Democratic Space : While popular among specific marginalized groups, the party's capacity to absorb tensions shrank, leading to growing popular unrest and economic deprivation that culminated in a major political crisis.

The 1973 Hindi film 'Zanjeer', directed by Prakash Mehra and starring Amitabh Bachchan, introduced the archetype of the 'angry young man'. The protagonist, Vijay, a police officer framed on false charges, takes the law into his own hands to fight anti-social elements.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: The film reflected the deep public frustration, erosion of moral values, and growing disillusionment with state institutions during the early 1970s. It captured the volcanic eruption of protest against an indifferent political-economic system, mirroring the real-world popular unrest that preceded the Emergency.
