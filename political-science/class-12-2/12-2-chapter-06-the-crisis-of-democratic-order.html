Political Science: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 6: The Crisis of Democratic Order

Background to Emergency

The period leading up to 1975 in Indian politics was marked by intense polarization, bitter party competition, and growing friction between the executive and the judiciary. Indira Gandhi had emerged as a highly popular and powerful leader, but her style of governance led to accusations of personalization of authority and centralization of power.

Economic Context
  • Garibi Hatao Slogan vs. Reality: Despite the popular 1971 slogan, the economic situation deteriorated significantly after 1971-72.
  • The Bangladesh Crisis : The influx of nearly eight million refugees from East Pakistan put an immense strain on India's economy, followed by a costly war with Pakistan.
  • International Pressures: The United States government stopped all aid to India post-war, and the global oil shock led to a massive increase in commodity prices.
  • Inflation and Hardship: Inflation rates soared to 23% in 1973 and 30% in 1974, causing severe distress to the common people.
  • Government Austerity: To curb expenditure, the government froze the salaries of its employees, leading to widespread dissatisfaction.
  • Agricultural Failure: Monsoons failed in 1972-73, resulting in an 8% decline in food grain productivity and severe food scarcity.
Gujarat and Bihar Movements
  • Gujarat Movement (January 1974): Students protested against rising prices of food grains, cooking oil, and corruption. The agitation was joined by opposition parties, leading to the imposition of President's rule. Morarji Desai went on an indefinite fast demanding fresh elections, which were held in June 1975, resulting in a defeat for the Congress.
  • Bihar Movement (March 1974): Students protested against food scarcity, unemployment, and corruption. They invited veteran leader Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) to lead them. JP accepted on the condition that the movement remain non-violent and expand nationally, calling for a Total Revolution (Sampoorna Kranti) in social, economic, and political spheres.
Railway Strike of 1974

In May 1974, the National Coordination Committee for Railwaymen's Struggle, led by George Fernandes , organized a nationwide strike of railway employees demanding bonus and better service conditions. The government declared the strike illegal, deployed the territorial army, and arrested key leaders, forcing the strike to be called off after 20 days without a settlement.

Conflict with the Judiciary

The executive and the judiciary clashed over several constitutional issues, including whether Parliament could abridge Fundamental Rights or curtail the Right to Property. This culminated in the landmark Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973), which established the Basic Structure Doctrine . Tensions escalated when the government bypassed three senior judges to appoint Justice A. N. Ray as the Chief Justice of India.

Declaration of Emergency
The Allahabad High Court Judgment

On 12 June 1975, Justice Jagmohan Lal Sinha of the Allahabad High Court declared Indira Gandhi's 1971 election to the Lok Sabha invalid on the grounds of using government machinery for her campaign. This meant she could not remain Prime Minister unless re-elected within six months. On 24 June, the Supreme Court granted a partial stay, allowing her to remain an MP but barring her from participating in Lok Sabha proceedings.

Crisis and Response

On 25 June 1975, the opposition led by Jayaprakash Narayan organized a massive rally at Delhi's Ramlila grounds, demanding Indira Gandhi's resignation and calling for a nationwide satyagraha. JP urged the police, military, and government servants to refuse to obey "illegal and immoral" orders.

The Proclamation

In response to the growing political challenge, the government declared a state of emergency on the night of 25 June 1975, invoking Article 352 of the Constitution on the grounds of "internal disturbance." President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed signed the proclamation. Electricity to major newspaper offices was cut off, and prominent opposition leaders were arrested overnight.

Consequences of the Emergency
Censorship and Suspensions
  • Press Censorship : Newspapers were required to get prior approval from the government before publishing any material.
  • Banning of Organizations: The government banned the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS) and Jamait-e-Islami .
  • Suspension of Fundamental Rights: Citizens lost the right to move courts for the restoration of their Fundamental Rights, including the Right to Life and Liberty.
  • Preventive Detention : Thousands of political workers and leaders were arrested and detained without being charged, purely on the apprehension that they might commit an offense.
Judicial Challenges and the Habeas Corpus Case

While several High Courts ruled that they could still entertain writs of habeas corpus during the Emergency, the Supreme Court overturned these decisions in April 1976 (the controversial ADM Jabalpur Case ), ruling that the government could suspend a citizen's right to life and liberty during an Emergency.

Dissent and Constitutional Amendments

Despite the crackdown, underground newsletters circulated, and writers like Shivarama Karanth and Fanishwarnath Renu returned their state awards in protest. Meanwhile, the Parliament passed the 42nd Amendment , which extended the term of the legislatures from five to six years and protected the elections of the Prime Minister, President, and Vice-President from judicial review.

Lessons of the Emergency

The Emergency highlighted both the vulnerabilities and the strengths of Indian democracy. It demonstrated that while democratic processes can be temporarily suspended, it is extremely difficult to permanently dismantle democracy in India.

Constitutional and Institutional Reforms
  • The term "internal disturbance" was replaced with "armed rebellion" for invoking Article 352.
  • The President can only declare an Emergency on the written advice of the Union Cabinet.
  • Civil liberties organizations grew rapidly, and the judiciary became far more proactive in protecting individual rights post-Emergency.
Politics after Emergency & Legacy
The 1977 Lok Sabha Elections

In January 1977, the government announced elections and released political prisoners. The opposition parties united to form the Janata Party under the leadership of JP. The election became a referendum on the Emergency, resulting in a historic defeat for the Congress, which won only 154 seats. Indira Gandhi and her son Sanjay Gandhi were both defeated in their constituencies.

The Janata Government and Its Collapse

The Janata Party government, led by Morarji Desai, was plagued by internal factionalism and leadership struggles between Desai, Charan Singh , and Jagjivan Ram . The government collapsed in less than 28 months. Charan Singh formed a brief government with Congress support, which was quickly withdrawn, leading to mid-term elections in 1980 where the Congress returned to power with a massive majority.

Key Political Figures & Thinker Profiles

Profile: Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) (1902-1979)

  • Role & Affiliation: Founder General Secretary of the Congress Socialist Party; leader of the Bihar Movement; symbol of anti-Emergency opposition.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed the concept of "Total Revolution" (Sampoorna Kranti) encompassing social, economic, and political spheres to establish true democracy.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Led the massive people's march to Parliament in 1975 and acted as the moral guide for the formation of the Janata Party.

Profile: Morarji Desai (1896-1995)

  • Role & Affiliation: Freedom fighter; leader of Congress (O); Prime Minister of India (1977-1979).
  • Ideological Contributions: Proponent of Gandhian principles, Khadi, naturopathy, and prohibition.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Led the first non-Congress government at the Center; served as Chief Minister of Bombay State and Deputy Prime Minister.

Profile: Chaudhary Charan Singh (1902-1987)

  • Role & Affiliation: Prime Minister of India (July 1979 - January 1980); founder of Bharatiya Kranti Dal and Lok Dal.
  • Ideological Contributions: Strong advocate for rural and agricultural development; champion of peasant interests.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Served as Chief Minister of Uttar Pradesh and Deputy Prime Minister in the Janata government.

Profile: Jagjivan Ram (1908-1986)

  • Role & Affiliation: Freedom fighter and Dalit leader from Bihar; Deputy Prime Minister (1977-1979); founder of Congress for Democracy.
  • Ideological Contributions: Dedicated to social equality, labor welfare, and the upliftment of backward classes.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Member of the Constituent Assembly; served as India's first Labor Minister and held various key portfolios from 1952 to 1977.
Conceptual Definition & Characterization Matrix
Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Emergency A constitutional state of exception where normal democratic politics are suspended and power is centralized in the Union government. Article 352 of the Indian Constitution. To protect national sovereignty and security against external threats or severe internal disturbances.
Press Censorship The requirement for media houses to obtain prior government approval before publishing any content. Emergency executive decrees. To control the flow of information, suppress dissent, and maintain public order during a crisis.
Preventive Detention The arrest and detention of individuals based on the apprehension that they may commit an offense, rather than for an actual crime. Preventive detention acts (e.g., MISA). To preemptively neutralize political opposition and prevent public unrest.
Basic Structure Doctrine The constitutional principle that certain core features of the Constitution cannot be amended by Parliament. Supreme Court ruling in the Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973). To limit parliamentary supremacy and preserve the foundational democratic character of the state.

Narrative Case Studies & Media Excerpts

The film "Hazaaron Khwaishein Aisi" (2005), directed by Sudhir Mishra, is set against the backdrop of the turbulent 1970s. It follows three students—Siddharth, Vikram, and Geeta—as they navigate the political idealism, student movements, and the subsequent state crackdown during the Emergency. Siddharth pursues revolutionary social transformation, while Vikram becomes a political fixer, reflecting the disillusionment and systemic shifts of the era.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: The film illustrates how the political crisis of the 1970s deeply impacted the youth, shifting their aspirations from democratic idealism to either radical extra-constitutional struggles or cynical political opportunism. It highlights the tension between institutional democracy and spontaneous popular movements that the existing party system failed to accommodate.

On 27 June 1975, the Hindi daily "Nai Dunia" published its editorial page with a completely blank space where the editorial column usually appeared. This was a silent protest against the government's imposition of press censorship. Several other newspapers, including the Indian Express and The Statesman, adopted similar blank-space protests until the government eventually banned the practice of leaving blank spaces.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: The blank editorial protest represents a powerful form of non-violent resistance against authoritarian overreach. It exposed the limits of constitutional dictatorship by turning the state's own censorship tools into a visible symbol of the loss of freedom of expression, mobilizing public opinion against the regime.
