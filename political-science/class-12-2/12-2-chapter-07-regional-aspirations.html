Political Science: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 7: Regional Aspirations

Region and the Nation

The 1980s in India witnessed a significant rise in regional aspirations for autonomy, often leading to armed conflicts, state repression, and negotiated accords. The Indian approach to diversity differs fundamentally from European models by rejecting the idea that cultural diversity is a threat to national unity.

Core Political Concept Baseline Definition / Entitlement Source of Authority / Basis Democratic Function & Purpose
Regionalism The political expression of regional identity, culture, and local interests within a nation-state. Linguistic, cultural, and geographical distinctiveness. Ensures local issues receive adequate attention and representation in national policy-making.
Autonomy The right of a region to self-govern and manage internal affairs within the constitutional framework. Federal provisions, Article 370 (historical), and the Sixth Schedule of the Constitution. Balances regional self-determination with national sovereignty and territorial integrity.
Secessionism The demand for complete separation from the nation-state to form an independent sovereign country. Extreme alienation, perceived cultural threat, or historical claims of independence. Challenges the nation-state, often resolved through democratic negotiations and accommodation.
The Indian Approach to Diversity
  • Democratic Accommodation : India adopted a democratic framework that allows political expressions of regional aspirations rather than treating them as anti-national.
  • Unity in Diversity : Indian nationalism seeks to balance national unity with regional distinctiveness, ensuring the nation does not negate the region.
  • Constitutional Flexibility : The use of special provisions (e.g., Article 371, Sixth Schedule) to accommodate unique regional requirements.
Areas of Tension and Early Challenges

Immediately after Independence, India faced severe challenges regarding national integration, leading to various regional agitations and demands for internal boundary reorganization.

Chronology of Early Regional Reorganisation
  1. 1950s : Mass agitations for linguistic states lead to the creation of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra, and Gujarat.
  2. 1950s-1960s : Protests in Tamil Nadu against making Hindi the official national language of India.
  3. 1966 : Creation of Punjab and Haryana after a long struggle for a Punjabi-speaking state.
  4. Later Years : Creation of smaller states like Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand, and Jharkhand to address regional developmental imbalances.
Jammu and Kashmir

The region of Jammu and Kashmir has been a major source of external and internal political conflict since 1947, characterized by constitutional disputes, cross-border militancy, and political instability.

Profile: Sheikh Mohammad Abdullah (1905–1982)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of Jammu and Kashmir, founder of the National Conference, Prime Minister/Chief Minister of J&K.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed Kashmiriyat (secular Kashmiri identity), led popular struggles against princely rule, and opposed joining Pakistan due to its non-secular character.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Initiated major land reforms in J&K; negotiated the 1974 Accord with Indira Gandhi to return as Chief Minister.
Roots of the Kashmir Problem
  • Princely State Status : Before 1947, ruled by Maharaja Hari Singh, who desired independent status rather than merging with India or Pakistan.
  • Tribal Invasion (1947) : Pakistan sent tribal infiltrators to capture Kashmir, forcing the Maharaja to sign the Instrument of Accession with India in October 1947.
  • Article 370 : Granted provisional autonomy to Jammu and Kashmir, which became a major point of constitutional and political debate.
  • Internal Divisions : J&K comprises three distinct social and political regions: Hindu-majority Jammu, Muslim-majority Kashmir Valley, and Buddhist/Muslim-populated Ladakh.
Politics and Insurgency Since 1948
  • Dismissal of 1953 : Sheikh Abdullah was dismissed and detained due to growing differences with the central government over Kashmir's status.
  • 1987 Assembly Elections : Widely believed to be rigged, leading to massive popular resentment and the rise of armed insurgency supported by Pakistan.
  • President's Rule : J&K was placed under direct central rule for several years starting in 1990, accompanied by intense counter-insurgency operations.
  • 2019 Reorganisation : On 5 August 2019, Article 370 was abrogated, and the state was bifurcated into two Union Territories: Jammu & Kashmir, and Ladakh.

The 1992 Tamil film "Roja", directed by Mani Ratnam, depicts the kidnapping of a cryptologist by militants in Kashmir against the backdrop of the Indo-Pakistan dispute.

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: The film reflects the national anxiety surrounding Kashmiri militancy in the early 1990s, highlighting the human cost of conflict, the rise of cross-border terrorism, and the complex emotional integration of the region with the rest of India.

The Dravidian Movement

One of India's earliest and most successful regional movements, the Dravidian movement in southern India used democratic means to assert regional pride and oppose northern hegemony.

Profile: E.V. Ramasami Naicker 'Periyar' (1879–1973)

  • Role & Affiliation: Social reformer, founder of the Self-Respect Movement and the Dravidar Kazhagam (DK).
  • Ideological Contributions: Strong proponent of atheism, anti-caste struggle, and Dravidian identity; opposed Brahmin dominance and northern cultural imperialism.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Propounded the thesis that North Indians and Brahmins were Aryans, mobilizing the non-Brahmin population of the South.
Evolution and Political Impact
  • Slogan : "Vadakku Vaazhgiradhu; Therkku Thaeikiradhu" (The north thrives even as the south decays) mobilized regional sentiments.
  • Three-Pronged Agitation (1953-54) : Opposed the renaming of Kallakudi railway station to Dalmiapuram, demanded greater importance for Tamil history in schools, and opposed the state's craft education scheme.
  • Anti-Hindi Agitation (1965) : Massive protests against making Hindi the sole official language led to the rise of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam (DMK) to power in 1967.
  • Bipolar State Politics : Since 1967, Tamil Nadu politics has been dominated by Dravidian parties (DMK and AIADMK), demonstrating the compatibility of regionalism with national integration.
Punjab and the Akali Movement

The regional movement in Punjab evolved from a demand for linguistic reorganisation into a violent struggle for autonomy and a separate Sikh state, eventually resolved through democratic accommodation.

Profile: Master Tara Singh (1885–1967)

  • Role & Affiliation: Prominent Sikh religious and political leader, senior leader of the Shiromani Gurudwara Prabandhak Committee (SGPC) and the Akali Dal.
  • Ideological Contributions: Strong advocate for the creation of a separate Punjabi-speaking state ( Punjabi Suba ).
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Led the Akali movement during and after the freedom struggle, opposing Congress policies that ignored Sikh minority interests.

Profile: Sant Harchand Singh Longowal (1932–1985)

  • Role & Affiliation: Sikh political and religious leader, President of the Akali Dal in 1980.
  • Ideological Contributions: Advocated for moderate political solutions and negotiated settlements within the Indian constitutional framework.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Signed the historic Rajiv Gandhi-Longowal Accord in July 1985; assassinated by Sikh extremists shortly after.
The Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973)
  • Asserted regional autonomy and demanded a redefinition of Centre-State relations.
  • Spoke of the aspirations of the Sikh qaum (community/nation) and declared the goal of attaining the bolbala (dominance) of Sikhs.
  • Had limited appeal among the masses initially but became a focal point of controversy as critics labeled it secessionist.
Cycle of Violence and Operation Blue Star
  • Rise of Extremism : Leadership of the Akali movement shifted to extremist elements who turned the Golden Temple in Amritsar into an armed fortress.
  • Operation Blue Star (June 1984) : Military action ordered by the Government of India to flush out militants from the Golden Temple, deeply hurting Sikh religious sentiments.
  • Assassination of Indira Gandhi : On 31 October 1984, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi was assassinated by her Sikh bodyguards.
  • 1984 Anti-Sikh Riots : Led to the systematic killing of over two thousand Sikhs in Delhi and other parts of northern India.

"I have no hesitation in apologising not only to the Sikh community but the whole Indian nation because what took place in 1984 is the negation of the concept of nationhood..." — Prime Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh, Rajya Sabha debate, 11 August 2005

Political Analysis & Systemic Significance: This historic apology by India's first Sikh Prime Minister served as a vital step toward national reconciliation, acknowledging state failures in protecting minority citizens and reinforcing the democratic commitment to healing historical wounds.

The North-East: Autonomy, Secession, and Outsiders

The North-East region, connected to the rest of India by a narrow 22-kilometer corridor, has faced complex political challenges categorized into three distinct issues: demands for autonomy, secessionist movements, and opposition to 'outsiders'.

Profile: Laldenga (1937–1990)

  • Role & Affiliation: Founder and leader of the Mizo National Front (MNF), Chief Minister of Mizoram.
  • Ideological Contributions: Led an armed secessionist struggle against India for two decades following the state's failure during the 1959 famine.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Signed the Mizo Peace Accord with Rajiv Gandhi in 1986, leading to Mizoram's statehood and his transition to democratic Chief Minister.

Profile: Angami Zapu Phizo (1904–1990)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the Naga national movement, President of the Naga National Council (NNC).
  • Ideological Contributions: Pioneered the demand for an independent sovereign Nagaland, rejecting any integration with the Indian Union.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Declared Naga independence in 1951; led an armed guerrilla struggle and spent his final decades in exile in the UK.
Three Major Political Dimensions
  • Demands for Autonomy : Non-Assamese communities opposed the imposition of the Assamese language, leading to the creation of Meghalaya, Mizoram, and Arunachal Pradesh out of the composite state of Assam.
  • Secessionist Movements : Mizoram and Nagaland witnessed long-drawn armed insurgencies demanding complete independence, which were met with military action and eventual peace accords.
  • Movements Against Outsiders : The Assam Movement (1979–1985) , led by the All Assam Students’ Union (AASU), targeted illegal migration from Bangladesh, culminating in the signing of the Assam Accord in 1985.
Sikkim's Merger and Goa's Liberation

The integration of Sikkim and Goa into the Indian Union represents two unique historical processes of decolonization and democratic expansion.

Profile: Kazi Lhendup Dorji Khangsarpa (1904–2007)

  • Role & Affiliation: Leader of the democratic movement in Sikkim, founder of the Sikkim National Congress.
  • Ideological Contributions: Opposed the autocratic rule of the Chogyal and advocated for Sikkim's integration with democratic India.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Led the Sikkim Congress to a landslide victory in the 1974 elections, paving the way for Sikkim to become the 22nd state of India in 1975.
Sikkim's Integration (1975)
  • At independence, Sikkim was an Indian protectorate ruled by the Chogyal monarch.
  • The majority Nepali population felt dominated by the minority Bhutia-Lepcha elite and demanded democratic reforms.
  • In 1975, the Sikkim Assembly passed a resolution asking for full integration, which was overwhelmingly approved by a popular referendum.
Goa's Liberation (1961) and Opinion Poll (1967)
  • Portugal refused to withdraw from Goa, Diu, and Daman after 1947, suppressing local civil rights movements.
  • In December 1961, the Government of India launched Operation Vijay , liberating the territories after a brief military action.
  • In January 1967, the historic Goa Opinion Poll was held, where citizens voted to remain a separate Union Territory rather than merging with Maharashtra. Goa attained full statehood in 1987.
Lessons for National Integration

The resolution of various regional crises in India offers critical insights into the functioning of a diverse, large-scale democracy.

Profile: Rajiv Gandhi (1944–1991)

  • Role & Affiliation: Prime Minister of India (1984–1989), leader of the Indian National Congress.
  • Ideological Contributions: Championed political reconciliation through negotiated accords to resolve long-standing regional insurgencies.
  • Key Policies / Milestones: Signed the Punjab Accord (1985), Assam Accord (1985), and Mizo Accord (1986); assassinated by an LTTE suicide bomber in 1991.
Four Key Democratic Lessons
  1. Aspirations are Democratic : Regional aspirations are a normal part of democratic politics and must be accommodated rather than suppressed.
  2. Negotiation over Force : The best response to regional movements is through democratic dialogue and constitutional settlements rather than military suppression.
  3. Power Sharing : True integration requires sharing power at both the state and national levels, giving regional groups a stake in national decision-making.
  4. Economic Balance : Addressing regional economic imbalances is crucial to preventing feelings of discrimination and alienation among backward regions.
