Psychology: Class 11 — Chapter 2: Methods of Enquiry in Psychology

Goals of Psychological Enquiry

Like any scientific research, psychological enquiry aims to describe, predict, explain, control, and apply knowledge about behavior and mental processes in an objective manner.

Core Goals of Enquiry

  • Description: Attempting to describe a behavior or phenomenon as accurately as possible to distinguish it from other behaviors (e.g., defining study habits).
  • Prediction: Forecasting the occurrence of a behavior under specific conditions based on established relationships between variables.
  • Explanation: Identifying the causal factors or determinants that make a behavior occur, establishing cause-effect relationships.
  • Control: Making a behavior happen, reducing it, or enhancing it by manipulating its antecedent conditions.
  • Application: Bringing about positive changes in people's lives by solving real-world problems (e.g., using yoga to reduce stress).

Steps in Conducting Scientific Research

Scientific research follows a systematic, objective, and testable procedure consisting of four major steps:

  1. Conceptualising a Problem: Selecting a theme or topic, narrowing the focus, and developing a tentative answer called a hypothesis.
  2. Collecting Data: Developing a research design (blueprint) regarding participants, methods of data collection, tools, and procedures.
  3. Drawing Conclusions: Analyzing collected data using statistical procedures (e.g., graphical representations, measures of central tendency) to verify the hypothesis.
  4. Revising Research Conclusions: Confirming, revising, or stating alternative hypotheses based on whether the data supports the initial theory.

Alternative Paradigms of Research

  • Scientific Paradigm: Assumes human behavior is predictable, caused by internal/external forces, and can be objectively observed, measured, and controlled. It focuses primarily on overt behavior.
  • Interpretive Paradigm: Emphasizes understanding subjective experiences, feelings, and meanings over mere explanation and prediction. It views reality as subjectively constructed and context-dependent.
Nature of Psychological Data

Psychological data are not independent entities; they are deeply embedded in physical, social, and temporal contexts. They approximate reality and serve to verify or falsify hypotheses.

Some Important Methods in Psychology

Observational Method

A powerful tool for describing behavior, consisting of selection, recording, and analysis of data.

  • Naturalistic vs. Controlled Observation: Naturalistic observation occurs in real-life settings without observer manipulation, while controlled observation takes place in highly regulated environments like laboratories.
  • Non-Participant vs. Participant Observation: In non-participant observation, the researcher observes from a distance without interfering. In participant observation, the researcher becomes an active part of the group being observed.

Experimental Method

Conducted to establish cause-effect relationships between variables in a controlled setting.

  • Independent Variable: The variable manipulated or altered by the researcher (the cause).
  • Dependent Variable: The variable on which the effect of manipulation is observed (the effect).
  • Experimental Group: The group exposed to the independent variable manipulation.
  • Control Group: The comparison group treated identically except that the manipulated variable is absent.

Case/Experiment Title: Bystander Intervention in Emergencies (Smoke Study)

Background/Subject: Columbia University students tested individually in a laboratory setting by Bibb Latane and John Darley (1970).

Method/Procedure: Students filled out a questionnaire in a waiting room. Conditions varied: waiting alone, waiting with two other naive students, or waiting with two passive confederates. Smoke was then introduced into the room through a wall vent.

Observations/Results: 75% of students waiting alone reported the smoke. Only 38% of those in groups of three naive students reported it. Only 10% reported it when waiting with passive confederates.

Psychological Inference: The presence of other people strongly inhibits the reporting of an emergency due to diffusion of responsibility.

Correlational Research

Determines the strength and direction of the relationship between two variables without manipulating them. The relationship is represented by a correlation coefficient ranging from +1.0 through 0.0 to -1.0.

Survey Research

Used to study opinions, attitudes, and social facts using structured techniques.

  • Personal Interviews: Can be structured (standardized questions, close-ended) or unstructured (flexible, open-ended).
  • Questionnaires: Self-report instruments consisting of predetermined open-ended or closed-ended questions.
  • Telephone Surveys: Quick data collection method, though prone to uncooperativeness and sampling bias.

Case/Experiment Title: What Makes the People of India Happy?

Background/Subject: 817 individuals aged 25-55 across eight major Indian cities surveyed in December 2004.

Method/Procedure: Survey conducted by "Outlook Saptahik" magazine using a questionnaire with 5-point scales and multiple-choice questions.

Observations/Results: 47% reported being extremely happy, 28% more or less happy. 80% expressed that money cannot buy happiness. Over 50% reported peace of mind and health as sources of maximum happiness.

Psychological Inference: Subjective well-being in this demographic is heavily tied to non-materialistic factors like peace of mind, health, and family.

Psychological Testing

An objective and standardized instrument used to assess an individual's standing in relation to others on mental or behavioral characteristics.

  • Reliability: Consistency of scores obtained by an individual on the same test on different occasions (test-retest) or internal consistency (split-half).
  • Validity: The degree to which a test measures what it claims to measure.
  • Norms: Standardized average performance levels of a target group used for comparison.
  • Types of Tests: Classified by language (verbal, non-verbal, performance), administration (individual, group), and difficulty (speed, power).

Case Study

An in-depth, detailed investigation of a single individual, group, institution, or specific event using multiple data collection methods (interviews, observations, tests).

Analysis of Data

Quantitative Method

Converts psychological attributes into numbers. It utilizes statistical methods (mean, median, mode, standard deviation, correlation) to analyze close-ended responses and draw objective conclusions.

Qualitative Method

Analyzes descriptive, non-numerical data (narratives, field notes, unstructured interviews). It uses techniques like Narrative Analysis and content analysis to identify thematic categories and subjective meanings.

Limitations & Ethical Issues

Limitations of Psychological Enquiry

  1. Lack of True Zero Point: Unlike physical sciences, psychological measurements do not start from absolute zero (e.g., no one has zero intelligence). Scores are relative.
  2. Relative Nature of Psychological Tools: Tests are context-bound and culturally specific; a test designed for urban students may not be valid for tribal children.
  3. Subjective Interpretation of Qualitative Data: Qualitative analysis is highly susceptible to researcher bias and subjective interpretation.

Ethical Issues in Psychological Research

  • Voluntary Participation: Participants must have the freedom to choose whether to participate and the right to withdraw at any stage.
  • Informed Consent: Participants must be fully informed about the study's nature and potential risks before data collection begins.
  • Debriefing: Reassuring and providing necessary information to participants after the study, especially if deception was used.
  • Sharing the Results: The moral duty of the researcher to share findings with the participants.
  • Confidentiality: Safeguarding participants' privacy by keeping all collected data strictly confidential.
