Class 11 — Chapter 3: Human Development

Introduction & Meaning of Development

Development is the pattern of progressive, orderly, and predictable changes that begin at conception and continue throughout life. It involves both growth (gains) and decline (decay/loss), as observed in old age.

Key Developmental Concepts

  • Growth: Refers to an increase in the physical size of body parts or the organism as a whole, which can be quantitatively measured.
  • Development: A broader, qualitative process by which an individual grows and changes throughout the life cycle, characterized by an orderly and directional relationship between stages.
  • Maturation: Biological changes that follow an orderly sequence dictated by a genetic blueprint (e.g., sitting, standing, walking).
  • Evolution: Species-specific changes driven by natural selection over generations.

Life-Span Perspective (LSP)

The study of human development is guided by several core assumptions:

  1. Development is Lifelong: It takes place across all age groups, from conception to old age.
  2. Multi-dimensionality & Multi-directionality: Biological, cognitive, and socio-emotional processes are interwoven. Some dimensions increase while others decrease with age.
  3. Plasticity: Skills and abilities can be modified or improved throughout the life course.
  4. Historical Context: Development is influenced by historical conditions and generational cohorts.
  5. Multi-disciplinary: It is studied by psychologists, anthropologists, sociologists, and neuroscientists.
  6. Contextual: Individuals respond to and act within physical, social, historical, and cultural contexts.
Factors Influencing Development

Human development is shaped by the continuous interaction of heredity (nature) and environment (nurture).

Genotype vs. Phenotype

  • Genotype: The actual genetic material or genetic heritage inherited from parents.
  • Phenotype: The observable and measurable physical and psychological characteristics of an individual, resulting from the interaction of the genotype with the environment.

Gene-Environment Correlations

According to Sandra Scarr, parents provide environments that match their own genetic predispositions, and children also actively select niches in their environment that correspond to their genotypes (e.g., an athletically inclined child seeking out sports environments).

Context of Development

Development does not occur in a vacuum; it is deeply embedded in socio-cultural environments.

Bronfenbrenner's Contextual View

Urie Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory views development as influenced by five nested environmental systems:

  1. Microsystem: The immediate setting in which the individual lives and directly interacts with social agents (family, peers, school, neighborhood).
  2. Mesosystem: Relations or links between microsystems (e.g., parent-teacher relations).
  3. Exosystem: Settings in which the developing person is not active but that affect their immediate context (e.g., parental job transfers).
  4. Macrosystem: The overarching cultural values, beliefs, laws, and customs of the society.
  5. Chronosystem: The socio-historical events and transitions across the life course (e.g., parental divorce, economic shifts).

Sinha's Ecological Model

Durganand Sinha proposed an ecological model for the Indian context, consisting of two concentric layers:

  • Upper/Visible Layer: Home, school, peer groups, and immediate physical amenities.
  • Surrounding Layer: General geographical environment, institutional settings (caste, class), and macro-level amenities.
Overview of Developmental Stages

1. Prenatal Stage

The period from conception to birth, lasting approximately 40 weeks. It is highly vulnerable to environmental hazards called Teratogens (drugs, infections, radiation, and pollution) which can cause developmental abnormalities or death.

2. Infancy

The first two years of life, characterized by rapid brain growth, motor development, and sensory maturation.

Cognitive Development (Piaget)

Infants are in the Sensorimotor Stage (0-2 years). They explore the world through sensory experiences and motor actions. A key milestone is Object Permanence—the understanding that objects continue to exist even when they cannot be seen, heard, or touched.

Socio-emotional Development & Attachment

Infants form a deep emotional bond with caregivers, known as Attachment. Erik Erikson emphasized that the first year of life is critical for establishing Trust vs. Mistrust based on sensitive caregiving.

Case/Experiment Title: Harlow and Harlow's Surrogate Mother Study (1962)

Background/Subject: Infant rhesus monkeys separated from their biological mothers at birth.

Method/Procedure: Monkeys were placed in cages with two surrogate mothers: one made of bare wire that provided milk, and another covered in soft cloth that provided no food.

Observations/Results: The infant monkeys spent significantly more time clinging to the cloth mother, running to her for comfort when frightened, and only visiting the wire mother to feed.

Psychological Inference: Tactile contact-comfort is a more critical factor in forming attachment bonds than the simple provision of nourishment.

3. Childhood

Childhood is marked by physical growth, motor refinement, and expanding social horizons.

Physical & Motor Trends

  • Cephalocaudal Trend: Physical growth and motor control progress from head to tail (upper body before lower body).
  • Proximodistal Trend: Growth and motor control progress from the center of the body outward to the extremities.

Cognitive Development (Piaget)

  1. Preoperational Stage (2-7 years): Characterized by symbolic thought, but limited by:
    • Egocentrism: Inability to distinguish between one's own perspective and someone else's.
    • Animism: Belief that inanimate objects have lifelike qualities and intentions.
    • Centration: Focusing on only one characteristic of an object or situation while ignoring others.
  2. Concrete Operational Stage (7-11 years): Children develop logical reasoning about concrete events, understand conservation, and can perform reversible mental operations.

Socio-emotional & Moral Development

  • Self & Gender: Children develop Gender Identity (labeling themselves as boys or girls by age 3) and undergo Gender Typing to adopt culturally prescribed gender roles.
  • Moral Development (Kohlberg): Young children think in terms of external authority (punishment and obedience), gradually internalizing societal rules to develop personal moral codes.

4. Challenges of Adolescence

The transitional period between childhood and adulthood, initiated by Puberty.

Physical & Cognitive Shifts

  • Development of Primary Sexual Characteristics (reproductive organs) and Secondary Sexual Characteristics (facial hair, voice changes, breasts). Girls experience Menarche (onset of menstruation).
  • Adolescents enter Piaget's Formal Operational Stage (11-15 years), characterized by abstract, idealistic, and Hypothetical-Deductive Reasoning.
  • Adolescent Egocentrism (Elkind): Includes the Imaginary Audience (feeling constantly observed) and the Personal Fable (sense of unique invulnerability).

Identity Formation

According to Erikson, the primary developmental task of adolescence is resolving Identity vs. Identity Confusion to establish a coherent sense of self.

Major Concerns

  • Delinquency: Socially unacceptable or criminal behaviors, often linked to low parental support or peer pressure.
  • Substance Abuse: Vulnerability to smoking, alcohol, and drug addiction as maladaptive coping mechanisms.
  • Eating Disorders: Obsession with body image leading to Anorexia Nervosa (self-starvation) or Bulimia Nervosa (binge-and-purge cycles).

5. Adulthood and Old Age

  • Adulthood: Defined by responsibility, maturity, and self-support. Key transitions include establishing a career, marriage, and parenthood.
  • Old Age: Characterized by physical declines (sensory loss, graying hair) and cognitive shifts (decline in speed-based tasks, but potential increases in wisdom). Coping with retirement, widowhood, and adjusting to changing family structures are major developmental tasks.
