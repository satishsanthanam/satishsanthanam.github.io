Psychology: Class 11 — Chapter 4: Sensory, Attentional and Perceptual Processes

Introduction to Sensation, Attention, and Perception

Our knowledge of the world around us is constructed through three highly interrelated cognitive processes: sensation, attention, and perception. Together, these processes form the basis of cognition, allowing us to gather information from both external and internal environments and interpret it meaningfully.

Nature and Varieties of Stimuli

The environment contains a wide variety of stimuli that activate our specialized sense organs. Human beings possess seven sensory systems, categorized into five external senses and two deep internal senses:

  • External Senses: Eyes (vision), Ears (hearing), Nose (smell), Tongue (taste), and Skin (touch, warmth, cold, pain).
  • Deep Senses: The kinesthetic system (provides information about body position and movement of body parts relative to each other) and the vestibular system (provides information about body balance and orientation in space).

Functional Limitations of Senses

Our sense organs function within biological limits. The scientific study of the relationship between physical stimuli and the sensations they evoke is called psychophysics.

  • Absolute Threshold (Absolute Limen - AL): The minimum value or intensity of a physical stimulus required to activate a given sensory system on 50 percent of trials.
  • Difference Threshold (Difference Limen - DL): The smallest difference between the values of two stimuli that is necessary to distinguish them as different on 50 percent of trials.
Attentional Processes

Attention is the cognitive process of selecting a limited number of stimuli from a larger pool of environmental inputs. It involves properties such as alertness, concentration, and active search.

Types of Attention

  • Selective Attention: Focusing on a specific stimulus while ignoring others. It is influenced by external factors (e.g., size, intensity, motion, novelty) and internal factors (e.g., motivational states, cognitive interests, preparatory sets).
  • Sustained Attention (Vigilance): The ability to maintain concentration on an object or event for an extended duration. It is influenced by sensory modality, clarity of stimuli, and temporal/spatial predictability.
  • Divided Attention: Allocating attentional resources to perform multiple tasks simultaneously. This is highly dependent on the automaticity of the practiced activities.

Theories of Selective Attention

TheoryDeveloperCore Mechanism
Filter TheoryBroadbent (1956)Stimuli enter a bottleneck where a selective filter allows only one input to pass for higher-level processing, screening out the rest.
Filter-Attenuation TheoryTreisman (1962)Unselected stimuli are not completely blocked but are weakened (attenuated), allowing personally relevant cues (e.g., one's name) to slip through.
Multimode TheoryJohnston & Heinz (1978)Attention is a flexible system operating across three stages (sensory, semantic, and conscious representation), with later stages requiring more mental effort.

Span of Attention and ADHD

The span of attention refers to the amount of information an observer can grasp in a single brief exposure. Using a tachistoscope, researchers established that the human attentional span is limited to the "magic number" $7 \pm 2$ items.

When attentional mechanisms are disrupted, individuals may exhibit Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), a behavioral disorder characterized by impulsivity, excessive motor activity, and an inability to sustain attention.

Perceptual Processes

Perception is the process by which we recognize, interpret, and give meaning to sensory information. It is an active construction of reality influenced by our past experiences, motivations, and cultural backgrounds.

Processing Approaches

  • Bottom-Up Processing: Recognition starts from the individual parts and features of a stimulus, which are integrated to form a perception of the whole.
  • Top-Down Processing: Recognition starts from the whole concept or expectation, which guides the identification of individual components.

The Perceiver's Influence

Perception is highly subjective and shaped by several internal factors:

  • Motivation: Needs and desires bias perception (e.g., hungry individuals perceive ambiguous pictures as food).
  • Expectations (Perceptual Sets): A mental predisposition to perceive what we expect to see.
  • Cognitive Styles: Field Dependent individuals perceive the environment holistically, whereas Field Independent individuals analyze the environment by breaking it down into smaller, distinct units.
Principles of Perceptual Organisation

Developed by Gestalt Psychology (Köhler, Koffka, and Wertheimer), these principles state that we perceive stimuli as organized, meaningful wholes rather than discrete sensory elements. The brain naturally seeks a "good figure" or pragnanz.

Figure-Ground Segregation

The most basic form of organization where we distinguish a prominent object (the figure) from its background (the ground). Figures have clear contours and appear closer, while the ground is relatively formless and recedes behind the figure.

Gestalt Laws of Grouping

  • Principle of Proximity: Objects close together in space or time are perceived as belonging together.
  • Principle of Similarity: Objects with similar physical characteristics are grouped together.
  • Principle of Continuity: We perceive objects as belonging together if they form a continuous, smooth pattern.
  • Principle of Smallness: Smaller areas tend to be seen as figures against larger backgrounds.
  • Principle of Symmetry: Symmetrical areas are more readily perceived as figures.
  • Principle of Surroundedness: Areas surrounded by others tend to be perceived as figures.
  • Principle of Closure: We tend to fill in gaps in sensory input to perceive complete, whole objects.
Perception of Space, Depth, and Distance

Although retinal images are flat and two-dimensional, we perceive the world in three dimensions using depth perception, which relies on two types of cues.

Monocular Cues (Pictorial Cues)

Cues that require only one eye to perceive depth and distance:

  • Relative Size: Smaller retinal images are perceived as farther away.
  • Interposition (Overlapping): An object that partially blocks another is perceived as closer.
  • Linear Perspective: Parallel lines appear to converge as they recede into the distance.
  • Aerial Perspective: Distant objects look hazy or blurry due to atmospheric dust and moisture.
  • Light and Shade: Highlights and shadows indicate depth and three-dimensional form.
  • Relative Height: Larger or higher-placed objects in the visual field are perceived as farther or closer depending on expectations.
  • Texture Gradient: Density of details increases as distance increases.
  • Motion Parallax: A kinetic cue where closer objects appear to move faster and in the opposite direction of our motion, while distant objects move slower and in the same direction.

Binocular Cues (Physiological Cues)

Cues that require the coordinated use of both eyes:

  • Retinal Disparity: The slight difference between the images projected onto the left and right retinas due to the horizontal distance between our eyes.
  • Convergence: The inward rotation of the eyes to focus on nearby objects, sending muscular feedback to the brain.
  • Accommodation: The physiological process where the ciliary muscles alter the thickness of the eye lens to focus on objects at varying distances.
Perceptual Constancies, Illusions, and Socio-Cultural Factors

Perceptual Constancies

The tendency to perceive objects as stable and unchanging despite variations in the sensory patterns projected onto our retinas:

  • Size Constancy: Perceiving an object's physical size as constant despite changes in its distance and retinal image size.
  • Shape Constancy: Perceiving an object's shape as constant despite changes in its orientation and retinal projection.
  • Brightness Constancy: Perceiving the lightness or darkness of an object as constant despite changes in illumination.

Illusions

Misperceptions resulting from the misinterpretation of sensory information. They are often called "primitive organizations" because they are experienced similarly by most people.

  • Geometrical Illusions: Examples include the Muller-Lyer illusion (where lines of equal length appear unequal due to arrowheads) and the Vertical-Horizontal illusion.
  • Apparent Movement Illusion (Phi-phenomenon): The perception of movement generated by projecting a rapid succession of stationary, flickering lights or pictures.

Socio-Cultural Influences

Experiment Title: Cross-Cultural Susceptibility to Illusions

Background/Subject: Comparative study by Segall, Campbell, and Herskovits involving subjects from remote African villages and Western urban settings.

Method/Procedure: Subjects from different environments were tested on their susceptibility to the Muller-Lyer and Vertical-Horizontal illusions.

Observations/Results: African subjects living in dense forests showed greater susceptibility to the vertical-horizontal illusion due to their regular experience with verticality (e.g., tall trees). Western subjects living in "carpentered" environments characterized by right angles showed greater susceptibility to the Muller-Lyer illusion.

Psychological Inference: Perceptual habits and susceptibility to illusions are learned and shaped by the physical and cultural environments in which individuals live.
