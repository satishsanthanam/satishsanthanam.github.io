Class 11 — Chapter 5: Learning

Nature of Learning

Learning is a fundamental psychological process defined as any relatively permanent change in behavior or behavioral potential produced by experience or practice. It is an inferred process that must be distinguished from temporary behavioral fluctuations and immediate performance.

Key Features of Learning

  • Involves Experience: Learning always occurs through some form of interaction or experience with the environment.
  • Relatively Permanent: Behavioral changes must endure over time to be classified as learning, distinguishing them from temporary states like fatigue, drug effects, or habituation.
  • Inferred Process: Learning is an internal cognitive change that cannot be observed directly; it is inferred from an organism's performance (observed behavior).
Paradigms of Learning

Psychologists have identified several distinct paradigms through which organisms acquire new behaviors, ranging from simple associative conditioning to complex cognitive and social processes.

Classical Conditioning

First investigated by Ivan P. Pavlov, Classical Conditioning is a form of associative learning where a neutral stimulus becomes associated with a biologically significant stimulus, eventually eliciting a similar reflexive response.

Case/Experiment Title: Pavlov's Classical Conditioning Experiment

Background/Subject: Hungry dogs

Method/Procedure: A dog was placed in a harness. A bell (Conditioned Stimulus, CS) was sounded, immediately followed by the presentation of meat powder (Unconditioned Stimulus, US), which naturally caused salivation (Unconditioned Response, UR). This pairing was repeated over multiple trials.

Observations/Results: Eventually, the dog began to salivate (Conditioned Response, CR) to the sound of the bell alone, anticipating the food.

Psychological Inference: Demonstrates S-S (Stimulus-Stimulus) learning, where the CS becomes a reliable signal for the occurrence of the US.

Determinants of Classical Conditioning

  • Time Relations: Conditioning efficiency depends on the temporal arrangement of CS and US:
    • Simultaneous Conditioning: CS and US are presented together.
    • Delayed Conditioning: Onset of CS precedes US, and they overlap (most effective).
    • Trace Conditioning: CS begins and ends before US onset, leaving a time gap.
    • Backward Conditioning: US precedes CS (rarely effective).
  • Type of Unconditioned Stimuli: Can be appetitive (pleasurable, like food) or aversive (painful, like electric shocks). Aversive conditioning typically establishes much faster.
  • Intensity of CS: More intense conditioned stimuli accelerate the acquisition of the conditioned response.

Operant / Instrumental Conditioning

Investigated by B.F. Skinner, Operant Conditioning involves learning voluntary behaviors (operants) that operate on the environment to produce rewarding or reinforcing consequences.

Case/Experiment Title: Skinner's Operant Conditioning Experiment

Background/Subject: Hungry rats

Method/Procedure: A hungry rat was placed in a specially designed chamber (Skinner Box). While exploring, the rat accidentally pressed a lever, which automatically delivered a food pellet (reinforcer).

Observations/Results: Over successive trials, the exploratory behavior decreased, and the rat learned to press the lever immediately upon being placed in the box.

Psychological Inference: Voluntary behaviors are shaped, maintained, or changed by their environmental consequences.

Determinants of Operant Conditioning

  • Types of Reinforcement:
    • Positive Reinforcement: Presenting a pleasant stimulus to strengthen a response (e.g., food, praise).
    • Negative Reinforcement: Removing an unpleasant stimulus to strengthen a response (e.g., escaping cold by wearing a jacket). Note: Negative reinforcement is not punishment; it increases response probability, whereas punishment suppresses it.
  • Schedules of Reinforcement:
    • Continuous Reinforcement: Reinforcing the response every time it occurs.
    • Partial/Intermittent Reinforcement: Reinforcing the response only sometimes, which creates high resistance to extinction.
  • Delayed Reinforcement: Delays in delivering reinforcement lead to a poorer level of performance.

Key Learning Processes

  • Extinction: The gradual weakening and disappearance of a learned response when reinforcement is withheld.
  • Generalisation: The tendency to respond to stimuli that are similar to the original conditioned stimulus.
  • Discrimination: The ability to differentiate between a reinforcing stimulus and non-reinforcing similar stimuli.
  • Spontaneous Recovery: The sudden reappearance of an extinguished conditioned response after a period of rest.

Case/Experiment Title: Seligman and Maier's Learned Helplessness Study

Background/Subject: Dogs

Method/Procedure: Dogs were subjected to inescapable electric shocks paired with a tone in a classical conditioning setup. Later, they were placed in an operant chamber where they could easily escape shocks by jumping a small barrier.

Observations/Results: Unlike control dogs, the pre-treated dogs did not attempt to escape; they simply lay down and whimpered.

Psychological Inference: Prior experience with inescapable, uncontrollable aversive events produces Learned Helplessness, a state of passive resignation linked to clinical depression.

Observational Learning

Also known as social learning, modeling, or imitation, Observational Learning occurs when individuals acquire new behaviors by watching the actions of others (models) and noting the consequences of those actions.

Case/Experiment Title: Bandura's Bobo Doll Experiment

Background/Subject: Children

Method/Procedure: Children watched a film of a model behaving aggressively toward an inflated Bobo doll. Group 1 saw the model rewarded; Group 2 saw the model punished; Group 3 saw no consequences. Children were then placed in a playroom with a Bobo doll.

Observations/Results: Children who saw the model rewarded imitated the aggressive behaviors most frequently, while those who saw the model punished showed the least aggression.

Psychological Inference: Observers acquire cognitive representations of behavior through observation, but the performance of that behavior is influenced by observed reinforcement or punishment.

Cognitive Learning

Cognitive learning focuses on internal mental processes, such as representation, memory, and problem-solving, rather than simple stimulus-response associations.

1. Insight Learning

Case/Experiment Title: Kohler's Chimpanzee Experiments

Background/Subject: Chimpanzees (e.g., Sultan)

Method/Procedure: Chimpanzees were placed in an enclosure where food (bananas) was hung out of reach. Tools like boxes and poles were scattered around the area.

Observations/Results: After initial trial-and-error failures, the chimpanzees sat quietly, then suddenly stood up, stacked the boxes, or joined poles together to reach the food.

Psychological Inference: Demonstrates Insight Learning, where solutions emerge suddenly through cognitive restructuring of the problem field, rather than gradual trial-and-error.

2. Latent Learning

Case/Experiment Title: Tolman's Maze Learning Experiment

Background/Subject: Rats

Method/Procedure: Two groups of rats explored a complex maze. Group 1 was rewarded with food at the end of the maze daily. Group 2 received no reward for the first several days, but was rewarded later.

Observations/Results: Once rewarded, Group 2 immediately navigated the maze as quickly and error-free as Group 1.

Psychological Inference: Demonstrates Latent Learning, where learning occurs without immediate reinforcement and is stored as an internal Cognitive Map, only being demonstrated when motivation is introduced.

Verbal Learning

Unique to humans, Verbal Learning involves acquiring associations between words, symbols, and concepts. It is studied using three primary experimental methods:

  1. Paired-Associates Learning: Learning to associate a stimulus word (often a nonsense syllable) with a response word (e.g., GEN - LOOT).
  2. Serial Learning: Memorizing a list of verbal items and recalling them in their exact sequential order.
  3. Free Recall: Memorizing a list of words and recalling them in any order. This often reveals Category Clustering (organizing words by semantic groups) and subjective organization.

Skill Learning

A skill is the ability to perform complex tasks smoothly and efficiently. According to Fitts, skill acquisition progresses through three distinct phases:

  1. Cognitive Phase: The learner must understand and memorize instructions, keeping every step alive in conscious awareness.
  2. Associative Phase: Sensory inputs are linked with appropriate motor responses. Errors decrease, and performance becomes smoother with practice.
  3. Autonomous Phase: The skill attains automaticity, requiring minimal conscious effort or attentional demands.
Factors Facilitating Learning
  • Motivation: A prerequisite for learning that energizes behavior toward a goal. Can be intrinsic (for personal enjoyment) or extrinsic (for external rewards).
  • Preparedness for Learning: Biological constraints and genetic endowments that make certain associations easy to learn for some species, but difficult or impossible for others.
Learning Disabilities

A heterogeneous group of neurological disorders manifested as significant difficulties in the acquisition of reading, writing, speaking, reasoning, or mathematical abilities, occurring in children of average to superior intelligence.

Common Symptoms

  • Attention Disorders: Easily distracted, difficulty sustaining attention, and hyperactivity.
  • Space and Time Disorientation: Confusion with directions (left/right, up/down) and poor sense of time.
  • Motor Coordination Issues: Poor manual dexterity, difficulties with fine motor tasks like writing or balancing.
  • Perceptual Disorders: Visual, auditory, or tactile misperceptions.
  • Dyslexia: Difficulty copying letters and words, frequently confusing similar-looking letters (e.g., b and d, p and q) or words (e.g., was and saw).
