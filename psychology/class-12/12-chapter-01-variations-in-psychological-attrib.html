Psychology: Class 12 — Chapter 1: Variations in Psychological Attributes

Individual Differences in Human Functioning

Individual variations are common within and across all species. People vary along both physical and psychological dimensions. Different traits exist in varying degrees in each individual, making everyone unique.

Individual Differences and Situationism

For psychologists, individual differences refer to distinctiveness and variations among people's characteristics and behaviour patterns.

While some psychologists believe behaviour is driven by personal traits, the perspective of situationism states that situations and circumstances in which one is placed strongly influence behaviour. The situationist perspective views human behaviour relatively more as a result of external factors rather than internal traits.

Assessment of Psychological Attributes

Assessment is the first step in understanding any psychological attribute. It refers to the measurement of psychological attributes of individuals and their evaluation, using multiple methods in terms of certain standards of comparison.

Assessments can be informal (subjective, varying from case to case) or formal (objective, standardised, and organised).

Domains of Psychological Attributes

Psychological attributes are multi-dimensional and complex. The primary domains assessed by psychologists include:

  • Intelligence: The global capacity to understand the world, think rationally, and use available resources effectively when faced with challenges.
  • Aptitude: An individual's underlying potential for acquiring skills or knowledge after proper training.
  • Interest: An individual's preference for engaging in one or more specific activities relative to others.
  • Personality: Relatively enduring characteristics of a person that make her or him distinct from others.
  • Values: Enduring beliefs about an ideal mode of behaviour.

Assessment Methods

Psychologists use several systematic methods to gather data and evaluate attributes:

  • Psychological Test: An objective and standardised measure of an individual's mental and/or behavioural characteristics.
  • Interview: Seeking information from a person on a one-to-one basis.
  • Case Study: An in-depth study of an individual in terms of her/his psychological attributes, history, and psychosocial environment.
  • Observation: Employing systematic, organised, and objective procedures to record behavioural phenomena occurring naturally in real time.
  • Self-Report: A method in which a person provides factual information about herself/himself and/or opinions and beliefs.
Theories of Intelligence

Theories of intelligence are broadly classified into the psychometric approach (which focuses on the structure of intelligence or an aggregate of abilities) and the information-processing approach (which focuses on the cognitive processes used in intellectual reasoning and problem-solving).

Psychometric Theories

  • Uni-Factor Theory (Alfred Binet): Conceptualised intelligence as consisting of one similar set of abilities which can be used for solving any problem in an individual's environment.
  • Two-Factor Theory (Charles Spearman): Proposed that intelligence consists of a general factor (g-factor) representing common mental operations, and specific factors (s-factor) representing unique abilities in specific domains.
  • Theory of Primary Mental Abilities (Louis Thurstone): Proposed that intelligence consists of seven independent primary abilities: Verbal Comprehension, Numerical Abilities, Spatial Relations, Perceptual Speed, Word Fluency, Memory, and Inductive Reasoning.
  • Hierarchical Model of Intelligence (Arthur Jensen): Proposed abilities operating at two levels: Level I (associative learning, where output is similar to input) and Level II (cognitive competence, involving higher-order skills).
  • Structure-of-Intellect Model (J.P. Guilford): Classifies intellectual traits along three dimensions: Operations (what the respondent does), Contents (the nature of information processed), and Products (the form in which information is processed), resulting in 180 structural cells.

Information-Processing Theories

  • Theory of Multiple Intelligences (Howard Gardner): Intelligence is not a single entity; eight distinct, independent types of intelligence exist:
    1. Linguistic (word-smart)
    2. Logical-Mathematical (abstract reasoning)
    3. Spatial (forming visual images)
    4. Musical (producing rhythms and patterns)
    5. Bodily-Kinaesthetic (using body flexibly)
    6. Interpersonal (understanding others' motives and feelings)
    7. Intrapersonal (understanding one's own feelings and desires)
    8. Naturalistic (identifying features of the natural world)
  • Triarchic Theory of Intelligence (Robert Sternberg): Views intelligence as the ability to adapt, shape, and select environments. It identifies three basic types of intelligence:
    • Componential / Analytical Intelligence: The analysis of information to solve problems (consisting of knowledge acquisition, meta-components, and performance components).
    • Experiential / Creative Intelligence: Using past experiences creatively to solve novel problems.
    • Contextual / Practical Intelligence: Dealing with environmental demands encountered on a daily basis ('street smartness').
  • PASS Model of Intelligence (J.P. Das, Jack Naglieri, Kirby): Intellectual activity involves the interdependent functioning of three neurological systems:
    • Arousal/Attention: State of arousal that enables a person to process information.
    • Simultaneous and Successive Processing: Integrating information into a knowledge system either simultaneously (perceiving relations) or successively (serial recall).
    • Planning: Activating courses of action, implementing them, and evaluating their effectiveness.
Individual Differences in Intelligence

Interplay of Nature and Nurture

Intelligence is a product of complex interactions between heredity (nature) and environment (nurture). Heredity sets the developmental range, while the environment shapes where within that range an individual's intelligence actually develops.

Assessment of Intelligence and IQ

In 1905, Alfred Binet and Theodore Simon made the first successful attempt to measure intelligence, introducing the concept of Mental Age (MA). In 1912, William Stern devised the concept of Intelligence Quotient (IQ), which is computed as:

$$IQ = \frac{\text{MA}}{\text{CA}} \times 100$$

Where CA is Chronological Age.

IQ scores in a population follow a symmetrical bell-shaped distribution called the normal curve, centered around a mean of 100.

Intellectual Deficiency vs. Intellectual Giftedness

Intellectual Deficiency: Individuals with IQs below 70 who show deficits in adaptive behaviour manifested during the developmental period (0-18 years). Levels of intellectual disability include:

  • Mild (IQs 55 to ~70)
  • Moderate (IQs 35-40 to ~50-55)
  • Severe (IQs 20-25 to ~35-40)
  • Profound (IQs below 20-25)

Intellectual Giftedness: Exceptional general ability shown in superior performance across a wide variety of areas (typically IQs above 130). It is distinct from talent, which refers to remarkable ability in a single specific field.

Types of Intelligence Tests

Classification of Tests

  • Individual vs. Group Tests: Individual tests are administered to one person at a time and require rapport-building. Group tests can be administered to multiple people simultaneously.
  • Verbal, Non-Verbal, or Performance Tests: Verbal tests require language skills. Non-verbal tests use pictures or illustrations (e.g., Raven's Progressive Matrices). Performance tests require manipulating physical objects (e.g., Kohs' Block Design Test).
  • Culture-Fair vs. Culture-Biased Tests: Culture-fair tests contain items common to all cultures or do not require language, reducing cultural bias.

Culture and Intelligence

Culture provides the social context in which intelligence develops. Western cultures promote technological intelligence, which values skills of analysis, performance, speed, and achievement orientation.

In contrast, non-western cultures value holistic, collectivistic orientations. In the Indian tradition, intelligence is viewed as integral intelligence, emphasizing connectivity with the social and world environment. The Sanskrit word buddhi represents a pervasive concept of intelligence that includes cognitive, affective, motivational, and ethical components.

Emotional Intelligence

Emotional Intelligence is a set of skills that underlie accurate appraisal, expression, and regulation of emotions. It is the feeling side of intelligence, expressed as Emotional Quotient (EQ).

Salovey and Mayer defined it as "the ability to monitor one's own and other's emotions, to discriminate among them, and to use the information to guide one's thinking and actions."

Characteristics of Emotionally Intelligent Persons:

  • Sensitivity to one's own feelings and emotions.
  • Sensitivity to others' emotions by noting body language, voice, and facial expressions.
  • Relating emotions to thoughts to solve problems and make decisions.
  • Understanding the powerful influence and nature of emotions.
  • Controlling and regulating emotions to achieve harmony and peace.
Special Abilities: Aptitude and Creativity

Aptitude: Nature and Measurement

Aptitude refers to special abilities in a particular field of activity. While intelligence represents general mental ability, aptitude indicates the capacity to acquire specific skills after training.

Aptitude tests can be independent (specialised, e.g., Typing Aptitude) or multiple (generalised test batteries, e.g., Differential Aptitude Tests (DAT), GATB, ASVAB).

Creativity

Creativity is the ability to produce ideas, objects, or problem solutions that are novel, appropriate, and unique. It involves divergent thinking (thinking in different directions, open-ended), whereas intelligence tests primarily involve convergent thinking (finding the single correct solution).

A certain level of intelligence is required for creativity, but beyond that, high intelligence does not guarantee high creativity.
