Psychology: Class 12 — Chapter 3: Meeting Life Challenges

Introduction & Nature of Stress

Case/Experiment Title: Examination Anxiety and Stress Response

Background/Subject: Raj, a student preparing for his final examination.

Method/Procedure: Raj studied until 1 a.m., slept poorly due to tension, missed breakfast, and experienced intense physical and cognitive anxiety symptoms during the exam.

Observations/Results: Heart pounding, clammy hands, mind going completely blank, and inability to perform.

Psychological Inference: High stress (distress) and evaluative apprehension can severely impair cognitive performance and physiological functioning.

The Concept of Stress

Stress is the pattern of responses an organism makes to stimulus events that disturb equilibrium and exceed coping abilities. It originates from the Latin words strictus (tight/narrow) and stringere (to tighten).

  • Eustress: The level of stress that is good for a person; a key asset for achieving peak performance.
  • Distress: The negative manifestation of stress that causes wear and tear on the body.
  • Strain: The reaction to external stressors.

Lazarus's Cognitive Theory of Stress

An individual's response to stress depends on cognitive appraisal:

  • Primary Appraisal: Perceiving a new or changing environment as positive, neutral, or negative. Negative events are appraised for harm, threat, or challenge.
  • Secondary Appraisal: Assessment of one's coping abilities and resources (mental, physical, personal, or social) to meet the harm, threat, or challenge.
Types and Sources of Stress

Three Major Types of Stress

  • Physical and Environmental Stress: Demands that change the state of our body (e.g., overexertion, lack of sleep, malnutrition) or environmental aspects (e.g., noise, pollution, crowding, natural disasters).
  • Psychological Stress: Stresses generated in our minds, unique to the individual. These include:
    • Frustration: Blocking of needs and motives by something or someone.
    • Conflicts: Incompatibility between two or more needs or motives.
    • Internal Pressures: Beliefs based upon high expectations from within ourselves.
    • Social Pressures: Excessive demands brought about by other people.
  • Social Stress: Induced externally from interactions with other people (e.g., death/illness in the family, strained relationships).

Sources of Stress

  • Life Events: Major changes (both planned and unplanned) that disturb our routine and cause upheaval.
  • Hassles: Personal daily stresses we endure, such as traffic, noisy surroundings, or water shortages.
  • Traumatic Events: Extreme events (e.g., fire, earthquake, tsunami) that may cause long-term anxiety, flashbacks, and intrusive thoughts.

Effects of Stress on Health and Functioning

Four Major Effects of Stress

  • Emotional Effects: Mood swings, erratic behavior, anxiety, depression, and decreased confidence.
  • Physiological Effects: Release of hormones like adrenaline and cortisol, increased heart rate, elevated blood pressure, and slowed digestion.
  • Cognitive Effects: Mental overload, poor concentration, reduced short-term memory capacity, and faulty decision-making.
  • Behavioural Effects: Disrupted sleep patterns, increased absenteeism, reduced work performance, and increased intake of stimulants or drugs.

General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)

Hans Selye defined the body's physiological response to prolonged stress through three distinct stages:

  1. Alarm Reaction Stage: The stressor triggers the activation of the adrenal-pituitary-cortex system, releasing hormones to prepare the body for fight or flight.
  2. Resistance Stage: If stress is prolonged, the parasympathetic nervous system calls for more cautious use of resources while the body continues to confront the threat.
  3. Exhaustion Stage: Continued exposure to the stressor drains the body's resources, making physiological systems ineffective and increasing susceptibility to illness.

Stress and the Immune System

Psychoneuroimmunology studies the links between the mind, the brain, and the immune system. Stress impairs the immune system by reducing the activity of white blood cells (leucocytes), such as T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, making the body vulnerable to pathogens.

Burnout and Lifestyle

Prolonged stress leads to burnout, a state of physical, emotional, and psychological exhaustion. It also promotes an unhealthy lifestyle, including poor nutritional habits, sleep deprivation, and health-risking behaviors like smoking or alcohol abuse.

Coping with Stress

Coping Strategies

Coping is a dynamic, situation-specific reaction to stress. Endler and Parker identified three major coping strategies:

  • Task-oriented Strategy: Obtaining information about the stressor and taking direct action to resolve it.
  • Emotion-oriented Strategy: Efforts to maintain hope and regulate emotional responses (e.g., venting anger).
  • Avoidance-oriented Strategy: Denying or minimizing the seriousness of the situation.

Lazarus and Folkman conceptualized coping as either problem-focused (altering the environment/problem) or emotion-focused (regulating the emotional response).

Stress Management Techniques

  • Relaxation Techniques: Deep breathing and progressive muscle relaxation to reduce physiological arousal.
  • Meditation Procedures: Yogic methods of refocusing attention to achieve an altered state of consciousness.
  • Biofeedback: Monitoring and learning to control physiological aspects of stress using feedback devices.
  • Creative Visualisation: Using imagery and imagination to build confidence and reduce stress.
  • Cognitive Behavioural Techniques: Replacing negative, irrational thoughts with positive, rational ones (e.g., Meichenbaum's Stress Inoculation Training).
  • Exercise: Regular physical activity (aerobics, swimming, walking) to improve cardiovascular efficiency and reduce stress hormones.
Promoting Positive Health and Well-being

Stress Resistant Personality (Hardiness)

Kobasa identified three key personality traits of hardiness that buffer against stress:

  • Commitment: Dedication to work, family, hobbies, and social life.
  • Control: A sense of personal purpose and direction in life.
  • Challenge: Viewing changes in life as normal and positive rather than threatening.

Life Skills

Abilities for adaptive and positive behavior to deal with daily demands:

  • Assertiveness: Communicating feelings, needs, and thoughts clearly and confidently.
  • Time Management: Planning and delegating time to reduce pressure.
  • Rational Thinking: Challenging distorted thinking and making positive statements.
  • Improving Relationships: Active listening, expressing feelings, and accepting others.
  • Self-care: Keeping healthy, fit, and relaxed through proper breathing and habits.
  • Overcoming Unhelpful Habits: Addressing perfectionism, avoidance, and procrastination.

Resilience and Health

Resilience is the dynamic developmental capacity to 'bounce back' in the face of stress and adversity. It relies on three resources:

  • I HAVE: Social and interpersonal strengths (trusted support networks).
  • I AM: Inner strengths (self-esteem, respect for self and others).
  • I CAN: Interpersonal and problem-solving skills.

Social Support

The existence and availability of people we can rely on. It takes three forms:

  • Tangible Support: Material aid such as money, goods, or services.
  • Informational Support: Advice and information to help cope with stressful events.
  • Emotional Support: Reassurance of being loved, valued, and cared for.
