Sociology: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 2: Terms, Concepts and Their Use in Sociology

I. Introduction: The Need for Sociological Terms and Concepts

Sociology requires a specialized vocabulary to distinguish its findings from common-sense explanations. While disciplines like nuclear physics use unfamiliar terms, sociology must redefine familiar everyday terms (like family, status, and role) to enable precise, objective analysis.

Concepts are not static; they have historical biographies reflecting the transition from pre-modern to modern societies:

  • Pre-modern societies : Characterized by small-scale, traditional, face-to-face, and intimate interactions.
  • Modern societies : Characterized by large-scale, complex, formal, and impersonal interactions.

Theoretical Divergences on Concepts: Different sociological perspectives interpret social reality through distinct conceptual lenses. For example, Karl Marx emphasized class and conflict , Emile Durkheim focused on social solidarity and collective conscience , and structural functionalists viewed society as a harmonious organism.

Core Sociological Concept Baseline Definition / Phenomenon Social Manifestation / Practice Impact on Social Stratification & Power
Social Group A collection of continuously interacting persons sharing common interests, culture, values, and norms. Families, peer groups, political parties, and trade unions. Determines collective bargaining power, social capital, and access to resources.
Social Stratification Structured inequalities between different groupings of people regarding access to material or symbolic rewards. Caste systems, class hierarchies, and estate systems. Systematically distributes power, health, educational success, and political influence.
Status A defined social position within a group or society, carrying specific rights, duties, and expectations. Being a doctor, mother, student, or citizen. Establishes social standing, prestige, and authority hierarchies.
Role The dynamic or behavioral aspect of a status; the actual performance of duties associated with a position. A teacher conducting a class; a parent caring for a child. Reinforces or challenges structural expectations through individual agency or conformity.
Social Control The social processes, techniques, and strategies by which individual or group behavior is regulated. Legal systems, police force, family surveillance, and social ridicule. Maintains the status quo of dominant classes (conflict view) or preserves social cohesion (functionalist view).
II. Social Groups and Society

A defining feature of human life is that humans interact, communicate, and construct social collectivities. However, not every gathering of people constitutes a social group.

Social Groups vs. Aggregates / Quasi-Groups
  • Aggregates / Quasi-groups : Simple collections of people who are in the same place at the same time but share no definite connection or "we" feeling (e.g., passengers waiting at a railway station, cinema audiences, crowds).
  • Evolution into Social Groups : Quasi-groups can become organized social groups under specific historical circumstances (e.g., social classes or castes forming political parties, women's movements organizing into formal groups).
  • Key Characteristics of a Social Group :
    • Persistent interaction to provide continuity.
    • A stable pattern of these interactions.
    • A sense of belonging to identify with other members.
    • Shared interests.
    • Acceptance of common norms and values.
    • A definable structure.
Types of Social Groups

Sociologists classify groups based on size, intimacy, and structural organization:

Group Type Key Characteristics Examples
Primary Group Small size, intimate face-to-face association, person-oriented, high emotional attachment. Family, village community, close friends.
Secondary Group Large size, formal and impersonal relationships, goal-oriented, contractual. Schools, government offices, hospitals, student associations.
Community Highly personal, intimate, and enduring relationships; total personal involvement. Traditional agrarian villages, close-knit neighborhoods.
Society / Association Impersonal, superficial, and transitory relationships; rational, calculating, and self-interested approach. Modern urban cities, commercial corporations.
In-Group A group to which one feels a sense of belonging; characterized by "us" or "we" feelings. Students of the same school, members of the same family.
Out-Group A group to which one does not belong; characterized by "them" or "they" feelings and potential hostility. Migrants viewed by local populations, rival school students.
Reference Group A group whose lifestyle, culture, and aspirations are emulated, though one does not belong to it. Middle-class Indians emulating British lifestyles during the colonial period.
Peer Group A primary group formed of individuals of similar age or common professional status; exerts peer pressure. Classmates, professional colleagues.

Profile: M.N. Srinivas (1916–1999)

  • Theoretical Framework: Structural-functional analysis of Indian society, focusing on caste dynamics, village communities, and social change.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Intensive ethnographic fieldwork in the village of Rampura, Karnataka (1948), utilizing participant observation and census methods.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Sanskritisation , dominant caste , and distinctions between recent and historical migrants ( nenne monne bandavaru vs. arsheyinda bandavaru ).

"I heard villagers use two expressions which I came to realise were significant: the recent immigrants were almost contemptuously described as nenne monne bandavaru ('came yesterday or the day before') while old immigrants were described as arsheyinda bandavaru ('came long ago') or khadeem kulagalu ('old lineages')." (Srinivas 1996:33)

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This field observation highlights how communities construct internal boundaries of belonging. Even within marginalized or migrant groups, a hierarchy of residency is established, transforming an objective category (migrant) into subjective social statuses (in-group vs. out-group) that dictate social acceptance and prestige.

III. Social Stratification

Social stratification refers to the existence of structured inequalities between groups in society in terms of their access to material or symbolic rewards. It is often compared to the geological layering of rock in the earth's surface, forming a hierarchy of strata.

Systems of Stratification

Historically, four basic systems of stratification have existed in human societies:

  1. Slavery : An extreme form of inequality in which some individuals are literally owned by others (e.g., ancient Greece, Rome, and the Southern States of the USA in the 18th and 19th centuries).
  2. Estate : Feudal European systems based on rigid social estates with distinct legal rights and obligations.
  3. Caste : A system where an individual's position depends entirely on status attributes ascribed by birth.
  4. Class : An open, achievement-based system where position is determined largely by economic relations and market capacity.
The Caste System in India
  • Ascribed Status : Position is determined entirely by birth rather than achieved during life.
  • Purity and Pollution : Traditional hierarchy defined in terms of ritual purity. The priestly Brahmins are at the top, while the Panchamas (outcastes) are at the bottom.
  • Varna vs. Jati : The traditional system is conceptualized as a four-fold division ( varna : Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras), but in reality, it consists of innumerable occupation-based groups called jatis .
  • Impact of Urbanization : Modern industries, public transport, and cities challenged traditional rules of ritual avoidance and purity, as noted by sociologist A.R. Desai. However, discrimination persists in private spheres.

Profile: A.R. Desai (1915–1994)

  • Theoretical Framework: Marxist sociology, focusing on nationalism, agrarian relations, state character, and the social consequences of urbanization and industrialization.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Historical-materialist analysis of Indian nationalism and urban class structures.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Class conflict, state capitalism, and the democratic rights of marginalized groups.

"Modern industries brought into being modern cities honey-combed with cosmopolitan hotels, restaurants, theatres, trams, buses, railways... In trains and buses one occasionally rubbed shoulders with members of the depressed classes... [Yet] in the mill there may be no open discrimination... but experience of private interactions tells another story: 'They will not even drink water from our hands...'" (Desai 1975 / Parmar 2004)

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This excerpt illustrates the dual nature of modern social change. While urban public spaces (transport, workplaces) force physical proximity and challenge traditional rules of purity and pollution, private spheres and informal interactions often preserve deep-seated caste discrimination, showing that structural change is uneven and contested.

The Class System

Unlike caste, class is open, achievement-based, and dynamic. Sociologists explain class through different theoretical frameworks:

  • Karl Marx (Marxist Theory) : Classes are defined by their relation to the means of production (owners of factories/land vs. owners of labor power).
  • Max Weber : Class is based on economic relations, but also on life chances (rewards and advantages afforded by market capacity), prestige, and political power.
  • Functionalist Theory : Stratification is an unconsciously evolved device by which societies ensure that the most important positions are filled by the most qualified persons.
IV. Status and Role

Status and role are twin concepts that form the building blocks of social structure.

Status: Types and Features
  • Definition : A status is a position in society or a group, carrying defined rights and duties.
  • Ascribed Status : A social position occupied by birth or assumed involuntarily (e.g., age, caste, race, kinship).
  • Achieved Status : A social position occupied voluntarily through personal ability, achievements, virtues, and choices (e.g., educational qualifications, income, professional expertise).
  • Status Set : The collection of multiple statuses occupied by an individual simultaneously (e.g., being a student, customer, sibling, and patient).
  • Status Sequence : Statuses attained in succession at various stages of life (e.g., son → father → grandfather).
  • Prestige : The value or social honor attached to a status, independent of the individual occupying it (e.g., high prestige of a doctor compared to a shopkeeper).
Role: Performance and Conflict
  • Definition : A role is the dynamic or behavioral aspect of status. Status is occupied, but roles are played.
  • Role Conflict : Incompatibility among roles corresponding to one or more statuses, occurring when contrary expectations arise (e.g., a working woman juggling her role as a mother/wife at home and a professional at work).
  • Role Stereotyping : The process of reinforcing specific, rigid roles for certain members of society (e.g., socialising men as breadwinners and women as homemakers).
  • Socialisation and Agency : Socialisation is not a passive programming process. Individuals exercise agency, negotiating, creating, and challenging social roles rather than simply accepting them.

"Khasi matriliny generates intense role conflict for men. They are torn between their responsibilities to their natal house on the one hand and to their wife and children on the other... The strain affects Khasi women, in a way more intensely. A woman can never be fully assured that her husband does not find his sister's house more congenial place than her own house." (Nongbri 2003:190)

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This case study demonstrates that role conflict is not exclusive to modern Western societies or specific genders. In the matrilineal Khasi society of Meghalaya, structural kinship rules create competing expectations for men (as uncles/brothers in their natal homes vs. fathers/husbands in their conjugal homes), illustrating how kinship systems shape psychological and social strain.

V. Society and Social Control

Social control refers to the various means used by a society to bring its recalcitrant or unruly members back into line.

Theoretical Perspectives on Social Control
  • Functionalist Perspective : Social control is the use of force and enforcement of values to maintain order, mitigate tensions, and preserve social cohesion and stability.
  • Conflict Perspective : Social control is a mechanism used by dominant social classes to impose their will and maintain power over the rest of society. Law is seen as the formal writ of the powerful.
Types of Social Control
  • Formal Social Control : Codified, systematic, and institutionalized mechanisms of control, exercised by specialized agencies like the state, law, and police.
  • Informal Social Control : Personal, unofficial, and uncodified mechanisms of control, exercised through daily interactions (e.g., smiles, frowns, body language, criticism, ridicule, family, kinship, and religion).
  • Sanctions : Modes of reward (positive sanctions) or punishment (negative sanctions) that reinforce socially expected forms of behavior.
  • Deviance : Modes of action that do not conform to the norms or values held by most members of a group or society. Ideas of deviance vary widely across cultures and historical periods.
