Sociology: Class 11 (Book 1) — Chapter 5: Doing Sociology: Research Methods

Introduction & Methodological Issues
The Scientific Nature of Sociology

Sociology is classified as a social science because of its systematic methodology for gathering knowledge, distinguishing it from common-sense observations of everyday life.

  • Familiarity vs. Science: Unlike natural sciences, sociology deals with familiar social institutions (groups, norms, relationships) that laypersons experience daily.
  • Methodological Distinction: Sociologists are distinguished not by what they know, but by how they acquire their knowledge through rigorous, scientific procedures.
  • Lived Experience: Sociology seeks to understand both the outsider's objective view and the insider's subjective point of view (e.g., meanings of friendship, religious rituals, or market bargaining).
Objectivity and Subjectivity

Achieving scientific neutrality is uniquely challenging in the social sciences because researchers are active participants in the social world they study.

  • Objectivity : The state of being unbiased, neutral, and relying solely on facts while ignoring personal feelings or attitudes.
  • Subjectivity : Knowledge or interpretations based on individual values, preferences, and social contexts.
  • The Natural vs. Social Science Divide: Geologists or botanists study a natural world external to themselves. Social scientists study the human relations in which they live, making them vulnerable to personal biases, likes, dislikes, and cultural prejudices.
  • Guarding Against Bias: Sociologists employ reflexivity (or self-reflexivity) to continuously examine their own ideas, feelings, and social backgrounds, consciously adopting the perspective of others.
  • Documentation and Citation: Rigorous documentation of all procedures and formal citing of evidence allow other researchers to retrace steps, verify conclusions, and check for unconscious bias.
  • Multiple Truths: The social world contains competing versions of reality (e.g., a shopkeeper's vs. a customer's view of a "good price"). Sociology seeks to understand why people hold these differing views rather than judging which is "correct."
  • Multi-paradigmatic Science : Sociology hosts competing, mutually incompatible schools of thought and theories that coexist within the discipline.

Classification of Sociological Methods
Methodological Classifications

Sociological methods are categorized along several dimensions depending on the nature of the research question, resources, and scale of inquiry.

Method Dimension Core Focus / Definition Key Examples
Quantitative vs. Qualitative Quantitative deals with countable, measurable variables; Qualitative deals with abstract, hard-to-measure phenomena. Quantitative: Censuses, structured surveys.
Qualitative: Participant observation, open-ended interviews.
Observable vs. Non-observable Studies visible, external behaviors vs. internal meanings, values, and interpretations. Observable: Public interactions, spatial layouts.
Non-observable: Religious beliefs, emotional attitudes.
Primary vs. Secondary Generates fresh, first-hand data vs. relies on existing records, documents, and artifacts. Primary: Interviews, fieldwork notes.
Secondary: Historical archives, census reports.
Micro vs. Macro Focuses on small-scale, intimate settings with a single researcher vs. large-scale research with massive populations. Micro: Participant observation, case studies.
Macro: National sample surveys, national censuses.
Participant Observation & Field Work
Field Work in Social Anthropology

Field work established anthropology as a rigorous social science by shifting the discipline away from "armchair scholars" who relied on second-hand colonial accounts.

  • Historical Shift: Early anthropologists (e.g., James Frazer, Emile Durkheim) relied on reports by travelers, missionaries, and colonial administrators. By the early 20th century, first-hand systematic surveys became the standard.
  • Methodological Immersion: Anthropologists live among the studied community for long periods (usually a year or more), learning the local language and participating intimately in daily life to acquire the "insider's" knowledge.
  • The Child Model: The researcher adopts the role of a child, asking endless questions about things the community takes for granted.
  • Key Techniques: Doing a community census, mapping physical layouts, constructing detailed genealogies (family trees) to understand kinship systems, and working with informants (teachers/guides from within the community).

Profile: Bronislaw Malinowski (1884–1942)

  • Theoretical Framework: Functionalism; established fieldwork as the distinctive, mandatory method of social anthropology.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Spent a year and a half (1914–1916) living in a tent in the Trobriand Islands (South Pacific) as an "enemy alien" interned during WWI. Avoided interpreters, learned the native language, and kept detailed daily diaries.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Participant observation, unmediated interaction, native point of view.
Field Work in Sociology

Sociological field work adapted anthropological methods to study diverse, modern, and urban communities rather than remote tribal societies.

  • Contextual Differences: Sociologists do not always "live in" the community but spend most of their time with its members. They study literate, modern populations where research subjects may read and critique the final reports.

Profile: William Foote Whyte (1914–2000)

  • Theoretical Framework: Urban sociology, participant observation of marginalized urban subcultures.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Lived for three and a half years "hanging out" with a street gang of poor, unemployed, first-generation Italian-American youth in an urban slum. Published the classic monograph Street Corner Society (1943).
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Street corner society, participant observation in modern settings, ethical responsibility of studying literate communities.

"Compared to the anthropologist who studies a primitive tribe... the student of a modern American community faces distinctly different problems. In the first place, he is dealing with a literate people. It is certain that some of these people... will read his research report... The people in the district, of course, know it is about them, and even the changed names do not disguise the individuals for them..."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This excerpt highlights the ethical complexities of sociological fieldwork. Unlike early anthropologists studying remote tribes, urban sociologists study literate populations who can read, recognize themselves, and potentially suffer social harm from the publication. This places a heavy ethical responsibility on the researcher to protect identities and minimize harm while maintaining scientific accuracy.

Village Studies in Indian Sociology

In the 1950s, village studies became the dominant focus of Indian sociology and social anthropology, serving as the equivalent of "bounded communities" for field research.

  • Nationalist Context: Nationalist leaders and educated Indians rejected colonial anthropology's obsession with "primitive" tribes, which they felt highlighted non-modern aspects of India. Studying villages was seen as progressive and essential for national development.
  • State Interest: The newly independent Indian government was deeply interested in rural development, "village uplift" programs, and agrarian reforms, making village studies highly relevant.
  • Key Styles of Village Studies:
    • Missionary/Early Style: William and Charlotte Wiser's Behind Mud Walls (five years in a UP village, analyzing the jajmani system ).
    • Classical Anthropological Style: M.N. Srinivas's The Remembered Village (fieldwork in Rampura near Mysore; written from memory after his field notes were destroyed in a fire).
    • Multi-disciplinary Style: S.C. Dube's Indian Village (study of Shamirpet near Secunderabad by a team of agricultural, economic, veterinary, and medical experts).
    • Academic Collaborative Style: The Cornell Village Study Project (1950s, multi-disciplinary study of eastern Uttar Pradesh villages).
Strengths and Limitations of Participant Observation
  • Strengths:
    • Provides a rich, detailed, "insider" perspective of social life.
    • Allows long-term correction of initial biases and mistaken impressions.
    • Enables tracking of social changes across different contexts (e.g., good vs. bad harvest years).
  • Limitations:
    • Scale and Generalizability: Usually limited to a single small community or village; findings cannot be easily generalized to larger regions or nations.
    • Researcher Bias: Risk that the final report represents the voice of the researcher rather than the actual subjects, due to selective note-taking.
    • One-sided Relationship: The researcher asks questions and speaks "for" the people, creating an unequal power dynamic.
    • The Dialogic Solution: Modern scholars suggest translating research back into the local language to seek the community's feedback and correct errors.
The Survey Method & Sampling
The Survey Method

A survey is a quantitative research method designed to provide a comprehensive, wide-ranging overview of a subject based on information from a representative sample of a population.

  • Characteristics: Uses structured questionnaires, involves large teams of researchers and investigators, and targets a specific set of respondents .
  • Modes of Administration: Personal interviews, telephone calls, mailed questionnaires, or digital/internet formats.
  • Main Advantage: Allows researchers to generalize findings to a large population using a relatively small, manageable sample, saving time and money.
Sampling Principles

The validity of a survey depends on the representativeness of its sample , which is selected using two main statistical principles:

  1. Stratification: Recognizing and representing all relevant sub-groups ( strata ) of a population (e.g., dividing a population by class, caste, gender, age, religion, or rural-urban sectors).
  2. Randomisation: Ensuring that the selection of actual units (households, individuals) within each stratum is based purely on chance (probability), using methods like drawing lots, rolling dice, or computer-generated random numbers.
  • Sampling Error : The unavoidable margin of error that arises because a small sample is used to represent a large population. It is a statistical property, not a researcher's mistake, and must be explicitly reported.
  • Non-sampling Error : Errors caused by flaws in research design, poorly framed questions, investigator bias, or untruthful answers from respondents.

The child sex ratio (number of girls per 1,000 boys in the 0–6 age group) is an aggregate macro-variable that can only be identified through large-scale surveys or censuses.

Strengths and Limitations of Surveys
  • Strengths:
    • Enables wide geographic coverage and macro-level generalizations.
    • Provides aggregate pictures of social trends (e.g., unemployment, expenditure, sex ratios).
    • Highly standardized, minimizing individual investigator variations.
  • Limitations:
    • Lack of Depth: High breadth of coverage comes at the cost of deep, localized understanding.
    • Inflexible Instruments: Questionnaires cannot be modified or rephrased once the survey begins.
    • Superficial Interactions: Conducted between strangers; cannot easily gather truthful answers on sensitive, personal, or complex topics.
The Interview Method
Features of the Interview

An interview is a guided conversation between a researcher and a respondent, occupying a middle ground between structured survey questionnaires and open-ended participant observation.

  • Flexibility: The primary advantage of the interview. Questions can be rephrased, reordered, or expanded based on the flow of the conversation.
  • Styles: Ranges from highly structured (specific questions asked to all) to loosely structured (using a checklist of topics).
  • Recording Formats: Video/audio recording, detailed note-taking, or post-interview recall writing.
Strengths and Limitations of Interviews
  • Strengths:
    • Allows deep exploration of personal opinions, feelings, and complex motivations.
    • High flexibility allows the researcher to adapt to the respondent's mood and responses.
    • Can supplement other methods (e.g., interviewing key informants during fieldwork or adding depth to survey data).
  • Limitations:
    • Vulnerability to Moods: Highly dependent on the immediate rapport, concentration, and mutual trust between interviewer and respondent.
    • Unpredictability: Can fail completely if the respondent becomes uneasy, formal, or defensive (especially when recording equipment is introduced).
    • Time-Consuming: Transcribing, editing, and analyzing long conversations requires significant effort.
Glossary & Key Terms
  • Census : A comprehensive survey covering every single member of a population.
  • Genealogy : An family tree outlining familial and kinship relations across generations.
  • Non-sampling Error : Errors in survey results due to mistakes in research design, questionnaire framing, or implementation.
  • Population : In statistics, the larger body of entities (persons, villages, households) from which a sample is drawn.
  • Probability : The likelihood or odds of an event occurring in a statistical sense.
  • Questionnaire : A written list of questions to be asked in a survey or interview.
  • Randomisation : Ensuring that the selection of a sample unit depends purely on chance.
  • Reflexivity : The researcher's capacity to observe, analyze, and document their own biases and social background.
  • Sample : A representative subset drawn from a larger population.
  • Sampling Error : The unavoidable margin of error in a survey because it is based on a sample rather than the entire population.
  • Stratification : The statistical subdivision of a population into distinct, relevant groups (strata) based on criteria like gender, class, or age.
