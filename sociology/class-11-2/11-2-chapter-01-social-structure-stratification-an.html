Sociology: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 1: Social Structure, Stratification and Social Processes in Society

Introduction to Social Structure and Stratification

Sociology examines the dialectical relationship between the individual and society, exploring how personal biographies intersect with social history. Individuals are located within overlapping collectivities (groups, classes, gender, castes, tribes, regions) which define their location in the social structure and determine their access to social resources.

The Concept of Social Structure
  • Social Structure refers to the underlying regularities, patterns, and arrangements in human behavior and relationships rather than random assortments of events.
  • The Building Metaphor: Anthony Giddens compares social structure to a building (walls, floor, roof giving shape). However, this metaphor is limited because social structures are dynamic, constantly reconstructed by the very "bricks" (human actions and relationships) that compose them.
  • Social Reproduction and social structure are closely related; repetitive patterns of behavior across time and space become institutionalized (e.g., school admission procedures, family duties).

Profile: Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)

  • Theoretical Framework: Functionalism, social facts, and social constraint. Argued that society has primacy over the individual and possesses a "firmness" or "solidity" comparable to structures in the material environment.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Empirical analysis of social solidarity, division of labour, and suicide rates across European societies.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Social Constraint , Mechanical Solidarity , Organic Solidarity , Anomie .

Profile: Karl Marx (1818–1883)

  • Theoretical Framework: Historical materialism and conflict theory. Emphasized constraints of social structure while stressing human agency and creativity to both reproduce and change social structures.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Historical analysis of economic systems, capitalist production relations, and class struggles in Europe.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Alienation , Capitalism , Class Struggle , Enforced Cooperation .
Social Stratification

Social Stratification refers to the existence of structured inequalities between groups in society in terms of their access to material or symbolic rewards. It is systematically linked to group membership and tends to persist across generations.

Form of Advantage Definition & Scope Examples in Society
Life Chances Material advantages that improve the quality of life of the recipient. Wealth, income, health access, job security, and recreation opportunities.
Social Status Prestige or high standing in the eyes of other members of society. Social honor, caste prestige, occupational respect, and community standing.
Political Influence Ability of one group to dominate others or influence decision-making. Control over state machinery, policy-making power, and institutional dominance.
Two Ways of Understanding Social Processes

Sociology rejects purely psychological or naturalist explanations that attribute cooperation, competition, or conflict to "human nature." Instead, these processes are explained through the actual social structure of society.

Core Sociological Concept Baseline Definition / Phenomenon Social Manifestation / Practice Impact on Social Stratification & Power
Social Structure Underlying regularities or patterns in human behavior and relationships. Institutionalized behaviors in schools (admission, codes of conduct) and families (marriage practices, duties). Constrains individual action, defining routes of exit and entry, setting limits on agency.
Social Stratification Structured inequalities between groups in society regarding access to resources. Class divisions, caste systems, gender hierarchies, and racial/ethnic stratification. Systematically links advantages to group membership, persisting across generations.
Cooperation Collective effort to meet basic needs and reproduce social life. Division of labour, mutual aid in agricultural tasks (e.g., operating a Charas). Can be voluntary or enforced; often conceals underlying structural conflicts.
Competition Struggle for scarce resources under dominant capitalist norms. Market competition, school admissions, job markets, and standardized examinations. Favors privileged groups who have better access to preparatory resources, reinforcing inequality.
Conflict Clash of interests over scarce material or symbolic resources. Peasant movements, labor strikes, gender struggles over property rights. Challenges dominant groups; can be overt (protests) or covert (subversion, passive resistance).
Functionalist vs. Conflict Perspectives
  • Functionalist Perspective: Focuses on "system requirements" and societal sustenance. Assumes different parts of society have specific roles to maintain the whole. Cooperation, competition, and conflict are seen as universal features that ultimately resolve to maintain system equilibrium. Key requirements include:
    1. Socialisation of new members.
    2. A shared system of communication.
    3. Methods of assigning individuals to roles.
  • Conflict Perspective: Emphasizes how social processes change historically and are shaped by unequal production relations. Focuses on how dominant groups sustain unequal orders through cultural norms, coercion, or violence. Cooperation in unequal societies is often enforced rather than voluntary.
Cooperation and Division of Labour

Cooperation is essential for human survival, but its nature varies significantly across different social structures and historical epochs.

Durkheim's Division of Labour and Solidarity

Durkheim argued that solidarity is the moral force of society. He distinguished between two forms of solidarity:

Feature Mechanical Solidarity Organic Solidarity
Societal Type Pre-industrial, traditional, simple societies. Complex, modern, industrial societies.
Basis of Cohesion Sameness, shared beliefs, and common conscience. Division of labour and economic interdependence.
Division of Labour Low specialization, limited beyond age and sex. High specialization, complex occupational roles.
Individualism Low; individual is absorbed into the collective. High; growth of individualism and personal autonomy.
Marx's View: Alienation and Enforced Cooperation
  • Marx distinguished human cooperation from animal cooperation by the presence of consciousness and active transformation of nature.
  • In class-divided societies, cooperation is not voluntary but enforced by structural constraints.
  • Alienation refers to the loss of control on the part of workers over the concrete content of their labour, the products of their labour, and the organization of their work, transforming cooperation into an alien, coercive force.
Case Study: Gender, Property, and Covert Conflict

A study on women's attitudes toward taking natal property revealed that 41.7% of women evoked themes of love but emphasized apprehension. They chose not to claim their share of natal property because they feared it would sour relations with their brothers or cause their brothers' wives to hate them, leading to exclusion from their natal homes.

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This demonstrates how apparently cooperative behavior (women relinquishing property rights) is actually a product of deep-seated conflicts and patriarchal risk. Women engage in "enforced cooperation" and "maternal altruism" (investing in sons as allies) to secure long-term survival within a discriminatory social structure, using covert subversion rather than overt conflict.

Competition as an Idea and Practice

Competition is not a universal natural phenomenon but a social entity that emerged and became dominant with the rise of modern capitalism.

Capitalism and the Ideology of Competition
  • Capitalism relies on the growth of individualism and competition to maximize efficiency and profits.
  • Underlying Assumptions of Capitalism:
    1. Expansion of trade.
    2. Division of labour.
    3. Specialisation.
    4. Rising productivity.
  • The ideology of competition assumes individuals compete on an equal basis, ignoring structural inequalities (e.g., school drop-out rates among lower castes in India) that exclude large sections of society from the competition entirely.
  • The African School Anecdote: A teacher in a remote African village proposed a competitive race with chocolates as prizes. The children refused to run individually, preferring to cross the finish line together so everyone could share, demonstrating that competitive behavior is culturally learned rather than natural.
Conflict and Cooperation

Conflict \implies a clash of interests over scarce resources. It is a continuous feature of society, though its visibility changes with social development and the assertion of democratic rights.

Overt vs. Covert Conflict
  • Conflict is overt when it is openly expressed (e.g., peasant movements, strikes).
  • The absence of overt protest does not mean conflict is absent; subordinate groups often employ covert strategies to cope with conflict and resist power.
Case Study: Land Conflicts and Technological Shifts

In 1956, Harbaksh (a Rajput) borrowed Rs 100 from Nathu Ahir (Patel) by informally mortgaging 2 acres of land. After Harbaksh died, his successor Ganpat claimed the land back in 1958, offering Rs 200. Nathu refused. Ganpat, a police constable, used violence and police influence to force Nathu to return the land. Later, a village meeting was convened to finalize the exchange.

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This case study highlights how land conflicts are mediated by caste power, state machinery (police influence), and informal village institutions. It also connects to technological shifts: the transition from a Charas (requiring 4 men and 2 pairs of bullocks, necessitating mutual cooperation) to a Rehat (requiring only 1 person and 1 pair of bullocks) reduces the structural necessity for inter-household cooperation, showing how technology alters social relations.
