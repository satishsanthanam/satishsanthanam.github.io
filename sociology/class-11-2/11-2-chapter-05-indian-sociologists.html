Sociology: Class 11 (Book 2) — Chapter 5: Indian Sociologists

1. Evolution of Sociology in India
Historical Context and Institutionalization

Sociology is a relatively young discipline, established in Europe about a century ago and introduced formally in India in the early 20th century.

  • Formal university teaching of sociology began in 1919 at the University of Bombay.
  • In the 1920s, the Universities of Calcutta and Lucknow initiated teaching and research programs in sociology and anthropology.
  • Early Indian sociologists had to adapt Western sociological frameworks to the unique historical, colonial, and cultural context of India.
The Pioneers of Indian Anthropology

Profile: L.K. Ananthakrishna Iyer (1861-1937)

  • Theoretical Framework: Self-taught anthropologist who pioneered ethnographic surveys in princely states under British administration.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Conducted comprehensive ethnographic surveys of Cochin and Mysore states; functioned as the unpaid Superintendent of Ethnography.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Voluntary ethnography, state-sponsored social mapping.

Profile: Sarat Chandra Roy (1871-1942)

  • Theoretical Framework: Accidental anthropologist and lawyer who studied tribal customs to interpret laws for courts.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Intensive fieldwork among tribal communities of the Chhotanagpur region (Oraon, Mundas, and Kharias).
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Tribal customary law, founder of the journal Man in India (1922).
2. Govind Sadashiv Ghurye (1893-1983)
Biographical Profile and Institutional Contributions

Profile: G.S. Ghurye (1893-1983)

  • Theoretical Framework: Indological and diffusionist perspective; combined social anthropology and sociology into a composite discipline.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Textual analysis of classical Sanskrit literature combined with empirical studies on caste, tribes, and kinship.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: backward Hindus , segmental division, hierarchical division, endogamy.
Ghurye on Caste and Race

Ghurye provided a detailed critique of Herbert Risley's racial theory of caste in his seminal work Caste and Race in India (1932).

  • Risley's view: Caste originated in race; higher castes approximated Indo-Aryan traits, while lower castes belonged to non-Aryan aboriginal groups.
  • Ghurye's critique: Risley's thesis of upper castes being Aryan and lower castes being non-Aryan was broadly true only for northern India (Hindustan proper).
  • In other parts of India, inter-group differences in anthropometric measurements were not systematic, indicating long-term racial mixing.
Ghurye's Six Features of Caste
  1. Segmental Division: Society is divided into closed, mutually exclusive compartments decided strictly by birth.
  2. Hierarchical Division: Every caste is strictly unequal to every other, arranged in a graded hierarchy.
  3. Restrictions on Social Interaction: Elaborate rules governing food sharing and social contact, based on ideas of purity and pollution .
  4. Differential Rights and Duties: Castes have distinct religious and secular rights and obligations.
  5. Restricted Choice of Occupation: Occupations are hereditary and function as a rigid division of labor.
  6. Strict Restrictions on Marriage: Enforced through caste endogamy (marrying within the caste) and rules of exogamy .
The Tribal Debate: Ghurye vs. Elwin

During the 1930s and 1940s, a major debate emerged regarding the place of tribal societies in India.

  • Protectionists (Verrier Elwin): Believed tribals were primitive groups with distinct cultures who needed state protection from Hindu exploitation and cultural degradation.
  • Nationalists (Ghurye): Argued that tribals were backward Hindus who had been in constant interaction with mainstream Hinduism over a long period and should be integrated into the development process.
3. Dhurjati Prasad Mukerji (1894-1961)
Biographical Profile and Theoretical Framework

Profile: D.P. Mukerji (1894-1961)

  • Theoretical Framework: Marxist method of social analysis; focused on the synthesis of tradition and modernity.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Historical-sociological analysis of Indian culture, music, and social traditions.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: living tradition , shruti , smriti , anubhava .
Tradition and Change

D.P. Mukerji argued that the crucial distinctive feature of India was its social system, which was over-developed compared to its political and economic dimensions.

  • An Indian sociologist must first be an Indian, sharing in the folk-ways, mores, customs, and traditions to understand the social system.
  • Tradition is a living tradition : it maintains links with the past while adapting to the present and evolving over time.
  • Indian society is group-oriented rather than individualistic; social action is driven by group, sect, or caste-action.
Principles of Social Change

Mukerji identified three principles of change recognized in Indian traditions:

  1. Shruti: Revealed scriptures and high traditions.
  2. Smriti: Remembered traditions and legal codes.
  3. Anubhava: Personal experience, which Mukerji identified as the revolutionary principle of change that quickly flowers into collective experience (e.g., the Bhakti and Sufi movements).
4. Akshay Ramanlal Desai (1915-1994)
Biographical Profile and Marxist Sociology

Profile: A.R. Desai (1915-1994)

  • Theoretical Framework: Consistent Marxist analysis of social institutions, nationalism, and the state.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Historical-materialist analysis of Indian nationalism, peasant struggles, and agrarian relations.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Myth of the welfare state, Marxist theory of the state.
Critique of the Welfare State

In his essay 'The myth of the welfare state', Desai provided a detailed critique of the capitalist welfare state using specific test criteria.

  • A true welfare state must be a positive state (not laissez-faire ), democratic, and involve a mixed economy.
  • Desai's test criteria include: freedom from poverty/discrimination, removal of income inequalities, subservience of profit motive to community needs, stable development, and full employment.
  • Desai concluded that modern capitalist states (like the USA and Britain) fail these criteria, making the welfare state a myth.
5. Mysore Narasimhachar Srinivas (1916-1999)
Biographical Profile and Methodological Contributions

Profile: M.N. Srinivas (1916-1999)

  • Theoretical Framework: Structural-functionalist perspective; emphasized intensive fieldwork and participant observation.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Intensive fieldwork in Rampura village near Mysore; studied the Coorgs of South India.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Sanskritisation, dominant caste, village studies.
Srinivas on the Indian Village

Srinivas was instrumental in making village studies the dominant field in Indian sociology during the 1950s and 1960s.

  • He argued against the view of British administrator-anthropologists who saw Indian villages as unchanging, self-sufficient little republics .
  • Srinivas showed that villages had experienced considerable historical change and were integrated into regional economic, social, and political networks.
  • The Dumont-Srinivas Debate: Louis Dumont argued that the village was not a significant social category and that institutions like caste were far more important. Srinivas countered that the village was a vital, unifying social entity in rural life.
6. Conceptual and Comparative Matrices
Conceptual Definition & Characterization Matrix
Core Sociological Concept Baseline Definition / Phenomenon Social Manifestation / Practice Impact on Social Stratification & Power
Caste (Ghurye) Segmental and hierarchical division of society based on birth. Enforced through endogamy, occupational restrictions, and purity/pollution rules. Reinforces rigid social inequality and unequal distribution of rights/duties.
Living Tradition (D.P. Mukerji) A dynamic system of social practices that maintains links with the past while adapting to the present. Manifests as collective group action, folk-ways, and adaptation of customs. Shapes social change through internal non-economic factors like anubhava.
Welfare State (A.R. Desai) A state that actively intervenes in the economy and society to ensure citizen well-being. Manifests as mixed economy, democratic institutions, and social security programs. Often acts as a myth that fails to resolve capitalist inequalities and class divisions.
Indian Village (M.N. Srinivas) A key social entity and site of research representing rural social life and identity. Manifests as inter-caste relations, agricultural cooperation, and regional networks. Serves as a unifying identity that challenges the view of static, isolated 'little republics'.
