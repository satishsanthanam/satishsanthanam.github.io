Introduction to Demography and Sociology

Demography is the systematic study of populations, derived from the Greek words demos (people) and graphein (to describe). It analyzes trends and processes associated with population size, patterns of births, deaths, migration, and the structure and composition of the population.

Types of Demography
  • Formal Demography : A highly quantitative field focusing on the measurement, analysis, and mathematical forecasting of population change and composition.
  • Social Demography : A broader field that investigates the social, economic, and political causes and consequences of population structures and changes.
Demography and the Emergence of Sociology

The academic establishment of sociology in Europe during the late 18th century was closely linked to the rise of nation-states and the development of modern statistics. Modern states began collecting social statistics to manage public health, policing, taxation, and urban governance.

  • The American Census of 1790 is considered the first modern census.
  • In India, the British colonial administration conducted the first systematic census between 1867 and 1872, establishing regular decennial censuses from 1881 onwards.
  • Post-independence India has continued this decennial practice since 1951, with the 2011 census being the latest recorded in this \text.

Theories of Population Growth
The Malthusian Theory of Population Growth

Thomas Robert Malthus proposed a pessimistic view of population growth, arguing that human populations grow faster than the means of subsistence.

  • Population increases in a geometric progression ($2, 4, 8, 16, 32, \dots$).
  • Agricultural production increases in an arithmetic progression ($2, 4, 6, 8, 10, \dots$).
  • This imbalance leads to inevitable poverty unless controlled by checks.
  • Preventive Checks : Voluntary human actions to reduce growth, such as postponing marriage, sexual abstinence, or celibacy.
  • Positive Checks : Natural disasters, famines, and epidemics that violently reduce population size.

Profile: Thomas Robert Malthus (1766–1834)

  • Theoretical Framework: Classical political economy and demographic pessimism.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Theoretical analysis of population dynamics and resource constraints, published in his Essay on Population (1798).
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Geometric vs. arithmetic progression, preventive checks, positive checks.

Criticisms of Malthusian Theory: Historical developments in Europe refuted Malthus's predictions. Agricultural productivity rose dramatically due to technological advancements, and birth rates declined alongside rising standards of living. Liberal and Marxist theorists argued that poverty is caused by the unequal distribution of economic resources rather than absolute population growth.

The Theory of Demographic Transition

This theory suggests that population growth is linked to overall levels of economic development, with every society passing through three distinct stages:

  1. Stage 1 (Underdeveloped/Backward): High birth rates and high death rates result in low net population growth.
  2. Stage 2 (Transitional/Developing): Death rates fall rapidly due to advances in medicine, public health, and nutrition, while birth rates remain high, leading to a population explosion .
  3. Stage 3 (Developed): Both birth and death rates are low, resulting in low population growth in a highly industrialized society.
Common Concepts and Indicators

Demographic indicators are typically expressed as rates or ratios comparing specific statistics against a standard population base.

  • Birth Rate : Total number of live births in a given area per 1000 of the population per year.
  • Death Rate : Total number of deaths in a given area per 1000 of the population per year.
  • Rate of Natural Increase : The difference between the birth rate and the death rate. When this difference is near zero, the population has reached replacement level .
  • Fertility Rate : The number of live births per 1000 women in the child-bearing age group (typically 15 to 49 years).
  • Infant Mortality Rate : The number of deaths of infants before completing one year of age per 1000 live births.
  • Maternal Mortality Rate : The number of women who die in childbirth per 100,000 live births.
  • Life Expectancy : The estimated number of years an average person is expected to survive based on age-specific death rates.
  • Sex Ratio : The number of females per 1000 males in a given population.
  • Age Structure : The proportion of the population in different age cohorts.
  • Dependency Ratio : The ratio of dependent age groups (under 15 and over 64 years) to the working-age population (15 to 64 years). Formula: $$\text{Dependency Ratio} = \frac{\text{Population under 15} + \text{Population over 64}}{\text{Population aged 15-64}} \times 100$$
  • Demographic Dividend : A temporary economic advantage resulting from a low dependency ratio, where the working-age population is significantly larger than the dependent population.

Famines and Epidemics

Historically, high death rates in India were driven by famines and epidemic diseases. The decline in mortality after 1921 was achieved through better control over these factors.

The Influenza Epidemic of 1918–19 killed about 12.5 million people in India, representing nearly 5% of the total population. The pandemic spread rapidly due to troop movements during World War I and caused massive global mortality.

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Epidemics and pandemics expose the vulnerability of populations under colonial rule, where public health infrastructure was severely limited. The high mortality rate reflects the intersection of biological vulnerability with structural neglect and poor living conditions.

Famines were also a major cause of mortality. Sociologist Amartya Sen demonstrated that famines are often caused not by a decline in food production, but by a failure of entitlements —the inability of vulnerable groups to buy or access food due to poverty, unemployment, and distribution failures.

Causes of the Declining Child Sex Ratio

The decline is steepest in prosperous regions like Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, and Maharashtra, indicating that poverty is not the primary cause. Key factors include:

  • Son Preference : Deeply ingrained cultural norms that value male children over female children for economic, religious, and lineage reasons.
  • Neglect of female infants, leading to higher mortality rates.
  • Misuse of modern medical technologies, such as ultrasound/sonograms, for sex-selective abortions.

To combat this, the government enacted the Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Regulation and Prevention of Misuse) Act in 1996 (strengthened in 2003) and launched campaigns like Beti Bachao, Beti Padhao to alter social attitudes.

Literacy, Rural-Urban Differences, and Population Policy
Literacy Trends

Literacy is a key instrument of empowerment. While overall literacy has risen to 73% in 2011, significant disparities remain:

  • Gender Gap: Female literacy (64.6%) lags behind male literacy (80.9%) by 16.3%.
  • Social Groups: Historically marginalized groups like Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST) have lower literacy rates.
  • Regional Variations: States like Kerala approach universal literacy, while states like Bihar lag far behind.
Rural-Urban Differences

According to the 2011 Census, 68.8% of India's population lives in rural areas, while 31.2% lives in urban areas. Urbanization is accelerating due to:

  • Rural-to-urban migration driven by agrarian distress and the decline of common property resources (forests, ponds, grazing lands).
  • The attraction of cities for social reasons, particularly the relative anonymity they offer, which can protect marginalized castes from daily discrimination.
Population Policy in India

India was the first country to launch an official state-sponsored population policy, the National Family Planning Programme, in 1952.

  • During the National Emergency (1975–76), the program suffered a major setback due to coercive mass sterilization campaigns.
  • Post-Emergency, the program was renamed the National Family Welfare Programme, shifting from coercive targets to voluntary, broad-based socio-demographic goals.
  • The National Health Policy 2017 outlines targets for reducing fertility, maternal mortality, and infant mortality while increasing public health expenditure.
