Sociology: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 3: Social Institutions: Continuity and Change

Caste and the Caste System
Caste in the Past

Caste is a distinct social institution uniquely associated with the Indian sub-continent, though similar social stratification systems exist globally. While rooted in Hindu society, caste practices have historically spread to non-Hindu communities including Muslims, Christians, and Sikhs.

The English word caste originates from the Portuguese casta , meaning pure breed. In Indian languages, the institution is understood through two distinct concepts: varna and jati .

Dimension Varna Jati
Literal Meaning Colour Species, kind, or breed
Scope All-India broad aggregative classification Regional or local sub-classification
Divisions Strictly four-fold ( brahmana , kshatriya , vaishya , shudra ) Thousands of localized castes and sub-castes
Rigidity Historically flexible in early Vedic period (not determined by birth) Highly rigid, hereditary, and localized hierarchy

Defining Features of Caste:

  • Determined by Birth: A child is born into the caste of their parents. Caste is never a matter of choice, and one can neither change nor leave it.
  • Endogamy: Strict rules restrict marriage to members of the same caste group.
  • Food and Food-Sharing Rules: Prescribed rules dictate what kinds of food may be eaten and with whom food can be shared.
  • Hierarchical Arrangement: Every caste occupies a specific ordered rank in a ladder-like system based on ritual status.
  • Segmental Organisation: Castes contain internal sub-divisions (sub-castes and sub-sub-castes).
  • Hereditary Occupation: Traditionally linked to specific occupations passed down through generations, preventing occupational mobility.

Purity and Pollution: The hierarchical ordering of castes is governed by the distinction between purity and pollution . Ritual purity is associated with closeness to the sacred, while ritual pollution is associated with contact with substances considered impure (e.g., leather, waste, bodily fluids). This distinction underpins the exclusion and subordination of lower castes.

Colonialism and Caste

Colonial administrative practices fundamentally altered the institution of caste, transforming fluid regional identities into rigid, legally defined categories.

  • Ethnographic Surveys: British administrators conducted intensive surveys on the "customs and manners" of various tribes and castes to govern efficiently.
  • The Decennial Census: Begun in the 1860s and made regular from 1881. The 1901 Census directed by Herbert Risley sought to classify and record the precise social hierarchy of all castes. This led to massive caste mobilization as groups petitioned for higher ranks.
  • Legal Recognition: The Government of India Act of 1935 gave legal recognition to lists of "Scheduled Castes" and "Scheduled Tribes", formalizing the categorization of the most marginalized groups.
Caste in the Present

Post-Independence India witnessed a complex interplay of caste dynamics, marked by state-led reform, political mobilization, and economic shifts.

  • Constitutional Abolition: The independent state committed to abolishing caste distinctions and explicitly outlawed untouchability in the Constitution, while introducing reservations for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Sanskritisation: A process coined by M.N. Srinivas where lower or middle castes attempt to raise their social status by adopting the ritual, domestic, and social practices of higher-status castes.
  • Dominant Caste: A term coined by M.N. Srinivas referring to intermediate castes that acquired land rights through post-Independence land reforms, possessed large populations, and wielded significant political and economic power (e.g., Yadavs of Bihar/UP, Vokkaligas of Karnataka, Reddys of Andhra Pradesh, Jats of Punjab/Haryana).
  • The Paradox of Visibility: For the urban upper-caste middle class, caste has become "invisible" because they have successfully converted their historical caste privilege into modern educational and economic capital. Conversely, for lower castes, caste remains highly "visible" as they rely on caste identity as a collective asset to access state support and combat ongoing discrimination.
Tribal Communities
Classifications of Tribal Societies

Tribes in India are defined as ancient communities that did not practice a religion with a written \text, lacked a state or formal political structure, and did not have sharp class divisions. They are classified based on permanent and acquired traits.

Classification Basis Trait Categories Details and Distribution
Permanent Traits Geographical Region 85% live in "Middle India" (Gujarat to West Bengal/Odisha); 11% in the North Eastern states; 3% elsewhere.
Language Four groups: Indo-Aryan (shared, 1% tribals), Dravidian (shared, 3% tribals), Austric (100% tribals), and Tibeto-Burman (over 80% tribals).
Physical-Racial Negrito, Australoid, Mongoloid, Dravidian, and Aryan.
Population Size Ranges from millions (Gonds, Bhils, Santhals, Oraons, Minas, Bodos, Mundas) to under a hundred (Andamanese islanders). Tribals constitute 8.6% of India's population (2011 Census).
Acquired Traits Livelihood Mode Fishermen, food gatherers, hunters, shifting cultivators, peasants, plantation workers, and industrial laborers.
Assimilation into Hindu Society Measures the degree of integration, ranging from high-status absorption to low-status Shudra-equivalent assimilation.
Tribe: Career of a Concept

During the 1960s and 1970s, anthropologists debated whether tribes were fundamentally different from castes or merely represented a less stratified end of a continuum with Hindu peasant society. Opponents argued that tribes were distinct because they lacked the concepts of purity and pollution central to the caste system.

Scholars now argue that tribes are not "pristine" societies untouched by civilization, but are "secondary" phenomena shaped by historical, exploitative, and colonialist contact with state societies.

National Development versus Tribal Development

The state's focus on national development (building dams, factories, and mines) has disproportionately displaced tribal communities, as tribal lands are concentrated in mineral-rich and forest-covered regions.

  • Loss of Forests: Systematic exploitation of forest resources began in the British era and continued post-Independence, depriving tribals of their primary livelihood.
  • In-migration: Industrialization has caused heavy in-migration of non-tribals, diluting tribal populations and reducing them to minorities in regions like Jharkhand and Tripura.
  • Tribal Identity Today: Modern tribal identities are formed through resistance against state-led dispossession and the emergence of an educated tribal middle class asserting cultural and economic rights.
Family and Kinship
Diverse Forms of the Family

The family is a core social institution that can be structured in various ways based on residence, inheritance, and authority.

Structural Dimension Form A Form B
Composition Nuclear Family: Consists of one set of parents and their children. Extended/Joint Family: Multiple couples and generations living together.
Rule of Residence Patrilocal: Newly married couple lives with the husband's parents. Matrilocal: Newly married couple lives with the wife's parents.
Rule of Inheritance Patrilineal: Property passes from father to son. Matrilineal: Property passes from mother to daughter.
Authority Structure Patriarchal: Men exercise authority and dominance. Matriarchal: Women play a dominant role (theoretical concept; no empirical evidence of true matriarchy exists).
Sociological Thinkers and Reformers

Profile: Jotirao Govindrao Phule (1827–1890)

  • Theoretical Framework: Anti-caste social reform, human rights, and social justice for low-caste people.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Active in Western India (Maharashtra); denounced the scriptural rules of purity and pollution.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Founded the Satyashodhak Samaj (Truth Seekers Society) in 1873.

Profile: Savitri Bai Phule (1831–1897)

  • Theoretical Framework: Women's education, anti-caste empowerment, and social welfare.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Founded the country's first school for girls in Pune; started night schools for Shudras, Ati-Shudras, and laborers.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Pioneer of women's education and social reform in Maharashtra.

Profile: Ayyankali (1863–1914)

  • Theoretical Framework: Dalit mobilization, public space access, and educational rights.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Led struggles in Kerala for the rights of lower castes to walk on public roads and for Dalit children to attend schools.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Lower-caste assertion and public space democratization.

Profile: Sri Narayana Guru (1856–1928)

  • Theoretical Framework: Spiritual humanism, universal brotherhood, and anti-caste reform.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Led a quiet social revolution in Kerala against the ill effects of the caste system.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Preached "One Caste, One Religion, One God for all men".

Profile: Periyar (E.V. Ramasami Naickar) (1879–1973)

  • Theoretical Framework: Rationalism, self-respect movement, and anti-caste mobilization.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Led the lower-caste movement in South India (Tamil Nadu).
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Self-Respect Movement, rationalism, and gender equality.

Profile: M.N. Srinivas (1916–1999)

  • Theoretical Framework: Structural-functionalism, village studies, and social change in India.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Conducted extensive fieldwork in Rampura, Karnataka; published *The Remembered Village*.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Sanskritisation , Dominant Caste .
