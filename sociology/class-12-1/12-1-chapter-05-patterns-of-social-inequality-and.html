Sociology: Class 12 (Book 1) — Chapter 5: Patterns of Social Inequality and Exclusion

What Is Social About Social Inequality and Exclusion?

Social inequality and exclusion are often perceived as individual failings or natural outcomes of differences in ability and effort. However, sociological analysis reveals that these patterns are deeply structural and systemic rather than individual. There are three key reasons why these phenomena are fundamentally "social":

  • They are about groups, not individuals: Social inequality is a relation between categories of people (e.g., caste, class, gender, race) rather than a reflection of individual differences in talent or hard work.
  • They are not purely economic: Although strongly linked to economic disparities, social inequality is also driven by non-economic factors such as social status, honor, and cultural capital.
  • They are systematic and structured: There is a definite, predictable pattern to social inequalities, which are reproduced over generations through institutionalized systems.
Forms of Capital and Social Resources

According to sociologist Pierre Bourdieu, social resources can be categorized into three distinct forms of capital, which often overlap and can be converted into one another:

  • Economic Capital : Material assets, property, income, and financial wealth.
  • Cultural Capital : Educational qualifications, status, linguistic skills, and cultural knowledge that confer social advantages.
  • Social Capital : Networks of contacts, social associations, and influence that facilitate access to opportunities.
Three Principles of Social Stratification

Sociologists use the term social stratification to refer to a system by which categories of people in a society are ranked in a hierarchy. This system is governed by three key principles:

  1. Social stratification is a characteristic of society, not simply a function of individual differences: It is a society-wide system that unequally distributes resources regardless of personal abilities. In technologically primitive societies, stratification was rudimentary; in advanced societies with surplus production, resources are highly stratified.
  2. Social stratification persists over generations: A person's social position is largely ascribed at birth. Children inherit the social positions of their parents. This is reinforced by the practice of endogamy (marriage within the same social group/caste).
  3. Social stratification is supported by patterns of belief, or ideology: No system of stratification survives without being viewed as fair or inevitable. For example, the caste system is traditionally justified through the ideology of purity and pollution .
Prejudice, Stereotypes, and Discrimination

Social exclusion and inequality are sustained through cognitive and behavioral mechanisms that target specific social groups:

Conceptual Definitions
Core Sociological Concept Baseline Definition / Phenomenon Social Manifestation / Practice Impact on Social Stratification & Power
Prejudice Pre-conceived opinions or attitudes held by members of one group towards another, formed in advance of direct evidence or familiarity. Holding unfavorable views about a minority group based on hearsay; resisting change even when presented with new facts. Legitimizes social distance and reinforces psychological barriers between dominant and subordinate groups.
Stereotypes Fixed, inflexible, and highly simplified characterisations of an entire group of people. Categorizing communities as "lazy," "cunning," or labeling certain groups as "martial races" or "untrustworthy." Erases individual variation, treating an entire community as a single homogenous category with a single trait.
Discrimination Actual exclusionary behavior and practices directed towards members of a specific group. Refusing to hire someone based on their religion or caste; denying housing to minority professionals in metropolitan colonies. Disqualifies marginalized groups from equal opportunities, directly translating prejudice into economic and social deprivation.
Social Exclusion Systematic processes by which individuals or groups are cut off from full involvement in the wider society. Denying access to essential goods and services like education, healthcare, transportation, banking, and legal protection. Prevents individuals from living a full and active life, reinforcing poverty and structural isolation.
Cross-Cultural Comparison: Apartheid in South Africa

"To ensure their political control, the White European minority developed the policy of apartheid, or separation of the races. An informal practice for many years, apartheid became law in 1948 and was used to deny the Black majority South African citizenship, ownership of land, and a formal voice in government... millions of Blacks were forcibly relocated to 'Bantustans' or 'homelands' – dirt-poor districts with no infrastructure or industry or jobs."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Apartheid represents an extreme, state-sanctioned system of racial stratification. Like the Indian caste system, it used physical segregation, legal barriers, and economic exploitation to concentrate wealth and power in the hands of a minority while systematically impoverishing and disenfranchising the majority.

Caste and Tribe: Systems of Inequality
The Caste System as a Discriminatory Institution

The caste system is a distinct Indian social institution that legitimises and enforces practices of discrimination against people born into particular castes. Historically, it classified people by occupation and status, enforcing rigid boundaries. While modern \times have seen a weakening of the link between caste and occupation at the micro level, the correlation remains remarkably stable at the macro level.

  • Macro-level stability: Privileged, high-status sections of society continue to be overwhelmingly "upper" caste, while disadvantaged, low-status sections are dominated by "lower" castes.
  • Untouchability : The most extreme and vicious aspect of the caste system, prescribing stringent social sanctions against those at the bottom of the purity-pollution scale. It is defined by three main dimensions:
    • Exclusion: Forced physical segregation, denial of access to common resources (e.g., drinking water sources, temples).
    • Humiliation-Subordination: Compulsory signs of deference, verbal abuse, and denial of dignity.
    • Exploitation: Imposition of forced, unpaid, or under-paid labor, and confiscation of property.

State and Non-State Initiatives Against Discrimination

The Indian state has implemented several legal and policy measures to address historical caste inequalities:

  • Reservations: Setting aside a percentage of seats in legislatures, government jobs, and educational institutions for SCs, STs, and later OBCs, proportional to their population share.
  • Constitutional Provisions: Article 17 of the Constitution abolished untouchability in 1950.
  • Key Legislation:
    • Caste Disabilities Removal Act of 1850: Disallowed curtailment of rights due to change of religion or caste.
    • Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act of 1989: Strengthened legal provisions to punish acts of violence and humiliation against Dalits and Adivasis.
    • 93rd Constitutional Amendment Act of 2005: Introduced reservations for OBCs in institutions of higher education.
The Other Backward Classes (OBCs)

Unlike Dalits, OBCs were not subjected to untouchability but faced severe social and educational disadvantages. They are a highly diverse group, defined negatively by what they are not (neither forward castes nor Dalits).

  • First Backward Classes Commission (1953): Headed by Kaka Kalelkar; its recommendations were sidelined due to political climate.
  • Second Backward Classes Commission (Mandal Commission): Appointed in the late 1970s under B.P. Mandal; its recommendation of 27% reservation for OBCs in central government jobs and educational institutions was implemented in 1990, transforming national politics.
  • Internal Disparities: A sharp divide exists between "upper OBCs" (landed, dominant rural castes) and "lower OBCs" (impoverished, economically similar to Dalits).
Adivasi Struggles and Internal Colonialism

The Scheduled Tribes (Adivasis) have faced systematic marginalisation driven by resource extraction and state-led development:

  • Loss of Forest Rights: Beginning in the late 19th century, the colonial government reserved forest tracts for timber production, criminalising traditional Adivasi livelihoods. This monopoly continued after Independence.
  • Displacement: Capital-intensive industrialisation, mining, and mega-dam projects (e.g., Sardar Sarovar dam on Narmada, Polavaram dam on Godavari) displaced millions of Adivasis without adequate compensation or rehabilitation, a process termed internal colonialism .
  • Political Assertion: Unlike Dalits, Adivasis were concentrated in contiguous geographic regions, enabling them to demand and achieve separate statehood for Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh in post-Independence India.
Struggle for Women's Equality and Rights

Gender inequality is often naturalized based on biological differences. However, sociology demonstrates that gender disparities are socially constructed, as evidenced by the existence of matrilineal societies (e.g., the Khasis of Meghalaya, Nairs of Kerala) where women inherit property and head households.

19th-Century Social Reform Movements

The "women's question" arose in modern India through middle-class social reform movements, drawing on both Western democratic ideals and classical Indian texts:

Profile: Raja Rammohun Roy (1772–1833)

  • Theoretical Framework: Combined Western rationalism with humanitarian interpretations of Hindu shastras to challenge orthodox practices.
  • Key Campaigns: Led the anti-sati campaign in Bengal, which resulted in the legal banning of Sati; founded the Brahmo Samaj in 1828.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Natural rights, humanitarianism, shastric sanction against violence.

Profile: Jyotiba Phule (1827–1890)

  • Theoretical Framework: Radical critique of Brahminical patriarchy and caste hegemony; linked the subordination of women directly to the caste system.
  • Key Campaigns: Founded the Satyashodhak Samaj; opened the first school for girls and untouchables in Pune.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Anti-caste patriarchy, Satyashodhak (truth-seeking), education as liberation.

Profile: Tarabai Shinde (1850–1910)

  • Theoretical Framework: Early feminist critique of male-dominated society, exposing the double standards applied to men and women.
  • Key Publications: Wrote Stree Purush Tulana (Comparison of Men and Women) in 1882 as a protest against the harsh treatment of widows.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Gender double standards, critique of religious patriarchy.

Profile: Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (1880–1932)

  • Theoretical Framework: Islamic feminist critique of extreme gender segregation (purdah) and denial of education to women.
  • Key Publications: Wrote Sultana's Dream (1905), a pioneering work of feminist science fiction depicting "Ladyland" where gender roles are reversed.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Purdah, educational emancipation, gender role reversal.
Nationalist Movement and Post-Independence Shifts

Women's rights became integral to the nationalist struggle, culminating in formal commitments to equality:

  • Karachi Session of the Indian National Congress (1931): Issued a historic declaration on the Fundamental Rights of Citizenship, committing to:
    1. Equality of all citizens before the law, irrespective of religion, caste, creed, or sex.
    2. No disability attaching to any citizen in public employment or office by reason of sex.
    3. Franchise based on universal adult suffrage.
  • The 1970s Resurgence: The women's movement re-emerged with a focus on "modern" issues: representation in media, gendered impacts of development, domestic violence, rape, and the declining child sex ratio.
The Struggles of the Disabled

The differently abled are marginalized not merely by physical or mental impairments, but by a society built exclusively to cater to the able-bodied. Disability studies scholar Anita Ghai compares the social invisibility of the disabled to Ralph Ellison's concept of the "Invisible Man."

Social Construction of Disability

Sociology challenges the common features central to the public perception of disability:

Common Public Perception Sociological Deconstruction
Disability is a biological given. Disability is socially constructed; physical environments and social structures render individuals "disabled."
Problems originate from the individual's impairment. Problems originate from an unaccommodating society (e.g., buildings without ramps, lack of accessible transport).
The disabled person is a victim ("bechara"). The victim status is a cultural construction that denies agency and reinforces dependency.
Disability is linked to past karma (destiny). Disability is a social and economic issue, closely linked to poverty, malnutrition, and poor healthcare.
Disability and Poverty

There is a close, cyclical relationship between disability and poverty:

  • Malnutrition, frequent childbirth in weakened mothers, lack of immunization, and hazardous working conditions in overcrowded homes increase the incidence of disability among the poor.
  • Disability, in turn, exacerbates poverty by creating economic strain, isolation, and limiting employment opportunities for both the individual and their family.
Census 2011: Approach to Disability

The Census of India 2011 adopted a highly structured approach to capture disability data (Box 5.5):

  • Information was collected during the Population Enumeration phase via the "Household Schedule."
  • Enumerators were trained to directly contact the disabled person in the household.
  • The census expanded its scope to collect information on eight distinct types of disabilities, compared to five in the 2001 Census.
  • Questions were designed using simple nomenclature for easy comprehension by both enumerators and respondents.
