Sociology: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 2: Cultural Change

Social Reform Movements in the 19th and Early 20th Century

The social reform movements of the 19th and early 20th centuries arose as creative responses to the challenges posed by colonial rule, combining modern western liberal ideas with a reinterpretation of traditional literature to target social evils like sati, child marriage, ban on widow remarriage, and caste discrimination.

Profile: Raja Ram Mohun Roy (1772–1833)

  • Theoretical Framework: Humanitarianism, natural rights doctrines, and rationalist interpretation of Hindu shastras.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Textual analysis of Hindu scriptures; public campaigns and petitions against social evils in Bengal.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Anti-sati advocacy, monotheism, and rationalist reform.

Profile: Sir Syed Ahmed Khan (1817–1898)

  • Theoretical Framework: Islamic reformism, emphasizing the compatibility of Koranic revelations with the laws of nature and modern science.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Educational institution building (Aligarh Movement); theological reinterpretation.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Ijtihad (free enquiry), modern scientific education for Muslims.

Profile: Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922)

  • Theoretical Framework: Feminist critique of patriarchal Hindu texts; Christian-influenced social welfare.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Travel across India and internationally; establishment of shelters and schools for widows (Sharada Sadan).
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Gender equality, widow rehabilitation, critique of high-caste patriarchy.

Profile: Jotiba Phule (1827–1890)

  • Theoretical Framework: Anti-Brahminical critique, glorification of the pre-Aryan egalitarian age, and universal education.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Establishing the Satyashodhak Samaj; opening the first school for women in Pune (1848).
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Non-Brahmin assertion, gender and caste emancipation, critique of priestly monopoly.
Modern Framework of Change (Satish Saberwal)

Sociologist Satish Saberwal outlines three key aspects of the modern framework of change in colonial India:

  • Modes of Communication: Rapid transmission of ideas via printing press, telegraph, microphone, steamships, and railways, enabling cross-regional dialogue (e.g., Keshav Chandra Sen visiting Madras in 1864).
  • Forms of Organisation: Emergence of modern secular and religious associations (e.g., Brahmo Samaj in Bengal, Arya Samaj in Punjab, All-India Muslim Ladies Conference founded in 1914).
  • Nature of Ideas: Introduction of liberalism, freedom, new concepts of homemaking, mother-daughter roles, and self-conscious cultural pride.
Processes of Cultural Change: Conceptual Matrix

The cultural transformations in modern India are understood through four major sociological processes. These processes often overlap, co-exist, and operate paradoxically.

Core Sociological Concept Baseline Definition / Phenomenon Social Manifestation / Practice Impact on Social Stratification & Power
Sanskritisation Process where low castes/tribes adopt customs, rituals, beliefs, and lifestyles of high/twice-born (dwija) castes. Adoption of vegetarianism, wearing sacred thread, performing Vedic rituals, changing caste names. Leads to positional change for specific groups within the caste hierarchy, but does not alter the structural inequality of the system.
Westernisation Changes brought in Indian society and culture as a result of over 150 years of British rule, spanning technology, institutions, ideology, and values. Adoption of western attire, food habits, English education, nuclear family structures, and scientific temper. Creates a westernised middle-class elite; often leads to generational conflicts and cultural hybridization.
Modernisation Shift from local/parochial ties to universal commitments, scientific calculation, and individual-centric social structures. Occupational choice based on achievement rather than birth; separation of work from family/residence. Undermines traditional ascribed statuses, promoting achieved status and democratic values.
Secularisation Decline in the social and material influence of religion, alongside the transformation of religious rituals into secular social events. Rituals serving as displays of wealth/status; caste groups operating as political pressure groups. Shifts the basis of caste from ritual purity/pollution to secular political mobilization.
Sanskritisation

Coined by M.N. Srinivas, Sanskritisation is a complex process of cultural imitation and mobility within the Hindu social space.

Profile: M.N. Srinivas (1916–1999)

  • Theoretical Framework: Structural-functionalism, caste dynamics, and social change in modern India.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Intensive ethnographic fieldwork among the Coorgs of South India.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Sanskritisation, dominant caste, westernisation.
Key Dynamics and Regional Variations
  • Pre-requisites: Often presupposes an improvement in the economic or political position of the lower group, or contact with a source of the 'Great Tradition' (pilgrim centres, monasteries).
  • De-Sanskritisation: In regions where non-Sanskritic castes were dominant, their culture influenced the entire region (e.g., strong Persian influence in Punjab until the late 19th century).
  • Sanctions and Obstacles: Historically, dominant castes actively punished lower castes who attempted to adopt upper-caste rituals or lifestyles.

“Kumudtai’s journey into Sanskrit began with great interest and eagerness with Gokhale Guruji, her teacher at school… At the University, the Head of the Department was a well-known scholar and he took great pleasure in taunting Kumudtai… Despite the adverse comments she successfully completed her Masters in Sanskrit…”

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Kumud Pawade's autobiography highlights how the study of Sanskrit—historically a monopoly of upper-caste men—was used by a Dalit woman to break barriers of gender and caste. It illustrates the hostility, resistance, and eventual guarded acceptance encountered when lower-caste individuals attempt to access sacred/high-status cultural capital.

Major Criticisms of Sanskritisation
  1. No Structural Change: It leads only to positional change for some individuals/groups while leaving the unequal caste structure intact.
  2. Acceptance of Caste Superiority: It implicitly accepts the ways of the upper castes as superior and those of the lower castes as inferior.
  3. Justification of Inequality: It adopts a model resting on exclusion, pollution, and purity, thereby legitimising discriminatory practices.
  4. Gendered Disadvantage: Adoption of upper-caste norms often results in the seclusion of women, replacement of bride-price with dowry, and erosion of women's economic independence.
  5. Erosion of Dalit Culture: It devalues Dalit culture, labor, and indigenous knowledge systems (medicine, ecology, agriculture) as 'shameful' or 'useless' in comparison to Sanskritic rituals.
Westernisation

Westernisation refers to the multi-layered cultural and institutional changes initiated by British colonial rule. It operates at different levels, from elite sub-cultures to the general adoption of western material traits.

Levels and Paradoxes of Westernisation
  • Elite Sub-culture: A minority section of Indians who first came into contact with Western culture adopted its cognitive patterns, lifestyle, and democratic values (e.g., 19th-century social reformers).
  • General Material Spread: Broad adoption of western technology, consumer goods (TVs, fridges, sofas), and dress codes across middle-class homes without necessarily adopting western values of equality or democracy.
  • The Paradox of Values: Westernised appearances can co-exist with highly conservative social values (e.g., using modern medical technology for female foeticide).

“In 1870 Ravi Varma received his first paid commission to paint the portrait of Kizhakke Palat Krishna Menon’s family… This is a transitional work which blends elements of a flatter, two-dimensional style popular within earlier water-colours with the newer techniques of perspective and illusionism, made possible by the use of a medium like oil…”

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Raja Ravi Varma's art represents a cultural synthesis where western techniques (oil medium, perspective, bourgeois family portrait style) were applied to Indian subjects. Paradoxically, it depicted a matrilineal Kerala community (the Nayars) in the format of a western patrilocal nuclear family, reflecting the structural pressure of western family ideals on indigenous kinship systems.

“When I feel listening to a Meera-Bhajan, Kallol and Kingkini choose to play their favourite pop numbers by Whitney Houston. At times, when I feel like singing a few lines of Bargeet, Kinkin likes to play western tunes on her guitar.”

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Excerpted from Anima Dutta's “As Days Roll On”, this passage highlights the generational divide within the westernised middle class. It shows how cultural consumption (music, dress like pants vs. mekela-chadar) becomes a site of identity negotiation and alienation between parents and children.

Modernisation and Secularisation

Modernisation and secularisation are linked processes that reshape individual consciousness, institutional structures, and the role of religion in public life.

The Concept of Modernity

According to Rudolph and Rudolph (1967), modernity assumes:

  • Local and parochial ties give way to universal commitments and cosmopolitan attitudes.
  • Scientific calculation and utility take precedence over emotion and the sacred.
  • The individual, rather than the group, becomes the primary unit of society and politics.
  • Associations are based on choice rather than birth; identity is achieved rather than ascribed.
Secularisation of Caste

In traditional India, the caste system operated within a religious framework of purity and pollution. In modern India, caste has undergone secularisation, transforming into political pressure groups and electoral coalitions.

“Politicians mobilise caste groupings and identities in order to organise their power… And as they everywhere change the form of such organisations, they change the form of caste as well.”

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Rajni Kothari's analysis shows that caste does not simply disappear under the impact of modern politics. Instead, politics provides caste with a secular, competitive function. By organizing as political parties and interest groups to demand state resources, caste groups shed their ritual character and become modern democratic actors.
