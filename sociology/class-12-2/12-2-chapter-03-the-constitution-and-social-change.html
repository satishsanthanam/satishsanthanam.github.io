Sociology: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 3: The Constitution and Social Change

Constitutional Norms and Social Justice

The Indian Constitution serves as the foundational document of the nation, establishing basic norms of social justice and providing a framework for democratic governance and social transformation.

Law versus Justice
  • Essence of Law : Backed by the coercive power of the state; demands obedience through force.
  • Essence of Justice : Rooted in fairness, equity, and moral correctness.
  • Constitutional Role : The Constitution acts as the basic norm ( Grundnorm ) from which all other laws derive legitimacy.
Dimension Law Justice
Core Driver State force, coercion, and formal procedures. Fairness, equity, and ethical distribution of rights.
Authority Source Legislative bodies and constitutional mandates. Social movements, judicial interpretations, and moral consensus.
Judicial Interpretation and Social Justice (Box 3.1)

The Supreme Court of India acts as the ultimate interpreter of the Constitution, dynamically expanding the scope of Fundamental Rights to aid social justice.

Constitutional Provision Original Scope Expanded Judicial Interpretation Social Impact & Relief Provided
Article 21 (Right to Life and Liberty) Protection against arbitrary deprivation of life or personal liberty. Includes quality of life: livelihood, health, shelter, education, and human dignity. Rehabilitation of bonded labourers, relief to tortured prisoners, protection against environmental degradation, and access to primary healthcare/education.
Article 19(1)(a) (Freedom of Speech) Right to express views and opinions freely. Includes the Right to Information (RTI) as an incidental right (1993 ruling). Empowers citizens to demand transparency and accountability from public authorities.
Article 14 (Right to Equality) Equality before the law and equal protection of laws. Incorporates the Directive Principle of Equal Pay for Equal Work . Provides legal relief and fair wages to plantation, agricultural, and casual labourers.
Panchayati Raj and Rural Social Transformation

Panchayati Raj translates to "Governance by five individuals," aiming to establish vibrant, functioning grassroots democracy in rural India.

Historical Debates: Ambedkar vs. Gandhi
  • Mahatma Gandhi's Vision : Envisaged Gram-Swarajya (village self-rule), where each village is a self-sufficient, autonomous unit conducting its own affairs.
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's Critique : Argued that local self-government in rural India would lead to further exploitation of downtrodden masses. He asserted that local elites and upper castes were too deeply entrenched in rural society and would silence lower castes.
  • Constitutional Compromise : Initially placed under the non-justiciable Directive Principles of State Policy (Article 40) due to these conflicting views, before gaining constitutional status in 1992.
The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act (1992)

The 73rd Amendment institutionalized local self-government, introducing a mandatory three-tier structure across rural India.

Feature Institutional Mechanism & Specifications
Three-Tier Structure Mandatory for states with populations over 20 lakhs: Gram Panchayat (village), Panchayat Samiti (block), and Zilla Parishad (district).
Gram Sabha The foundational unit comprising all registered voters in a village; serves as an open forum for local development and accountability.
Mandatory Elections Elections must be conducted every five years. If dissolved early, elections must occur within six months.
Social Reservations Proportional reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs).
Gender Empowerment Mandatory reservation of 33% (one-third) of all seats for women, including 17% reserved for SC/ST women.
Planning Bodies Constitution of District Planning Committees to draft comprehensive district-level development plans.
Powers, Responsibilities, and Finances of Panchayats
  • Economic Development : Preparing plans and schemes for local economic growth.
  • Social Justice : Implementing schemes to enhance social justice and protect marginalized groups.
  • Financial Powers : Authorized to levy, collect, and appropriate local taxes, duties, tolls, and fees.
  • Social Welfare Duties : Maintenance of burial/burning grounds, recording births and deaths, establishing maternity/child welfare centers, and promoting family planning.
  • Infrastructure Development : Construction of roads, public buildings, wells, tanks, schools, and minor irrigation works.
  • Scheme Monitoring : Overseeing central schemes like the Integrated Rural Development Programme (IRDP) and Integrated Child Development Scheme (ICDS).
  • Financial Transparency : Mandatory display boards outside Panchayat offices detailing funds received and utilized, promoting grassroots Right to Information .
  • Nyaya Panchayats : Local judicial bodies in some states empowered to hear petty civil and criminal cases, impose fines, but cannot award prison sentences. Highly effective in addressing domestic violence and dowry harassment.
Panchayati Raj in Tribal Areas & Traditional Institutions
  • Exclusion from 73rd Amendment : Many tribal areas (e.g., in Meghalaya, Nagaland, Mizoram) were excluded from the 73rd Amendment to prevent interference with traditional political systems.
  • Traditional Systems : Ethnic groups like Khasis, Jaintias, and Garos in Meghalaya have long-standing traditional political institutions operating at village, clan, and state levels (e.g., the Khasi clan council known as Durbar Kur ).

Profile: Tiplut Nongbri

  • Theoretical Framework: Feminist sociology, tribal studies, and social stratification in Northeast India.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Critical analysis of the Bhuria Committee Report and traditional tribal political institutions in Meghalaya.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Tribal Stratification , gender exclusion in traditional councils, and the distortion of egalitarian tribal ethos through social change.

Nongbri argues that traditional tribal institutions are not necessarily democratic. Despite an egalitarian reputation, they often exhibit deep gender bias (excluding women from decision-making) and internal stratification, which have been further distorted by modern social changes.

Case Studies & Grassroots Realities

"Kalavati, a Dalit by caste, was apprehensive about standing for elections. She is now a Panchayat member and realizes her confidence and self-esteem have grown. Most importantly, 'she has a name' now, whereas previously she was only referred to as 'Ramu's mother' or 'Hiralal's wife'."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Demonstrates how political reservation under the 73rd Amendment acts as a catalyst for individual and collective empowerment, breaking patriarchal naming conventions and elevating the social status of Dalit women.

"In Uttarakhand, women have set up Van Panchayats (Forest Councils) to combat deforestation and secure firewood and fodder. Members develop nurseries, plant tree saplings, and police nearby forests to prevent illegal felling."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Highlights localized environmental resource management led by women, directly linking grassroots democratic structures to ecological preservation and the legacy of the Chipko Movement.

"Mahila Samakhya uses 'phad' (traditional scroll storytelling with folk music) to train illiterate women about Panchayati Raj. The story of two villages—Dhukipur (sad, corrupt village) and Sukhipur (happy, well-developed village)—teaches women the value of integrity, active participation, and informed voting."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Illustrates the use of indigenous cultural media to bypass literacy barriers, fostering political literacy, civic awareness, and democratic accountability among marginalized rural women.

Political Parties, Pressure Groups, and Interest Groups

Democratic politics extends beyond formal state institutions to include voluntary associations, interest groups, and political parties that mediate between citizens and the state.

Definitions and Distinctions
  • Political Party : An organization established with the aim of achieving legitimate control of government power through an electoral process to pursue a specific program.
  • Interest Groups : Organized bodies formed to pursue specific interests in the political arena, operating primarily by lobbying legislative bodies (e.g., industrialist associations like FICCI and ASSOCHAM ).
  • Pressure Groups : Organizations that attempt to influence government policies and decisions from the outside, often when their specific interests are ignored by mainstream political parties.
Feature Political Parties Interest / Pressure Groups
Primary Goal Capture state power through elections. Influence state policy and protect specific group interests.
Scope of Program Broad, multi-issue national or regional agenda. Narrow, focused on specific sector-based issues (e.g., labor, business, environment).
Examples National and regional political parties. FICCI, ASSOCHAM, INTUC, CITU, Shetkari Sangathan.
Max Weber on Parties (Box 3.7)

Profile: Max Weber (1864–1920)

  • Theoretical Framework: Classical sociological theory, social action, bureaucracy, and the sociology of power.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Comparative-historical analysis of world religions, capitalism, and political authority.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: House of Power , class, status, and party as distinct dimensions of social stratification.

Weber distinguished between classes (located in the economic order), status groups (located in the social order), and parties (located in the "house of power"). He noted that party actions are always directed towards a goal striven for in a planned manner, which can be a "cause" (ideal/material program) or "personal" (sinecures, power, and honor for leaders and followers).
