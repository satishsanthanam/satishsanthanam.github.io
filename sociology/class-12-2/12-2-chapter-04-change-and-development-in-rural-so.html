Sociology: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 4: Change and Development in Rural Society

Rural Society and Agrarian Structure

Indian society is fundamentally rural, with 69% of the population residing in rural areas according to the 2011 Census. Agriculture serves not merely as a source of livelihood but as a comprehensive way of life, deeply shaping cultural practices, regional identities, and social structures.

Cultural and Occupational Diversity
  • Agrarian Festivals : Major regional New Year and harvest festivals like Pongal (Tamil Nadu), Bihu (Assam), Baisakhi (Punjab), and Ugadi (Karnataka) mark the agricultural cycle.
  • Non-Agricultural Livelihoods : Rural life historically supported diverse specialists, including artisans ( potters , carpenters , weavers , ironsmiths , goldsmiths ) and service providers ( story-tellers , astrologers , priests , oil-pressers ).
  • Colonial Decline : Traditional rural crafts and artisan numbers have steadily declined since the colonial period due to the influx of cheap manufactured goods.
  • Modern Diversification : Increasing rural-urban linkages have expanded non-farm employment, including government services (postal, education), factory work, and military service.
Caste and Class Dynamics in Agrarian Structure
  • Agrarian Structure : Refers to the unequal distribution and ownership patterns of agricultural landholdings.
  • Land Inequality : Highly skewed distribution where a small minority owns large tracts, while 40% to 50% of rural families are completely landless, forcing dependency on agricultural wage labour.
  • Gender Exclusion : Women are systematically excluded from land ownership due to patrilineal kinship systems, possessing only limited, indirect access through male relatives despite legal rights to equal inheritance.
  • Agrarian Classes :
    • Medium and Large Landowners : Earn substantial incomes from cultivation, highly dependent on market prices and monsoons.
    • Agricultural Labourers : Suffer from low wages (often below statutory minimums), extreme job insecurity, and chronic underemployment .
    • Tenants : Cultivators who lease land from landowners, paying exorbitant rents ranging from 50% to 75% of the crop share.
  • Caste-Class Correspondence : Generally, higher castes correlate with higher landownership and income. However, the highest ritual caste ( Brahmins ) are often not major landowners, falling outside the core agrarian structure.

Profile: M.N. Srinivas (1916–1999)

  • Theoretical Framework: Structural-functionalism, caste dynamics, and social change in rural India.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Extensive ethnographic fieldwork in Rampura, Karnataka; documented the transition of rural power structures.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Dominant Caste — groups that are numerically large, hold significant landownership, and wield decisive economic and political power locally (e.g., Jats/Rajputs in UP, Vokkaligas/Lingayats in Karnataka, Kammas/Reddis in Andhra Pradesh, Jat Sikhs in Punjab).

The Impact of Land Reforms
Colonial Land Revenue Systems
  • Zamindari System : Implemented by the British, granting formal property rights to local landlords ( zamindars ). This system led to high tax extraction, agricultural stagnation, peasant flight, and frequent famines.
  • Raiyatwari System : Direct land settlement with actual cultivators (often landlords themselves) in southern and western India. It reduced tax burdens, incentivised agricultural investment, and led to relative prosperity compared to Zamindari areas.
Post-Independence Land Reform Legislation
  1. Abolition of the Zamindari System (1950s) : The most successful reform; removed intermediary landlords, weakened their economic/political power, and strengthened the rights of actual cultivators.
  2. Tenancy Abolition and Regulation Acts : Aimed to outlaw tenancy or regulate rents to secure tenant rights. Largely ineffective across India, except in West Bengal and Kerala , where radical restructuring successfully redistributed land rights to tenants.
  3. Land Ceiling Acts : Imposed upper limits on family landholdings based on land productivity. Proved highly ineffective ("toothless") in most states due to loopholes, benami transfers (transferring land to fictitious names or relatives), and strategic divorces to claim separate shares.

"In areas of assured irrigation, those with plentiful rainfall or artificial irrigation works... more labour was needed for intensive cultivation. Here the most unequal agrarian structures developed, characterised by a large proportion of landless labourers, who were often 'bonded' workers belonging to the lowest castes." (Box 4.1)

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Demonstrates the direct ecological-structural link where high agricultural productivity under traditional systems correlates with extreme social stratification, caste-based exploitation, and the institutionalisation of bonded labour.

The Green Revolution and its Social Consequences

Launched in the 1960s and 1970s, the Green Revolution was a state-sponsored agricultural modernisation programme aimed at achieving foodgrain self-sufficiency through high-tech inputs.

First Phase (1960s–1970s): Technological Package and Inequality
  • Core Inputs : High-Yielding Variety ( HYV ) seeds, chemical fertilisers, pesticides, and modern irrigation.
  • Geographical Target : Restricted to regions with assured irrigation (Punjab, western U.P., coastal Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu) and focused primarily on wheat and rice.
  • Class Differentiation : Benefited medium and large farmers who could afford expensive inputs. Small and marginal peasants practicing subsistence agriculture were left behind, widening rural inequalities.
  • Tenant Displacement : Landowners evicted tenant-cultivators to resume direct cultivation, as farming became highly profitable, worsening landlessness.
  • Caste Displacement : Introduction of machinery (tractors, harvesters) displaced traditional service castes, accelerating rural-to-urban migration.
Second Phase (1980s): Commercialisation and Ecological Crisis
  • Dryland Expansion : Extended to semi-arid regions, prompting a shift from dry to wet cultivation and cash crops (e.g., cotton).
  • Mono-cropping Risk : Farmers abandoned traditional multi-crop systems for high-risk mono-cropping, increasing vulnerability to market price fluctuations and crop failures.
  • Loss of Indigenous Knowledge : Traditional seed varieties and ecological farming practices were replaced by hybrid seeds, leading to genetic erosion and high chemical dependency.

"What…they used to grow some wheat, red sorghum…plant a few tubers, chilli plants…cotton. Now there’s only hibrad (hybrid)…where’s the javari (organic/local)? Hybrid seeds…hybrid crops…even the children are hybrid." (Box 4.2 - Bhargawa Hugar, Madbhavi village)

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Highlights the cultural alienation and loss of autonomy experienced by rural communities. The transition from "sampurna" (wholeness/organic) to "hibrad" (hybrid) symbolises the deep penetration of market forces into the ecological and social fabric of rural life.

Post-Independence Transformations and Labour Circulation
Transformations in Rural Social Relations
  • Intensive Labour Use : Increased demand for agricultural wage labour due to intensive cultivation.
  • Monetisation : A rapid shift from payment in kind (grain) to cash wages, which often worsened workers' real incomes due to inflation.
  • Capitalist Transition : Loosening of traditional, hereditary patron-client bonds (bonded labour) and the rise of a class of "free" wage labourers.
  • Patronage to Exploitation : Sociologist Jan Breman describes this shift as a transition from paternalistic protection to naked economic exploitation.
  • Rural Elites and Diversification : Wealthy dominant-caste farmers invested agricultural surpluses into non-farm businesses (trading, transport, education), creating a new regional, entrepreneurial elite.

Profile: Jan Breman (b. 1936)

  • Theoretical Framework: Agrarian sociology, labour relations, informal sector economics, and capitalist transitions.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Long-term ethnographic studies in South Gujarat; documented the lives of landless labourers and migrant workers.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Patronage to Exploitation — the breakdown of traditional mutual obligations between landlords and labourers; Footloose Labour — highly exploited, unprotected migrant workers circulating without job security or legal rights.
Circulation of Labour and Feminisation
  • Seasonal Migration : Breakdown of local patronage and high seasonal demand in Green Revolution areas led to thousands of workers migrating periodically (e.g., from Bihar/East UP to Punjab/Haryana).
  • Double Displacement Pattern : Local landless labourers migrate out of their home villages for work, while cheaper, more easily exploitable migrant workers are brought in from other regions.
  • Feminisation of Agricultural Labour : As male family members migrate to cities or prosperous farms, cultivation in source villages becomes primarily a female task, leading to the feminisation of the agricultural labour force .
  • Gendered Vulnerability : Women face extreme insecurity, earning lower wages than men, while remaining excluded from land ownership due to patrilineal inheritance systems.
Globalisation, Liberalisation, and Agrarian Distress
Globalisation of Agriculture
  • Liberalisation Policies : Initiated in the late 1980s, involving participation in the World Trade Organization ( WTO ), reduction of import tariffs, and exposure of Indian farmers to global competition.
  • Contract Farming : Farmers enter agreements with multinational corporations (e.g., PepsiCo) to grow specialized cash crops (tomatoes, potatoes, flowers). MNCs dictate inputs and guarantee purchase prices, but create high dependency and ecological strain.
  • Corporate Input Entry : Multinationals replaced state-run agricultural extension services, aggressively promoting expensive hybrid seeds, chemical fertilisers, and pesticides.
Agrarian Distress and Farmers' Suicides
  • Agrarian Distress : Caused by rising production costs, falling state subsidies, unstable market prices, and heavy reliance on high-cost corporate inputs.
  • Debt Trap : Marginal and small farmers borrow heavily from institutional and informal sources to finance expensive inputs, leading to systemic debt cycles when crops fail.
  • State Interventions : Government schemes designed to mitigate distress include Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana , Kisan Credit Card , and the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture .

"The spate of farmers’ suicides that has been occurring in different parts of the country since 1997–98 can be linked to the ‘agrarian distress’ caused by structural changes in agriculture... According to official statistics, there have been 8,900 suicides by farmers between 2001 and 2006 in Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala and Maharashtra." (Box 4.3)

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Farmers' suicides are not merely individual psychological events but structural manifestations of "risk societies" where the withdrawal of state support, commercialisation, and global market integration expose vulnerable marginal cultivators to catastrophic financial risks.
