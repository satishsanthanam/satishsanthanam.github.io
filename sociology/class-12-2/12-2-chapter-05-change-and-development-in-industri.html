Sociology: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 5: Change and Development in Industrial Society

Images of Industrial Society

The transition from agrarian to industrial society has been a central theme in classical sociology, marking a shift from face-to-face community relations to anonymous, structured, and highly specialized systems of production.

Profile: Classical Sociologists (Marx, Weber, Durkheim)

  • Theoretical Framework: Analyzed the social consequences of industrialisation, including urbanisation, the loss of close-knit rural relationships, and the rise of bureaucratic systems.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Alienation (Marx), Division of Labour (Durkheim), Rationalisation (Weber).
Core Sociological Concept Baseline Definition / Phenomenon Social Manifestation / Practice Impact on Social Stratification & Power
Alienation Marxist concept where workers lose control over their work, the product, and themselves. Repetitive, exhausting factory tasks where workers produce only a small part of a product. Reduces workers to mere extensions of machines, stripping them of agency and power.
Division of Labour Specialisation of cooperative labor in specific, circumscribed tasks and roles. Detailed breakdown of tasks in modern factories and offices. Replaces face-to-face rural relationships with anonymous professional relationships.
Organised Sector Units employing 10 or more people, registered with the government to ensure benefits. Government jobs, large public/private enterprises with secure salaries and pensions. Creates a small, privileged class of secure workers (less than 10% of Indian workforce).
Unorganised Sector Informal employment lacking registration, regular wages, and social security. Agriculture, casual construction labor, home-based bidi rolling. Perpetuates high vulnerability, low wages, and lack of collective bargaining power for over 90% of workers.
Social and Economic Inequalities
  • Industrialisation reduces certain social distinctions (e.g., caste barriers on trains, buses, or in cyber cafes).
  • Older forms of discrimination persist or adapt to new factory and workplace settings.
  • Social inequality and income inequality often overlap, as seen in the domination of upper-caste men in well-paying professions like medicine, law, or journalism.
  • Gender discrimination remains prevalent, with women often paid less than men for similar work.
Industrialisation in India

The experience of industrialisation in India differs significantly from the Western model, characterized by a unique sectoral distribution of labor and a massive informal economy.

Sectoral Distribution of Employment

Unlike developed nations where the majority of the workforce is in the services sector, India retains a massive share of employment in agriculture despite its declining contribution to GDP.

Sector Employment Share (2018–19) Economic Contribution (GDP) Key Characteristics
Primary (Agriculture & Mining) 42.5% (Agriculture) + 0.4% (Mining) Declining sharply (less than 20%) Employs maximum people but generates low income per capita.
Secondary (Manufacturing, Construction, Utilities) 12.1% (Manufacturing) + Construction/Utilities Moderate growth Relatively small share of employment compared to developed nations.
Tertiary (Services, Trade, Transport, IT) 32% (Total Services) More than 50% Rapidly growing, highly unequal (high-paying IT vs. low-paying retail).
Employment Status and the Informal Economy

The Indian workforce is heavily dominated by self-employment and casual labor, with only a small fraction enjoying regular salaried positions.

Employment Status Share of Workers (2018–19) Social Implications
Self-employed Over 52% High level of informal entrepreneurship, lack of steady income.
Regular salaried employees About 24% Highly coveted, but increasingly insecure due to contract labor.
Casual wage labour Approximately 24% Extreme vulnerability, daily wage dependence, no social security.
Globalisation, Liberalisation, and Changes in Indian Industry
  • Liberalisation (since the 1990s) opened sectors like telecom, civil aviation, and power to private and foreign investment.
  • Many Indian companies were acquired by multinationals (e.g., Parle drinks bought by Coca-Cola).
  • Disinvestment : The government selling its share in public sector companies, leading to job insecurity and forced early retirements (e.g., 60% of workers forced to retire in the first five years at Modern Foods).
  • Outsourcing : Large companies reduce permanent staff, outsourcing work to smaller, non-unionised firms or home-based workers to keep wages low and avoid labor regulations.
  • Land Acquisition : Government policies acquiring agricultural land for industrial use, displacing farmers and adivasis (who constitute 40% of those displaced) and forcing them into casual labor.
How People Find Jobs

Job recruitment in India relies heavily on personal networks, contractors, and informal systems rather than formal advertisements or employment exchanges.

  • Personal Contacts : Crucial for self-employed workers (plumbers, electricians, carpenters) and professionals (teachers, architects, photographers). Mobile phones have expanded these networks.
  • Contractors and Jobbers : Historically, workers in textile mills were recruited through jobbers known as mistris in Kanpur, who exercised community-related pressure and authority.
  • Badli Workers : Substitute workers who replace permanent employees on leave. Many work for years without receiving permanent status or security.
  • Contractor System in Casual Labour : Contractors recruit casual labor from villages for construction sites or brickyards, often using advance loans as a mechanism to bind workers until the debt is repaid.
  • Government Schemes : Initiatives like MUDRA , Aatmanirbhar Bharat , and Make in India aim to generate employment and self-employment, particularly supporting marginalized sections (SC, ST, and OBCs).
How is Work Carried Out?

Managerial control over the labor process focuses on maximizing output through extended hours, increased work intensity, and technological control.

In Maruti Udyog Ltd., two cars roll off the assembly line every minute. Workers get only 45 minutes rest in the entire day - two tea breaks of 7.5 minutes each and one lunch break of half an hour. Most of them are exhausted by the age of 40 and take voluntary retirement.

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Demonstrates extreme speed-up of work under modern assembly-line systems. Outsourcing of services (cleaning, security, parts) reduces permanent jobs, while just-in-time systems keep costs low but increase worker tension and physical exhaustion.

'Time Slavery' and Taylorist Control in the IT Sector

Even highly educated software professionals face intense managerial control and long working hours, often referred to as Taylorist labor processes.

An average work day has 10–12 hours and it is not uncommon for employees to stay overnight in the office (known as a 'night out'), when faced with a project deadline. Long working hours are central to the industry's 'work culture'... legitimised by the common management practice of 'flexi-time'.

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Highlights how high-tech service work is subjected to Taylorist control. 'Flexi-time' acts as a mechanism of self-exploitation where workers extend their hours to meet underestimated project timelines, driven by peer pressure and global time-zone differences.

Profile: Harry Braverman (1920–1976)

  • Theoretical Framework: Marxist analysis of the labor process under monopoly capitalism.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Deskilling (the process by which technology and managerial control strip workers of their specialized skills, making them easily replaceable, such as computers replacing manual drafting for architects).
Working Conditions

Many workers in India operate under hazardous, unregulated, and highly exploitative conditions, particularly in mining and processing industries.

  • Mining Hazards : Coal mines employ 5.5 lakh workers. Despite the Mines Act 1952 (now part of the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Condition Code, 2020 ), safety rules are widely flouted in small mines and quarries through sub-contracting.
  • Underground Mining Risks : Workers face flooding, fire, roof collapses, toxic gas emissions, and high rates of tuberculosis and silicosis.
  • Overground Mining Risks : Exposure to extreme weather, injuries from blasting, and falling objects.
  • Migrant Labour and Gender : Fish processing plants along the coastline employ single young women from Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and Kerala. They are housed in cramped rooms (10-12 per room) and work in shifts, valued by employers who view them as submissive.
  • Social Isolation : Single male and female migrants face loneliness and vulnerability, cut off from traditional joint family networks, though for some young women, it also represents a degree of economic autonomy.
Home-based Work

Home-based production is a massive, highly gendered segment of the informal economy where workers are paid on a piece-rate basis.

  • Key Industries : Manufacture of lace, zari/brocade, carpets, bidis, and agarbattis, primarily carried out by women and children.
  • Piece-rate System : Workers are paid strictly by the number of pieces they produce, shifting the overhead costs of space, electricity, and equipment from the employer to the worker.
  • The Bidi Industry Process :
    1. Villagers pluck tendu leaves in forested areas and sell them to contractors or the forest department.
    2. The government auctions leaves to bidi factory owners, who distribute them to contractors.
    3. Contractors supply tobacco and leaves to home-based workers (mostly women) who damp, cut, fill, roll, and tie the bidis.
    4. Contractors collect the rolled bidis and sell them to manufacturers, who roast, label, and distribute them to wholesalers and retail pan shops.
Strikes and Unions

Workers use strikes as a tool of collective bargaining to protest harsh working conditions, while managements counter with lockouts.

  • Strike : A collective decision by workers to stop working to demand better wages or conditions.
  • Lockout : Management shuts the workplace gates to prevent workers from entering and working.

The Bombay Textile strike of 1982, led by Dr. Datta Samant, affected nearly a quarter of a million workers and lasted nearly two years. Workers wanted better wages and the right to form their own union. Desperation forced many back, while nearly one lakh workers lost their jobs permanently.

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Illustrates the devastating impact of failed collective action in the face of rigid management and state apathy. The strike led to de-industrialisation of Mumbai, with mill owners selling lucrative land to real estate developers, shifting the city's class dynamics from industrial workers to service-class luxury consumers.
