Sociology: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 7: Mass Media and Communications

Introduction to Mass Media and Communications

Mass media comprises a wide variety of forms—including television, newspapers, films, magazines, radio, advertisements, video games, and CDs—that reach massive, diverse audiences. The relationship between mass media and society is fundamentally dialectical , meaning they mutually influence and shape one another.

Core Characteristics of Mass Communication
  • Mass Audience Reach: Unlike interpersonal communication, mass media targets very large, anonymous, and heterogeneous populations.
  • Dialectical Relationship: The structure and content of mass media are shaped by economic, political, and socio-cultural contexts, while simultaneously exerting a far-reaching influence back on society.
  • Formal Structural Organisation: Mass communication requires large-scale capital, formal production structures, and major investments, making the state and the market key players in its functioning.
  • Digital Divide: Sharp disparities exist in how easily different sections of society can access and use modern communication technologies.

Transnational networks like STAR Plus shifting from English to Hindi programming and dubbing US shows. Expands market penetration for global conglomerates while validating local languages and cultural idioms.
7.1 The Beginnings of Modern Mass Media

The inception of modern mass media is historically tied to the development of the printing press and the subsequent rise of industrial societies.

Historical Evolution of Print Media
  1. Gutenberg's Press (1440): Developed by Johann Gutenberg in Europe, initial printing was restricted to religious texts (e.g., the Bible).
  2. Industrial Revolution: Enabled mass production of printed materials. Early products were restricted to literate elites.
  3. Mid-19th Century Expansion: Technological advancements, improved transportation, and rising literacy rates allowed newspapers to reach a mass audience.

Profile: Benedict Anderson (1936–2015)

  • Theoretical Framework: Cultural roots of nationalism and the rise of the nation-state.
  • Field Studies & Methodologies: Comparative historical analysis of print capitalism and linguistic standardisation.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Imagined Community —arguing that print capitalism allowed people who would never meet to read the same news, fostering a sense of national belonging and togetherness.
The Nationalist Press in Colonial India
  • Anti-Colonial Mobilisation: The growth of Indian nationalism was closely linked to the struggle against British colonialism. Social reformers and nationalists used newspapers to debate social issues and nurture anti-colonial public opinion.
  • Colonial Censorship: The vocal opposition of the nationalist press led the colonial government to impose strict censorship, notably during the Ilbert Bill agitation in 1883.
  • Underground Influence: Under British rule, newspapers and magazines were not widely circulated due to low literacy, but their influence was multiplied as news was read aloud and spread by word of mouth in commercial hubs, markets, and courts.

7.2 Mass Media in Independent India

Following independence in 1947, the Indian state adopted a developmental approach, utilizing mass media as a tool for nation-building and social reform.

The Nehruvian Developmental Approach
  • Jawaharlal Nehru called upon the media to function as the watchdog of democracy .
  • The media was expected to promote self-reliance, national integration, and a rational, scientific ethos.
  • It was actively encouraged to fight social evils such as untouchability, child marriage, and superstition.
  • The government's Films Division produced newsreels and documentaries shown in every movie theatre before feature films to document state-led development.
The Evolution of Radio (All India Radio - AIR)
  • Origins: Commenced in the 1920s via amateur 'ham' broadcasting clubs in Kolkata and Chennai; transformed into a state-controlled public broadcasting system in the 1940s during WWII for Allied propaganda.
  • Post-Independence Expansion: In 1947, India had only 6 radio stations for 350 million people. The government prioritised expanding infrastructure to state capitals and border areas.
  • Vividh Bharati: Launched in 1957, this popular entertainment channel broadcast Hindi film songs and eventually carried sponsored programmes and advertisements, becoming a major revenue generator for AIR.
  • The Transistor Revolution: In the 1960s, the introduction of cheap, battery-operated transistor sets made radio highly mobile and accessible to rural and lower-income households.
  • Three-Tiered Service: AIR developed a formidable infrastructure operating on national, regional, and local levels to cater to India's geographic and linguistic diversity.

"In the 1960s, when the high yielding varieties of food crops, as a part of the Green Revolution, were introduced... It was All India Radio which undertook a major countryside campaign on these crops on a sustained day-to-day basis for over 10 years from 1967."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This case highlights the state's use of public broadcasting as an instrument of social engineering and agricultural modernization. By deploying subject specialists to conduct field visits and broadcast first-hand farmer accounts, the state bypassed literacy barriers, directly linking mass media to rural economic transformation.

The Evolution of Television (Doordarshan)
  • Experimental Phase: Introduced in 1959 to promote rural development.
  • SITE (Satellite Instructional Television Experiment): Conducted between August 1975 and July 1976, broadcasting directly to 2,400 community TV sets across rural areas in six states for four hours daily.
  • Expansion & Colour: By 1975, stations were set up in Delhi, Mumbai, Srinagar, and Amritsar. The introduction of colour broadcasting during the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi marked a rapid expansion of the national network.
  • Commercialisation: A shift in target audience occurred as Doordarshan began carrying advertisements, directing entertainment programmes toward the growing urban consuming class.

"Hum Log was India’s first long-running soap opera (1984–85)... It promoted social themes, such as gender equality, small family size and national integration... A study of Hum Log’s audience showed that a high degree of parasocial interaction occurred... many viewers routinely adjusted their daily schedules to 'meet' their favourite characters."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Hum Log demonstrated the power of the "entertainment-education" strategy. It achieved massive audience ratings (65-90% in North India) and generated over 400,000 viewer letters. It also marked the beginning of rapid commercialisation, famously promoting "Maggi 2-Minute noodles" and proving the immense power of television commercials to shape consumer habits.

7.3 Globalisation and the Media

The post-1990 period of globalisation and economic liberalisation brought profound structural transformations, shifting the media from state-dominated or highly regulated domestic sectors to a fluid, market-driven global landscape.

The Indian Language Newspaper Revolution
  • Contrary to Western trends where print media declined with the rise of the internet, India experienced a massive boom in vernacular newspaper circulation.
  • Demographic Drivers: Rise in literate populations, migration of rural working-class populations to cities, and the specific local needs of readers in small towns and villages.
  • Localisation Strategies: Dominant regional papers like Malayala Manorama and Eenadu pioneered "district dailies" and block editions, introducing highly localised news, local advertisements, and simplified colloquial language (e.g., Dina Thanthi ).
  • Technological Integration: Transition to fully automated production from reporter's desk to final page proof using LANs, newsmaking software, digital cameras, and modems (e.g., Amar Ujala ).

The Television Revolution (Post-1991)
  • Multiplication of Channels: In 1991, India had only one state-controlled channel (Doordarshan). By 1998, there were nearly 70 channels, and by 2020, there were about 900 private television networks.
  • Catalysts of Change: The Gulf War of 1991 popularised CNN, and the launch of Star-TV by the Whampoa Hutchinson Group of Hong Kong signalled the arrival of private satellite channels.
  • Regional Language Broadcasting: Private cable and satellite channels emerged catering exclusively to regional audiences, such as Sun-TV, Eenadu-TV, Udaya-TV, Raj-TV, and Asianet.
  • Localisation of Transnational Media: International networks realised that cultural familiarity was key to market penetration. STAR Plus shifted from English to 100% Hindi programming by 1999, popularising "Hinglish" slogans like 'Aapki Boli. Aapka Plus Point' .
  • 24x7 News & Reality TV: News became informal, immediate, and interactive. Reality shows like Kaun Banega Crorepati , Indian Idol , and Bigg Boss (modelled on Western formats) became highly popular.

"Prince, a 5-year old boy had fallen into a 55-ft borewell shaft in Aldeharhi village in Kurukshetra, Haryana... Two news channels... suspended all other programmes and reporting... for two days continuous footage of the child bravely fighting off insects, sleeping or crying out to his mother was splashed on the TV screens."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This event illustrates the rise of sensationalism and commercial competition among 24x7 news channels. To secure higher viewership ratings, channels resorted to live, continuous, and highly emotional coverage, turning a localized rescue operation into a national hysteria, highlighting the media's tendency to intrude into personal lives and prioritize spectacle over structural issues.

The Radio Revolution: FM and Community Radio
  • Privatisation of FM (2002): The introduction of privately owned FM radio stations provided a massive boost to entertainment. Because private FM channels are legally barred from broadcasting political news, they specialise in niche music programming to retain audiences.
  • Corporate Conglomerates: Most popular FM channels are owned by major media houses (e.g., Radio Mirchi is owned by the Times Group, Red FM by Living Media, and Radio City by the Star Network).
  • Community Radio: Emerged as a vital alternative to commercial radio, allowing local communities to run their own stations to address local issues, languages, and cultural needs.

"On a balmy morning in India’s northern state of Bihar, young Raghav Mahato gets ready to fire up his home-grown FM radio station... Raghav Mahato’s outback FM radio station plays films songs and broadcasts public interest messages on HIV and polio, and even snappy local news... out of Raghav’s thatched-roof Priya Electronics Shop."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: Raghav FM Mansoorpur 1 (built for just Rs 50 using local, low-cost equipment) represents the democratic potential of community-led media. It bypassed state licensing barriers to serve the immediate needs of an impoverished rural population, proving that local communities crave media that reflects their own language, culture, and immediate developmental concerns.
