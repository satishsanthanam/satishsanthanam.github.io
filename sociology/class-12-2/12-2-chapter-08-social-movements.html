Sociology: Class 12 (Book 2) — Chapter 8: Social Movements

Features of a Social Movement

A social movement is a sustained, organized collective effort aimed at bringing about or resisting social change. It is distinct from spontaneous, disorganized protests or riots due to its structural features and long-term orientation.

Core Defining Features
  • Sustained Collective Action: Requires continuous mobilization and protest over a prolonged period rather than a single, isolated event.
  • Degree of Organisation: Marked by a clear organizational structure, including leadership, defined roles, and decision-making mechanisms.
  • Shared Objectives and Ideologies: Participants are united by common goals, values, and a shared understanding of the social issues they seek to address.
  • Orientation Towards Change: Directed at either bringing about systemic changes (e.g., demanding policy reforms from the state) or preventing changes (counter movements).
  • Modes of Protest: Utilizes distinct repertoires of action, such as candle-light marches, street theater, boycotts, picketing, and civil disobedience.
Social Change vs. Social Movements

It is vital to distinguish between broad historical processes of social change and deliberate social movements.

Dimension Social Change Social Movements
Nature Continuous, ongoing, and often unconscious historical process. Directed, conscious, and goal-oriented collective action.
Agency Sum total of countless individual and structural actions across time. Driven by organized groups, activists, and specific leadership.
Examples Sanskritisation , Westernisation , industrialization. The Right to Information (RTI) campaign, anti-sati reforms.
Counter Movements

Social movements often trigger defensive reactions from groups seeking to maintain the status quo. These are known as counter movements .

  • When Raja Rammohun Roy campaigned against sati and formed the Brahmo Samaj, orthodox defenders of the practice formed the Dharma Sabha to petition the British against reform.
  • Early campaigns for girls' education and widow remarriage faced severe social boycotts and public protests.
  • Mobilization by lower-caste groups often provokes retaliatory actions and counter-mobilization by dominant, upper-caste groups.
Sociology and Social Movements

Sociology as a discipline emerged during a period of massive social upheaval in Europe, making the study of collective action and social movements central to its development.

Profile: Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)

  • Theoretical Framework: Structural functionalism, social integration, and organic solidarity.
  • View on Social Movements: Perceived collective protests and rapid social movements as forces of disorder and social disintegration.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Anomie , division of labour, social solidarity. Durkheim's work reflected elite anxieties about maintaining social order amidst industrialization.

Profile: Karl Marx (1818–1883) & E.P. Thompson (1924–1993)

  • Theoretical Framework: Historical materialism, class struggle, and conflict theory.
  • View on Social Movements: Collective action is a rational, necessary response to systemic exploitation and deprivation.
  • Key Terms / Concepts: Moral economy of the crowd. Thompson argued that peasant and working-class protests were not anarchic riots but were driven by a shared moral consensus of rights and justice.
Types of Social Movements

Social movements can be classified based on their scope, methods, and the nature of the changes they seek to achieve.

Classification Matrix
Type of Movement Core Objective Key Examples
Redemptive / Transformatory Aims to change the personal consciousness and individual practices of its members. The Ezhava community movement in Kerala led by Narayana Guru to reform social and religious practices.
Reformist Strives to change existing social and political arrangements through gradual, incremental steps. The 1960s movement for the linguistic reorganization of Indian states; the Right to Information (RTI) campaign.
Revolutionary Attempts to radically transform social relations, often by capturing state power. The Bolshevik Revolution in Russia (1917); the Naxalite movement in India.
Old vs. New Social Movements

Sociologists distinguish between "old" class-based movements of the industrial era and "new" social movements that emerged in the late 20th century.

  • Old Social Movements: Focused primarily on economic redistribution, class-based exploitation, and the reorganization of power relations. They operated largely within the framework of political parties and trade unions (e.g., working-class movements, anti-colonial struggles).
  • New Social Movements: Focus on quality-of-life issues, identity politics, cultural anxieties, and environmental sustainability. They often transcend class boundaries and operate through non-party political formations and international networks (e.g., feminist movements, ecological movements, peace movements).
Ecological Movements

Ecological movements arise in response to developmental models that prioritize unchecked natural resource exploitation over environmental sustainability and local livelihoods.

The Chipko Movement

The Chipko Movement in the Himalayan foothills is a classic example of an ecological movement that interlinked economic subsistence with environmental conservation.

  • Conflict: Local villagers' subsistence needs (firewood, fodder, timber) were pitted against the state government's commercial timber logging contracts.
  • Action: Villagers, particularly women, stepped forward to hug trees to prevent them from being felled by government contractors.
  • Interlinked Issues: Combined "red" (social justice, livelihood, local representation) and "green" (ecological sustainability, prevention of floods and landslides) concerns.

"The unusually heavy monsoon of 1970 precipitated the most devastating flood in living memory in the Alaknanda valley... Villagers, who bore the brunt of the damage, were beginning to perceive the hitherto tenuous links between deforestation, landslides and floods... Gaura Devi, the head of the village Mahila Mandal, quickly mobilised the other housewives and went to the forest. Pleading with the labourers not to start felling operations... the women refused to budge, and the men were eventually forced to retire."

Sociological Analysis & Structural Significance: This case study highlights how ecological degradation directly impacts the survival of marginalized rural communities. It demonstrates the agency of rural women in environmental governance and illustrates how local cooperative organizations like the Dashauli Gram Swaraja Sangh (DGSS) can successfully challenge state-backed capitalist interests.

Class-Based Movements
Peasant Movements

Agrarian struggles in India have a long history, spanning pre-colonial, colonial, and post-colonial eras.

  1. Colonial Era (1858–1914): Characterized by localized, disjointed protests against specific grievances, such as the Bengal Indigo Revolt (1859–1862) and the Deccan Riots of 1857 against moneylenders.
  2. Nationalist Phase: Under Mahatma Gandhi's leadership, peasant struggles became linked to the freedom movement. Key campaigns included the Champaran Satyagraha (1917–1918) against indigo plantations and the Bardoli Satyagraha (1928) against land revenue hikes.
  3. Rise of Kisan Sabhas (1920–1940): The Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha (1929) and the All India Kisan Sabha (1936) were established to demand freedom from economic exploitation.
  4. Post-Independence Radical Movements:
    • Tebhaga Movement (1946–1947): A struggle by sharecroppers in Bengal demanding a two-thirds share of their harvest instead of the customary half.
    • Telangana Movement (1946–1951): A peasant uprising against feudal landlords in the princely state of Hyderabad, led by the Communist Party of India (CPI).
  5. New Farmers' Movements (1970s onwards): Emerged in green revolution areas like Punjab and Tamil Nadu. Led by market-oriented farmers rather than subsistence peasants, focusing on price procurement, agricultural input subsidies, and tax relief.
Workers' Movements

The growth of factory production in port cities like Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras during the late 19th century led to the emergence of an organized working class.

  • Early Phase: Characterized by cheap labor, unregulated working hours, and poor conditions. Initial protests were spontaneous and unorganized.
  • Trade Union Formation:
    • The first formal trade union, the Madras Labour Union, was founded in April 1918 by B.P. Wadia.
    • Mahatma Gandhi founded the Textile Labour Association (TLA) in 1918.
    • The All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) was established in Bombay in 1920, bringing together diverse political ideologies (communists, moderates, and nationalists).
  • Post-Independence Splits: Political divisions led to the creation of party-affiliated unions, such as the Indian National Trade Union Congress (INTUC) by the Indian National Congress in May 1947.
  • 1974 Railway Strike: A landmark national strike that highlighted the acute confrontation between the state and trade unions over wages and civil liberties.
Caste-Based Movements
The Dalit Movement

The Dalit movement is a struggle for recognition as equal human beings, self-determination, and the eradication of untouchability and caste-based stigmatization.

  • Term Origin: The word "Dalit" (meaning poor, broken, or oppressed) was popularized in Marathi by neo-Buddhist activists and followers of Babasaheb Ambedkar in the early 1970s.
  • Regional Manifestations: Key historical movements include the Satnami Movement of Chamars in Chhattisgarh, the Adi Dharma Movement in Punjab, the Mahar Movement in Maharashtra, and the Anti-Brahman Movement in South India.
  • Dalit Literature: A powerful cultural movement using distinct imagery and expressions to challenge mainstream social narratives and demand structural change.
Backward Class Castes Movements

The political mobilization of non-Brahmin, intermediate castes emerged in both colonial and post-colonial contexts.

  • Colonial Patronage: The colonial state's distribution of resources and jobs based on caste encouraged groups to organize politically.
  • Horizontal Stretch: Similarly placed caste groups united across regions to form larger political coalitions, secularizing caste identity for electoral mobilization.
  • Organizations: Early groups included the United Provinces Hindu Backward Classes League and the All-India Backward Classes Federation.
The Tribal Movements
Jharkhand Movement

The creation of the state of Jharkhand in 2000 was the culmination of over a century of tribal resistance against exploitation by outsiders ( dikus ).

  • Historical Roots: Led by charismatic figures like Birsa Munda , who spearheaded a major rebellion against British colonial rule and land alienation.
  • Role of Literacy: Christian missionaries spread literacy, enabling educated adivasis to document their history, myths, and cultural practices, fostering a unified Jharkhandi identity.
  • Key Grievances: Land acquisition for large irrigation projects, mining, and industrialization; alienation from forest lands; and exploitation by migrant traders and moneylenders.
The North-East Movements

Tribal movements in India's North-East exhibit distinct characteristics due to the region's unique history and geography.

  • Autonomy vs. Secessionism: Early phases of mobilization often showed secessionist tendencies, which have largely transitioned into demands for regional autonomy within the framework of the Indian Constitution.
  • Forest Alienation: A central issue binding these movements is the alienation of tribal communities from their traditional forest lands and resources.
The Women's Movement
Early Phase and Social Reform

The women's movement in India began with 19th-century social reform initiatives and grew through early 20th-century national organizations.

  • Key Early Organizations: The Women's India Association (WIA) in 1917, the All India Women's Conference (AIWC) in 1926, and the National Council for Women in India (NCWI) in 1925.
  • Evolution of Scope: Initially focused on "women's welfare" and education, these organizations quickly linked their struggles to the broader national movement for freedom from colonial rule.
Agrarian and Tribal Participation

Contrary to the assumption that only middle-class urban women participated in social movements, peasant and tribal women played vital roles in radical struggles.

  • Active participation in the Tebhaga movement in Bengal, the Telangana arms struggle in Hyderabad, and the Warli tribal revolt against bonded labor in Maharashtra.
Post-1947 and the Second Phase

The mid-1970s witnessed a powerful renewal of the women's movement in India, often referred to as its second phase.

  • New Focus Areas: Addressed systemic violence against women, dowry deaths, sexual harassment, land rights, and employment discrimination.
  • Institutional Impact: Led to significant legal reforms, changes in school admission forms (including both parents' names), and state-led initiatives like the Beti Bachao, Beti Padhao Yojana to improve the sex ratio and promote girls' education.
